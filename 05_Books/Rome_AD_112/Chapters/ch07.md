# 7 | The Informer

The man's name was Hilarus, and he did not look like anything.

He was perhaps forty-five, thin, with the stooped shoulders of a clerk and the hands of one, and he dressed in the decent brown wool of a freedman who worked for a family of moderate means. He had a wife and two daughters, a house in the sixth region, and a small property at Tibur that he visited four times a year. He was, by profession, a *delator*: a man who made his living by bringing accusations, and who had brought eleven in fourteen years, of which nine had succeeded.

Antonia had used him once before, in the matter of a will, and had paid him eleven thousand sesterces and had disliked him thoroughly, and had recommended him to two people since.

They met in the back room of a shoemaker's shop on the Via Sacra, which was not a place where anyone of consequence went, and which Hilarus used because the shoemaker owed him money and because the smell of the glue was, he said, conducive to clear thinking.

'You want a child found,' he said.

'I want a woman found. The child was twenty-six years ago. The woman is the way to her.'

'Twenty-six years.' Hilarus turned his cup around on the table. 'That is a long time, domina. Records rot. Records are burned, and clerks die, and the ones who don't die develop a convenient forgetfulness. And the tax records for the sale of slaves are kept for eleven years and then sold for waste paper, which means that the paper in which your child was entered has been, at some point, sold to a man who used it to wrap fish.'

'I know what a record is,' said Antonia. 'I have been in this city eleven years.'

'Then you know that what I am selling you is not a certainty. It is a probability, priced accordingly.' He set the cup down. 'Tell me everything. Not the parts that make you look well. Everything.'

She told him. She had decided before she came that she would, and she told it flatly, in the voice she used for the accounts, and she did not stop when her voice did something strange in the middle, and Hilarus did not look away or look sympathetic or, at any point, write anything down.

When she had finished he was silent for a while.

'The household at Arretium,' he said. 'The old consul died in February of that year. The heirs were the son and the daughter. The daughter was married to a man named —?'

'Servius Aelius Gracilis. Not the young one; his father. He died eight years ago. The widow is still alive and lives at Tibur and is seventy-nine and has nothing to do with anything.'

Hilarus nodded slowly. 'And the son — the present Tarquitius — was in Rome at the time of the birth.'

'He was in Rome from March until the autumn. I never had a letter after June.'

'Why not?'

'I have thought about that for twenty-six years,' said Antonia, 'and I have three answers, and two of them are unbearable, and the third is that his letters were intercepted, and I have no evidence for any of them, and the lack of evidence is the whole of my life.'

Hilarus looked at her.

'You want me to find a girl,' he said, 'who may be dead, or may be a slave in a house in Naples, or may be the wife of a farmer in Campania with four children and no idea that anything ever happened to her. And if I find her, you may not want what I find.'

'I am aware.'

'You are not aware. Nobody is aware until it is in front of them.' He turned his cup again. 'Fifty thousand sesterces. Twenty now, thirty on the day I put a name in your hand. If I find nothing in a year, you have lost twenty and I have lost a year, which for a man in my trade is worse.'

'Agreed.'

'And one other thing.' He looked up. 'You will appear as a witness in a case I am bringing in the autumn. A false will, in the matter of the estate of a man named Vipsanius, who died in the fifth region and whose brother has manufactured a codicil. You know the family. You will swear to a conversation you had with the dead man, in your own house, in front of five witnesses, in which he said that he intended to leave his property to his daughter. You will not be asked whether you liked him. You will be asked what he said.'

'And what did he say?'

'What I have just told you,' said Hilarus, 'which happens to be true, and which is why I am asking you and not one of the eleven people who would say it for money.'

Antonia looked at the shoemaker's back room: the glue pots, the lasts, the smell, and the narrow window with the light coming through it onto the table between them.

'Why do you do this?' she said. 'The accusations. You are not poor. You do not need it.'

'No,' said Hilarus. 'I do not need it.'

'Then why?'

He considered. It was the first time in the conversation that he had looked like a man rather than a profession.

'Because when I was nineteen my father was sold,' he said. 'Not by a bad master. By a good one, who needed the money for a dowry, and who wept when he did it, and who wrote him a letter of recommendation, which my father kept until he died. My father was sold to a man in Sicily who worked him in a stone quarry for nine years, and he came back at forty with a cough and died of it in my house, and the master who sold him was never once asked to explain himself, because the law had been obeyed.' He drank. 'I cannot do anything about the law. But I have found that when a man manufactures a codicil, or cheats an inheritance, or sells a girl he should not have sold, the law will sometimes, if it is pushed in exactly the right place, make him explain himself. That is all the justice there is. It is not much. I have made a living out of it and I am not ashamed of the living, and I would rather be doing it than be a shoemaker.'

Antonia put a purse on the table.

'Twenty thousand,' she said. 'And Hilarus — when you find her, you will tell me first. Not the household. Not a friend. You will tell me, and you will tell me in a room, and there will be no letter.'

'That is the first sensible thing you have said,' said Hilarus, and took the purse.

---

She did not tell anyone. That was the strange part of the summer: she carried it alone for eleven weeks, in the middle of everything else, and there were mornings when she woke and remembered it before she remembered her own name.

She told Myrrhine, in the end, in July, on the roof of the house at the second hour of the night, when the whole city was lying out on its balconies because the rooms were unbearable.

'I have hired the delator,' she said.

Myrrhine did not ask what for. She lay on her back on the tiles with her hands behind her head and looked at the sky.

'Since the spring,' she said.

'Since the Ides of June.'

'And you have been walking around with it for a month.' Myrrhine's voice was flat. 'You could have told me.'

'I could have.'

'Why didn't you?'

Antonia looked up. Above the roof the sky was the brown-orange of a Roman night, and there were three stars visible, which in July was two more than usual.

'Because if I said it out loud in April,' she said, 'it would have been a hope, and hopes are things I have learned to keep in a box. And now it is a search with a fee and a man and a paper, and a search is a business, and I can do business.'

Myrrhine was quiet for a while. Then she said, 'If it is a girl, and she is alive, and she is a slave —'

'I will buy her.'

'If she does not want to be bought?'

'Then I will buy her and free her and give her the money and go away,' said Antonia. 'I have thought about it. I have thought about very little else. I have written eleven letters in my head, and burned all of them.'

'And if she is dead?'

'Then I will know where she is buried, and I will buy the ground, and put a stone on it with her name, and that will be the end of it.'

Myrrhine turned her head.

'You bought the ground at Puteoli,' she said. 'Twenty years ago. You bought an empty piece of a midwife's garden and put a wall around it and never told me why.'

Antonia did not answer.

'I went there,' Myrrhine said. 'In the ninth year. I was in Puteoli on the business of the house and I went and looked at it. There was no stone. There was a wall and a lemon tree and a piece of turned ground, and a woman in the next house said that a rich woman from Rome had bought it and came once a year, in August, and stood in it.'

'You never said.'

'You never said,' said Myrrhine. 'That is the arrangement, isn't it. That is what we agreed when you were thirty and I was twenty-five and we decided to be sensible. I do not ask, and you do not have to say, and we have kept it for sixteen years and it has been a good arrangement and I have hated it for about eleven of them.'

Antonia lay still. On the next roof a man was singing, badly, and a woman told him to stop, and he stopped.

'I am sorry,' Antonia said.

'Do not be sorry. Be careful.' Myrrhine sat up. 'You have hired a man to find a girl, in a summer when the praetor's son is telling every dinner table in Rome that you refused him, and when the curator of the public works has the file on your portico open on his table, and when your banker's brothers-in-law are asking questions. Any one of those things can be survived. All three at once is a house with three fires in it.'

'Then I will put out two,' said Antonia.

'How?'

'By making one of them into an ally,' she said, 'which is what I am going to do to the curator of the public works, and which he is going to hate more than anything I have ever done to him.'

---

The case that made it possible was not hers. It came out of Lusitania, and it had been three years in the making, and by July it had begun to be spoken of in the Forum as a thing that would be heard in the autumn.

A governor named Titus Atilius Rufus had left his province in the fifth year of the emperor's reign, and the provincials had come to Rome with a bill of particulars: extortion, requisitions, a forced loan from the towns, three men flogged, one man's estate seized and given to a favourite, and a system of receipts, kept in duplicate, in which the sums paid by the province and the sums entered in the governor's own book differed by a factor of three. The case had come before the Senate, as such cases do, and had stalled, as such cases do, because half the men who would have to judge it had done something similar in their own provinces and did not care to establish a precedent.

Tarquitius Priscus had taken it up in June.

He had taken it up because he was a jurist and because the law on extortion was clear and because, Antonia thought, he had reached the age at which a man begins to want a monument. He had taken it up in the full knowledge that it would make him enemies, and he had done it, she suspected, precisely because it would: a man who has failed to be sent back to the Danube will find another field.

The difficulty was the receipts.

The province's receipts were in the hands of the publicani, the tax farmers, who had collected them and kept copies, and the copies were in the hands of the Roman agent of the company that had bought the contract for Lusitania — and that company's Roman agent was a man named Pollio, who was Cluvius Naso's partner, and who would not produce a single tablet without Naso's word.

Tarquitius had asked Naso. Naso, who was a banker and not a fool, had said that the tablets were the property of the company and that he would consider the matter with his colleagues, which is what a man says when he intends to do nothing until he is certain which way the wind will blow.

In the third week of July, Naso came to Antonia's house at the ninth hour and told her all of this, and then said, 'He will come to you.'

'He will not.'

'He will. He has no other road. Pollio will not move without me, I will not move without a reason, and the reason is that the praetor's son has spent the last two months telling the city that you keep a disorderly house. If the tablets come out, the case goes forward, and if the case goes forward the Sestii are damaged, because Sestius's brother was legate in Lusitania under Atilius and signed three of the receipts.' Naso spread his hands. 'You are the only person in Rome who has a reason to want both of those things at once and the means to arrange them, and he knows it, and he will hate it, and he will come.'

'What does he want from me?'

'He wants me to produce the tablets, and he will ask you to produce me, and he will call it a public duty, and you and I will both know what it is.' Naso looked at her for a moment. 'Do you want him to come?'

Antonia thought about a room on the Palatine with a bust on a bracket and a chair without a cushion.

'Yes,' she said. 'I want him to come, and I want him to have to ask, and I want him to know, while he is asking, that I am the person he is asking. Not because I am vengeful. Because everything I will ever need from that man will be cheaper if he has once been through that door.'

'And the portico?'

'Is a portico,' said Antonia. 'There are four hundred of them in the fourth region. It was never about the portico.'

---

Before that, she made peace with the praetor's son.

It was not a peace she wanted. It was a peace she judged necessary, in the way that a general judges a bridge necessary, and she went about it the way she went about all such things: in public, with a great deal of courtesy, and with a knife concealed in the courtesy.

The occasion was the games of the Augustan festival, at which the praetor's family had a place of honour, and at which Marcus Sestius Verus would be seen by the whole city. Antonia did not go to the games. She never went to the games; the crowds were a nuisance and the seats were for people who needed to be seen sitting in them. Instead she sent, on the morning of the first day, a gift to his house: a pair of silver cups, old, good, worth perhaps twenty thousand sesterces, with a note in her own hand that said that she had heard that the praetor's son had admired the cups at her table in May, and that she could think of no better home for them than a house that understood hospitality.

The cups had been on her table in May. Cirrus had not admired them. He had spilled wine on the cloth and had not noticed.

The gift did three things at once, and she counted them off to Myrrhine that evening like a woman laying out cards.

'First, it is public. Everyone in the city will know by the fourth hour that Antonia Cassia sent a gift to the house of Sestius, and they will conclude that the quarrel, whatever it was, has been settled. Second, it is expensive and useless, which is the only kind of gift that a proud man can accept from a person he has threatened: he cannot call it a bribe, because a bribe is money, and he cannot call it a submission, because he does not know what it is for. Third, and this is the one that matters —' she turned the ring on her finger '— it puts him in the position of a man who has received a favour from me. He cannot now bring a charge against my house without every man in Rome asking why, four weeks after he accepted twenty thousand sesterces of silver from me, he suddenly discovered that my house was disorderly. He will have to wait a year. A year is a long time, and in a year he will have spent the silver and found something else to be angry about.'

'Unless he simply denies that the gift was from you,' said Myrrhine.

'Then he calls me a liar in public, and I produce the note in my own hand, and he is a man who called a woman a liar about a pair of cups, and no one will believe a word he says about anything else.' Antonia smiled. 'The note is the point. It is four lines and it will cost me a great deal of trouble and it is the most useful thing I have written this year.'

Sestius accepted the cups. He sent back a note of thanks that was three lines long and had been dictated, and a few days later he made a speech in the Senate about the moral condition of the city in which he did not mention Antonia Cassia at all, and Myrrhine, hearing of it, said that Antonia had bought herself a month.

'A year,' said Antonia. 'Six months, at any rate. He is not a patient man.'

---

The other thing that happened in July happened on the Palatine, and she did not hear of it until much later, and she heard it from a woman who had it from a slave who had it from the steward, so it came to her third-hand, and she never knew how much of it was true.

Tarquitius Priscus had gone to the prefect's office on the business of the Lusitania case and had been told, by a clerk with a long face, that the company's tablets would not be forthcoming. He had then gone to Naso, and Naso had told him the truth, which was that the tablets existed, that they were in a strongbox in the Ostian warehouse, and that they would be produced for the Senate if the curator of the public works could arrange for a certain objection to be withdrawn from the file of a certain property in the fifth region.

That is to say: if he would drop the matter of the portico.

Tarquitius had said, in the flat voice of a man who has been insulted by a tradesman, that he did not make bargains of that kind.

Naso had said, with great politeness, that he had not proposed one, and had shown him out.

Three days later the case had begun to be spoken of in the Senate as a case that would fail, and two of the men who had promised to support the prosecution had found reasons to be in Baiae in October. Tarquitius had spent a night with the file, and in the morning he had sent his secretary to the house of Cluvius Naso with a letter that said that the curator's inspection of the fifth region had been delayed by the condition of the streets and would not now begin before the spring.

Naso had replied, with equal politeness, that the tablets would be in Rome by the ninth day of August.

That, Antonia thought, when she heard it, was the end of the affair, and the portico was safe, and Naso was a better friend to her than she deserved, and she had never in her life been so thoroughly insulted by a piece of good news.

She sent Naso a jar of wine and a note that said: *You have made me a woman who was saved by her banker. I will not forget it, and neither, I imagine, will he.*

Naso wrote back: *He will not know it was me. He believes he acted on a point of law. He is a jurist; that is how they think.*

She read that twice, and then she wrote, on the back of a shopping list, because she had nothing else to hand: *No. He knows. A man like that always knows. And now he has been made to give way to me in a matter of law, and he will not rest until he has taken something of equal value, and I have nothing of equal value except the one thing he cannot buy.*

She did not send that. She put it in the strongbox with the four other things, and she was glad, later, that she had kept it, because it turned out to be the truest thing she wrote that year.

---

On the second day of August, Eucharis asked to see her.

The Greek woman was twenty-five and had been in the house six years and was, by common consent, the reason half the men who came through the gate had come. She played the harp better than she sang and sang better than she played, and she had a way of listening to a man that made him feel that he had said something interesting, which is a rarer gift than beauty and lasts longer.

She came into the study and stood with her hands folded, and Antonia, who knew the difference between a woman who wants a favour and a woman who has come to make a statement, put down her stylus.

'Domina, I want to buy myself.'

Antonia leaned back.

'How much have you got?'

'Sixteen thousand sesterces. It is in the chest in my room and it is not stolen and Cinnamus counts it every month and he will tell you the same.' Eucharis's chin came up. 'I want to buy myself, and I want to stay. I have thought about it for a year and I know how it sounds. I want to be a free woman in this house, on wages, with a contract, and I want the contract in writing, and I want it registered, and I want to be able to leave in three years if I choose and to be paid the same whether I choose or not.'

Antonia was silent for a moment. In the corridor outside, a broom went past and stopped and then went on, and someone, somewhere, dropped a pan.

'You have thought about this,' she said.

'I have had nothing else to do with my evenings,' said Eucharis.

'You know what a contract would mean. If you are free and you stay, every man who comes through that gate will know that you are free and can be approached. They will offer you money to leave and set up in your own rooms, and two of them will offer you a house, and one of them will offer to marry you and will mean it and will be a fool, and you will have to decide, every month, whether to stay.' Antonia put her hands flat on the table. 'In this house you are protected by the fact that you are mine. It is not a noble kind of protection. It is the same protection as a good lock. But it is a lock, and the day you are free you will be a woman with sixteen thousand sesterces and no lock at all.'

'I know,' said Eucharis.

'You have been thinking about the wrong thing,' Antonia said. 'You have been thinking that freedom is a thing a person buys once. It is not. It is a thing a person buys every morning, and some mornings it costs more than others, and some mornings you will not be able to afford it, and you will go back to bed and be somebody's property for another week and you will hate yourself for it. I know. I have done it.'

'Then you will refuse.'

'I will not refuse.' Antonia took a tablet and a stylus. 'You will pay me twelve thousand, not sixteen, because the other four will be your first month's rent, and the room over the garden is yours, and the contract will say that you pay a rent, and that you are free to leave with a month's notice, and that the house will not sell your contract to anyone because there will be no contract to sell.' She began to write. 'And it will say one thing more. If you leave, you leave with your money and your clothes and nothing else, and you will not take any of the other women with you, and you will not tell a guest anything that you learned in this house. Not because I will punish you. Because a woman who tells is a woman no house will have, and I am not doing this to give you a way to ruin yourself.'

Eucharis stood very still. Then she said, in a different voice, 'You are crying, domina.'

'I am writing,' said Antonia.

'You are crying and writing.'

'It is the same thing when the writing is a contract,' said Antonia, 'and if you tell anyone I will deny it.' She sanded the tablet and held it out. 'Take it to Cinnamus. He will make the fair copy and he will register it, and he will charge you for the registration because he charges everyone for everything and it is why we are not poor.'

Eucharis took the tablet and held it against her chest with both hands, the way a person holds something that might break.

'Domina,' she said. 'Why twelve and not sixteen?'

Antonia looked up.

'Because a woman who buys her freedom should have something left over for the first bad month,' she said, 'and because when I bought mine I had a hundred and ten thousand and I still woke in the night for a year thinking I had spent it.'

---

The first report came on the last day of July, and it came in a way she had not expected: Hilarus sent a boy with a message asking her to come to his house, which was in the sixth region, above a fuller's, and which she entered by the back stairs with a veil and a hood, feeling absurd.

He had a room at the top with a table and a lamp and eleven boxes of tablets, and he had been, she could see, sleeping in it.

'For the sale of slaves,' he said, 'the tax is four per cent, and the record is kept at the station of the quattuorviri in the town where the sale is made, and a copy goes to the prefect's office in Rome. The town records at Puteoli are in the cellar of the basilica, and the cellar floods in February, and the boxes are stacked by year and nobody has looked at them since the reign of Domitian.' He sat down. 'I found nothing in the town records for that year, because the year is missing. The whole year. It is not rot; it is not water; the box is there and the year before it and the year after it are there, and the year itself is not, and there is a note in the index in a later hand that says "mislaid."'

Antonia sat down opposite him.

'So it is gone.'

'So somebody took it,' said Hilarus, 'which is the best news I have had in a month, because a man does not steal a record unless the record says something. So I went to the man who was clerk of that station in that year, and he is seventy-four and lives in a room in Naples and he remembers the year very well, because in that year the station was visited by a woman from the household at Arretium who paid him eleven sesterces to lose the box.' Hilarus looked up. 'A slave woman. He did not know her name. He described her: fifty years old, a mark on the left hand, a scar through the eyebrow, and a way of speaking that put him in mind of a household he had served in as a boy, which was the household of the Tarquitii at Arretium, because he was born on that estate.'

Antonia's hands were very cold.

'He took nine sesterces,' she said.

'He took eleven sesterces and he lost the box, and then he worried about it for twenty-six years, and when I found him he wept and asked me whether he was going to be prosecuted, and I told him that he was not, and he told me everything he remembered, which was more than I expected, because it turns out that a man who has been worrying for twenty-six years has been rehearsing.' Hilarus turned over a tablet. 'The box was taken by a woman he knew as Sabbia, who was in the household of the Tarquitii, and who was in Puteoli with a child. A girl child, two days old. And Sabbia took the child to a dealer in the town — a man named Caius Oppius Felix, who is dead, but whose business was bought by his partner, whose name is Lucius Annaeus Phoebus, and who is alive and is sixty-two and lives in Rome, on the Aventine, and who has been for thirty years a dealer in children.'

The lamp guttered.

'Where is the girl?' said Antonia.

'That is the next thing,' said Hilarus, 'and it will take another month. Phoebus sold her, and Phoebus will have a record, because dealers keep records, because a dealer who cannot prove where a child came from cannot sell it twice. He will have a record, and the record will have a price and a buyer, and the buyer will be a household or a family or a brothel.' He paused. 'Domina. Before I go to him, I need to know what you want me to do if the answer is a household in Rome, and the household is one that knows you.'

'Why would it know me?'

'Because there are perhaps forty families in this city who would buy a healthy girl child in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Corbulo at a price worth recording,' said Hilarus, 'and a third of them are families whose names are in your account books.'

Antonia stood up. She went to the little window and looked down into the street, where a man was leading a goat and two children were following it.

'You will tell me,' she said. 'And then I will decide. And Hilarus —'

'Domina.'

'When you go to Phoebus,' she said, 'you will not mention my name. You will go as an advocate's clerk, or a man from the prefect's office, or a brother of the child, or anything at all. You will not mention me, and you will not mention the Tarquitii, and you will not mention a price.'

'And if he asks why I want to know?'

'Tell him the truth,' she said. 'Tell him a woman is looking for her daughter. It is the one thing in this city that nobody will think is a lie, because nobody would invent it.'
