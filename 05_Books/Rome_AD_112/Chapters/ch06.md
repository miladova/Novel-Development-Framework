# 6 | The Price of a Girl

Puteoli, that summer.

They told her it was for the sea air.

The villa had been sold, or would be sold; the estate was in the hands of the heirs and the heirs were in Rome, and Faustus had gone up with the accounts in April and had come back with a list. On the list, fourth from the top, between two farm slaves and a pair of mules, in the same hand, was: *Cassia, ex linificio, to the house of Bassa at Puteoli, for her health.*

She saw the list. Faustus left it on the table in the tablinum when he went to speak to the mistress, and she stood over it and read it, and her hands were steady, and she thought: he has written something. It was the only thing she could think. *He has written something, and this is the arrangement, and I am to be got out of the way quietly until it is done, and then there will be a paper.*

The son of the house had gone to Rome in the last week of March, and had not come back. He had been sent for by his sister; there had been a letter, and then a second letter; and he had stood in the library with the second letter in his hand and said, 'I must go. It is about my father's will and there is a difficulty with the land at Volsinii and my sister will not sign without me. I will be back before the summer. I will be back before —' and he had stopped, and looked at her, and said, 'Before.'

'Yes,' she said.

'I will write.'

He did write. Two letters came in April and one in May, and they were careful letters, and the carefulness was itself a kind of tenderness: he asked about the books. He asked whether she had finished the fourth decade of Livy, and he sent a line of Greek that she was to translate and send back, and the line was from Euripides and meant, roughly, that the sea washes away the evils of men, which she thought was a strange thing for a man to send to a woman who was six months pregnant and had never seen the sea.

She read the letters until the wax was smooth. She slept with them under the mattress. She answered the Greek and gave the answer to Faustus to send, and she never knew whether her answers arrived.

---

The house of Bassa at Puteoli stood on the road to the Lucrine lake, in a garden of lemon trees, with a courtyard and a well and a room at the back that had a window looking east. It smelled of the sea, which Cassia smelled for the first time in her life at the age of sixteen, and which she did not like: it smelled of something enormous that had been left out and had gone off, and she could not understand why anyone would build a town next to it.

Bassa was sixty-one that summer and had been a midwife for forty years. She had been a slave of the family, freed by the old consul in the year of the earthquake for services that were never specified, and she had a house, a garden, a freedman's cap that she kept in a chest and never wore, and a way of looking at a woman's belly that was entirely without sentiment and entirely without contempt.

She looked at Cassia's belly on the day she arrived, and said, 'August. Perhaps the end of August. You are small, which is good, and you are frightened, which is normal, and you have been badly fed for the last two months, which I can see in your wrists, so you will eat what I give you and you will not argue with me about it.'

'I am not frightened,' Cassia said.

'You are frightened of me,' said Bassa, 'which is sensible. You will be less frightened in a week. Come and see the room.'

---

The weeks at Puteoli were the strangest of her life, and afterwards she thought of them as a kind of island. She was not working. She had never not worked. She sat in the garden in the mornings and read, and Bassa's two slaves — a woman called Sabbia, who was thirty and sour and kind, and a boy of twelve — treated her with the careful respect that is given to valuable property, and she learned to hate that respect more than she had ever hated being kicked.

She was, for the first time in her life, alone with her own thoughts for whole days together, and she found that she had a great many, and that they were not pleasant company.

There was a girl in the house next door who was pregnant also, the wife of a rope-maker, and she was nineteen and this was her third child and she was not frightened at all. She came over the wall in the afternoons and sat with Cassia, and talked without stopping, about her mother-in-law, and the price of fish, and the scandal of a woman in the town who had left her husband and gone to Rome with a ship's captain, and Cassia listened to all of it and understood that she was being given a lesson in how to be a woman, and that the lesson was being given by someone who had a name.

'Your husband is a sailor?' the rope-maker's wife said, the second week.

'Yes,' said Cassia.

'Where is he?'

'At sea.'

'They are always at sea,' said the woman, comfortably. 'Mine is at sea in his own way. He goes to the docks at the sixth hour and he comes back at the ninth and he has been at sea all day.'

Cassia laughed, and was surprised by it.

---

Bassa asked her, once, in the sixth week, when they were shelling peas in the courtyard.

'What has he promised you?'

Cassia's hands stopped.

'You may tell me or not,' said Bassa. 'I have heard worse. I have heard everything. There is nothing you can say that will make me think less of you, because I have already thought the worst, and it is not you.'

'He is going to free me,' Cassia said. 'There will be a paper. And then —' She stopped. 'And then I do not know.'

'No,' said Bassa. 'That is honest. Most girls would have said something pretty.' She dropped a pod in the bowl. 'I will tell you what I know, and then we will not speak of it again. In forty years I have delivered two hundred children in this town and a hundred and forty of them were born to women who had been promised something. Of the hundred and forty, eleven got the paper. Of the eleven, four got the money with it. Of the four, one is still alive and she keeps a bakery on the harbour and she is the hardest woman in Puteoli and she does not speak to me.' She wiped her hands. 'A paper is not a kindness, girl. A paper is a decision a man makes about his own honour. It costs him nothing and it makes him feel like a god, and it can be taken back by his heirs, and it is worth exactly as much as the man's family allows it to be worth.'

'He is not like that,' said Cassia.

Bassa looked at her for a long moment. There was no cruelty in it. That was the terrible thing.

'No,' she said. 'He is not like that. He is worse. The ones who are like that, the brutes, they sell you in the first month and you know where you are. It is the ones who are not like that who take a year to do it, and who do it kindly, and who send a letter afterwards explaining why it was necessary.' She picked up another pod. 'Eat your peas.'

---

The end of August came. The heat was enormous. The town filled with people from Rome and the bay filled with boats and the nights were so warm that nobody slept before the second hour, and Cassia sat in the courtyard in the dark with her hands on her belly and felt the child turn like something swimming in a sack.

She had begun to talk to it. She had not meant to. But it was there, and it was hers, and it was the only thing in the world that was entirely hers, and she talked to it in a whisper in the dark: about the sea, which she still did not like; about Livy, who had begun with the founding and had got no better; about the plan, which she had made in the garden over many afternoons and which involved a room above a shop and a lamp and a basin and the two of them, and which she had worked out in great detail because there was nothing else to do with her mind.

It was a girl, she had decided. She did not know. She had decided it was a girl because a girl could be kept.

The pains began on the twenty-ninth day of August, at the fourth hour of the night, and lasted thirty-one hours.

---

Afterwards, she remembered it in pieces, and the pieces did not fit.

She remembered Sabbia's hands, which were hard and quick, and Bassa's voice saying, over and over, in the flat tone of a woman doing a job, 'That is right. That is good. Again.'

She remembered the window going from black to grey, and a bird starting outside it, and thinking, with enormous clarity: the bird does not know.

She remembered being given something to drink that tasted of iron and honey and made the room go soft at the edges. She remembered the ceiling, which had a stain in the shape of a boot. She remembered a great pressure and a great tearing and the sound she made, which she had never heard herself make before, and which was not a scream but a kind of lowing, like an animal in a field.

And then she remembered a cry.

She was certain of it afterwards. She was certain for twenty-four years, and the certainty was the thing that kept her alive, and everybody who loved her told her, kindly, that a woman in that condition will hear anything, and that the mind makes what it wants to make.

It was a small sound, thin, and it stopped almost at once. And then there was a great deal of activity in the room that did not include her, and hands, and the murmur of voices at the door, and then nothing.

She woke in the afternoon. The room was hot and the light was yellow and Sabbia was sitting on a stool by the window with her hands in her lap, and she was crying without making any noise at all.

'Where is she?' Cassia said.

Sabbia stood up and went out of the room.

Bassa came in. She had washed and put on a clean dress and she stood at the foot of the bed with her hands folded.

'You had a girl,' she said. 'She was born at the ninth hour of the morning.'

'Where is she?'

'She did not breathe,' said Bassa. 'She was blue when she came out and she did not breathe. I worked on her for half an hour. It was very quick and it was not your doing and there was nothing in the world that could have been done.'

Cassia looked at her.

'I heard her,' she said.

'You heard the door,' said Bassa. 'You heard Sabbia. Women hear all sorts of things; it is the medicine and the blood and the pain, and it means nothing.'

'I heard her cry.'

'You heard what every woman in this town hears when it goes wrong,' said Bassa, and her voice was steady and she did not look away, and that was the thing Cassia remembered afterwards, for twenty-four years: that she did not look away. 'She is buried. It was done this morning, before the heat, in the ground beyond the wall of the garden. There is no stone. There will be no stone; she was not a person in law and there is no stone to be had for nothing, and I am sorry, and I have done this two hundred times, and the ones who get a stone are the ones whose fathers claim them.' She unfolded her hands. 'I will bring you something to eat.'

---

For three days Cassia did not speak. She lay in the room with the window and watched the light move along the wall and the stain on the ceiling in the shape of a boot, and she was not grieving in any way that she could have described. She felt as though a large object had been removed from a room and the room had not adjusted: the world was the wrong size and everything in it was too far away.

On the fourth day she got out of bed and went into the garden, and stood at the wall at the bottom, where the ground was turned over in a place about the length of a man, and stood there for an hour.

On the fifth day a man came to the house.

He was a freedman of the family, or said he was; he had the family's name, which meant nothing, because half of Italy had it. He was about forty, with a shaved head and a good cloak, and he came in the afternoon, and Bassa brought him into the courtyard, and Cassia was sitting there, and he did not sit down.

'You are Cassia, formerly of the household at Arretium.'

'I am.'

'I have the papers.' He unrolled a document. 'You are sold. The price is two thousand four hundred sesterces and it has been paid, and the sale is registered in the town records as of yesterday, and here is the copy.'

She heard the sea.

'Sold to whom?' she said.

'To the house of Caesonius Verus in this town,' said the man, 'which is a respectable establishment, and which has a good name among the merchants who come to the port, and which will treat you better than most, and where you will have work that is suited to you.' He rolled the document. 'There is a clause: you will not leave the house except with the permission of the proprietor, and you will not attempt to communicate with the household at Arretium or with any member of it, and if you do, the proprietor will be within his rights to have you corrected. The house does not sell. It does not sell, and it does not free, and you will not ask it to, because the asking is itself a disorder and the disorder will be corrected.'

'Who ordered it?'

'It is a lawful sale. It does not require an order. It requires a signature, and the signature is a valid signature, and I have seen it, and I have been paid to see it.'

'Whose signature?'

The man looked at her. He had the flat, patient eyes of a man who had done this a great many times.

'The signature of the master of the household at Arretium,' he said. 'And before you ask, because they all ask: he is the only master that household has. There is no other. There is no appeal. There is no letter. There is no man in Rome who will read a letter from a girl in a house in Puteoli.' He looked at Bassa. 'She is to come tomorrow, at the ninth hour, with her things. The price includes the clothes she stands in. Nothing else.'

He went out. Bassa stood in the doorway with her arms folded and her face like a wall.

'Bassa,' said Cassia. 'The burial. Show me the burial.'

'There is nothing to show you.'

'Show me the place.'

'I have shown you the place.' Bassa's voice was hard. 'There is a turned piece of earth at the bottom of my garden and you have stood on it and you will stand on it again if you like, and it is a piece of earth, and that is all it is.'

'Who came, that morning?'

'Nobody came.'

'Somebody came. There were voices at the door. I heard—'

'You heard me and Sabbia and the boy,' said Bassa. 'That is all that was in this house. Now go and pack your things, because I have a house full of women and I cannot keep a girl who is not mine, and if I could I would not, because the man who signed that paper paid for my house and my freedom and I am sixty-one years old and I am not going to be a hero for a girl I have known eight weeks.' She turned away. 'I am sorry. I am sorry. Go and pack.'

---

She went to the house of Caesonius Verus on the last day of August, in the afternoon, and it was not what she had feared, because she had no picture of what she feared, and the house was a house: a courtyard with a fountain, a garden with a vine on a trellis, eleven women, a cook, a doorkeeper, and a proprietor who was fifty and had a bad leg and who told her, with something that was almost kindness, that she would not be expected to work for a month, and that the first rule of the house was that no man touched a woman who was still bleeding, and that he was a businessman and not a barbarian.

Her name in that house was Cassia, and then, within a year, it was Cassia still, because she would not take another, and because Caesonius, who was a businessman, discovered that a woman who would not change her name could be sold as a novelty.

She learned the trade in the way that one learns a thing that must be learned. She learned to read a man in the first three sentences. She learned which ones wanted to be listened to and which wanted to be feared and which wanted, more than anything, to be told that they were not like the others, and she learned that the last was the largest group and the easiest to manage. She learned to keep her own body at a distance from herself, and she learned that the distance could be made and then taken away, and that the taking away was the part that cost.

The first man who came to her was a ship's captain from Massilia, forty years old, with a wife and three children at home and a great terror of the sea, and he sat on the edge of the bed and talked about his youngest daughter for an hour and then could not do anything at all, and wept, and she held him, because it was the only thing left to do.

The second was a magistrate of the town, who did what he did without speaking and left without speaking, and who came back the following week and the week after that.

The third was a young man from Rome who had been at the games and was drunk, and who hurt her, and who was thrown out by the doorkeeper before he had finished, and who came back the next day with a purse and an apology, and Cassia took the purse, because Caesonius took the purse, and the apology she kept.

It was in the fourth month that she spoke her first line as property.

There was a man in the house that night, a wool merchant from Mutina who had been drinking since the afternoon and who had begun, in the manner of such men, to explain the world. He had explained the corn supply, which he understood entirely. He had explained the war in Dacia, which he had not been to. And then he had said, spreading his hands, 'And of course, the whole of this — all of it, the city, the empire, the law — rests on one thing, and the one thing is that some men are born to command and some to serve, and a man who says otherwise is a liar or a fool. There is no third thing. Look at you. You are clever. Anybody can see you are clever. And yet here you are.'

Cassia had learned, by then, to smile at that. She had a smile she used for it, which she had practised in the polished bronze in her room, and which cost her nothing at all except the part of her that had been fifteen in a linen room with the sun coming through green glass.

She did not smile.

'There is a third thing,' she said.

The merchant blinked at her. 'What?'

'A man can be a fool,' said Cassia, 'and still be born to command. I have met six of them this month.'

He stared at her. Then he laughed, uncertainly, and looked around the room to see whether anyone had heard, and there was no one to hear, and that was the whole of it: there was no one to hear, and so nothing happened, and he drank his wine and later he paid and later he went back to Mutina and told a story about a clever whore in Puteoli, and the story went around Mutina for a year and then stopped.

But Cassia lay awake that night in a small hot room above a courtyard with a fountain, and she found that she was shaking, and that it was not fear, and that she had not felt anything like it since the morning she had woken to be told that her daughter was buried at the bottom of a garden.

Two things had happened to her in that house, and she knew it even then.

The first was that she had learned what she was worth.

The second was that she had begun, in a place where nobody could hear her, to say what she thought.

---

She was in the house of Caesonius Verus for six years, and she left it at twenty-two with a hundred and ten thousand sesterces, a freedman's cap, and the name she had refused to change.

The hundred and ten thousand was not a gift. She had made it in the way that a woman in her position can make money, which is to say by being useful to men in ways that they do not recognise as work. In the second year she had begun keeping a book. It had started because Caesonius's accounts were a disgrace — he was a decent man with a bad memory and a worse hand, and he was being robbed by his own cook — and because she had learned the numbers in a linen room at Arretium and had never lost the habit. She balanced his book for him. Then she balanced the book of the wine merchant who supplied the house, who was being robbed by his own son. Then she balanced the book of the widow who owned the insula next door, who had a great deal of property and no idea what it was worth.

She took a fee. It was four per cent, which was less than the bankers charged and more than nothing, and she took it in the name of the house and gave Caesonius a third of it, which he was delighted by, and which made him, for the rest of his life, her ally.

By the fourth year she had eleven clients, and a strongbox, and an arrangement with a scribe who came on the seventh day of each month. By the fifth year she had begun lending: small sums, at the legal rate, to men who could not go to a banker because a banker would ask them why they needed it.

Two things happened in the sixth year.

The first was that Caesonius Verus died, of a dropsy, at the age of fifty-six, and his heir was a nephew from Capua who arrived three days after the funeral with an inventory and an intention to sell everything in the house including the doorkeeper. Cassia sat down with him in the courtyard, and showed him the book she had kept for six years, and explained to him, in the tone of a woman doing him a favour, that eleven of the house's regular clients were hers and not the house's, and that if he intended to sell her he would find that he had bought a building with a fountain in it and a great deal of wine in the cellar, and nothing else.

The nephew, who was twenty-eight and had never run anything, asked her what she wanted.

She wanted her freedom, and she wanted it registered properly, in the town records, with a copy that she kept herself, and she wanted the hundred and ten thousand sesterces that were in the strongbox and that were, by any accounting, hers.

He gave her all of it. He gave it to her because he was frightened of her, which she noticed, and which she remembered, and which she thought about for the rest of her life.

'A woman like you,' he said, at the end, when the papers were done and the money counted, 'will do well in Rome.'

'I am not going to Rome,' she said.

'Everyone goes to Rome,' said the nephew, 'in the end. It is where the money is.'

---

The second thing happened four months later, in the winter, when she went back to the house on the Lucrine road to see Bassa.

She had not seen her in six years. She had not wanted to. But Bassa was old and the sea air had begun to be bad for her, and Cassia had, in the strongbox, a paper that she had paid a lawyer eleven sesterces for, and she went with it on the feast of the Saturnalia, in a good cloak, with her hair up and a slave girl behind her.

The house was the same. The lemon trees were the same. The window that looked east was shuttered against the cold, and Sabbia opened the door, and Sabbia was thin and grey and looked at her for a long moment and then began to cry, and that was the first thing that was wrong, because Sabbia had never in six years cried in front of her except the day the child was born.

Bassa was in the back room, in a chair, with a rug over her knees, and she was seventy and she had shrunk, and she looked at Cassia for a long time with her flat, patient eyes.

'You have done well,' she said.

'I have done well.'

'Caesonius is dead. I heard. He was a decent man, in his way. He never beat a girl who did not steal from him, which in that trade is almost a virtue.' Bassa moved her hand on the rug. 'Why have you come?'

Cassia took the paper out of her sleeve and put it on the table.

'I have bought the ground,' she said. 'At the bottom of your garden. The turned piece of earth. I have bought it from the town, and I have registered it, and I am going to put a stone there, and I am going to put a wall around it, and I want you to tell me what to write on the stone.'

Bassa looked at the paper on the table. Then she looked at Cassia, and her face did something that Cassia had never seen it do.

'There is nothing buried there,' Bassa said.

The room was very quiet. Outside, a gull was making its noise over the garden, and someone in the next street was shouting about a cart.

'Say that again,' said Cassia.

'There is nothing buried there,' said Bassa. 'There was nothing to bury. That is what I have been sitting in this chair for six years wishing I had the courage to write to you about, and I did not, because I am a coward and because I have a house and because the man who signed your paper paid for both.' She put her hands together in her lap. 'The child was born alive. She was born at the ninth hour and she cried, and I put her in the arms of a woman who was waiting in the corridor, and that woman took her out of this house before the sun went down, and I was told that if I ever spoke of it, the paper that freed me would be found to be a forgery, and my house would be taken, and I would die a slave in the house I was born in.'

Cassia found that she was standing. She did not remember standing.

'Who was the woman?'

'I do not know her name. She was a slave of the household at Arretium; she had come up with the man who brought the orders, and she was called Sabbia, and she was not my Sabbia, and I have thought about that for six years as though it meant something, and it does not.' Bassa's voice was steady. It had always been steady. 'The child was to be given to a family. That was what I was told. That was the whole of what I was told: that the child was to be given to a family, and that the mother was to be told it had died, because a mother who knows her child is alive will tear a town apart looking for it, and the family required quiet.'

'What family?'

'I do not know.'

'What did the child look like?'

'She had your hands,' said Bassa. 'That is all. She had your hands and she cried like a cat and she was very small.' She closed her eyes. 'I am sorry. I have been sorry for six years and it has done nothing at all, and now you are here in a good cloak and you have bought a piece of my garden, and I find that being sorry is even less use than I thought.'

Cassia walked out of the house. She walked down the Lucrine road in the winter light with her slave girl behind her, and she walked past the lemon trees and the wall and the town gate and onto the beach, and she stood at the edge of the sea, which she had never liked, and she looked at it for a long time.

She had been told, for six years, that her daughter was dead, and she had grieved a thing that was not there, and the grief had been real, and the ground had been empty. And on the other side of the world, somewhere, there was a girl of six with her hands.

She went to Rome in the spring.

She went with a hundred and ten thousand sesterces, a freedman's cap, a copy of her manumission that she had registered twice, and one name that she had refused to change. She was twenty-two years old. She had no family, no patron, no letters of introduction, and no intention of ever again asking a man to give her anything that she could not take.

The first house she rented was two rooms above a laundry on the Aventine, and the first thing she bought for it was a lock.
