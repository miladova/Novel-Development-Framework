# 15 | The Old Woman's Confession

The message came at the tenth hour of the night, two days before the ides of November, and it came in the hand of the steward of the house on the Palatine, and it said:

*My mother is not well. She has asked for you. She has asked in a manner that suggests she will not be refused and I have learned, at my age, not to argue with her. The gate will be open. Come at once.*

There was no signature. The seal was the boar's head.

Antonia read it in the study with the tablets of the defence spread out on the table and the lamp burning low, and she stood for a moment with the paper in her hand.

'It is the night before the trial,' said Myrrhine.

'It is the night before the trial.'

'You should sleep. You should be asleep now; you have seven hours before the second hour and you will be standing in a basilica in front of half the Senate.'

'She is seventy-eight,' said Antonia, 'and she has been sitting in that house for twenty-five years, and in the spring she told me that she would not be here to see what her granddaughter decided.' She reached for her cloak. 'If she dies tonight I will spend the rest of my life not knowing whether she would have said it, and I have spent twenty-five years not knowing things, and I am finished with it.'

'Then I am coming with you.'

'No.'

'Antonia.'

'No,' said Antonia. 'She will not speak in front of a stranger. She would not speak in front of her own son. She has asked for me, and she has asked for me tonight, which means that she has decided, and a woman of seventy-eight who has decided does not need an audience.' She went to the door. 'Have Brennus walk behind me and wait at the gate. If I am not out by the fourth hour, come and get me.'

---

The house of the Tarquitii at night was the same house and not the same house.

The torches were lit along the street front, and the gate was open, and the steward was waiting with a lamp in the hall, and the masks in the atrium looked out of their cupboards with their holes for eyes. There was no one else in the whole of the front of the house; it was the second hour, and the household was asleep or pretending to be, and the steward walked ahead of her with the lamp and did not speak, and their footsteps went ahead of them through the corridors.

At the door of the small room off the peristyle the steward stopped.

'She is awake,' he said. 'She has been awake since the ninth hour. The physician has been and gone and says there is nothing wrong with her that a younger body would not cure, and she told him that if he came again she would have him thrown out of the window.' He hesitated. 'Domina. There is something you should know before you go in. The young mistress is with her.'

'Marcellina?'

'The young mistress has been with her since the seventh hour. She has not been sent out. I was told to bring you to the room and to leave the door open.' The steward's face, in the lamplight, was old and careful. 'I have been in this house thirty-one years, domina. I have never known the old mistress to leave a door open for a conversation in her life.'

---

In the corridor that ran from the atrium to the peristyle, Tarquitius was standing in a doorway.

He had a lamp in his hand and a cloak over his arm, and he had clearly been there for some time, and he did not come forward. The steward went past him with his own lamp and his eyes down, and Antonia stopped.

'She has asked for me,' she said.

'I know. The steward told me.' His voice was low. 'I have been standing in this doorway for an hour.'

'Why?'

'Because I do not know what she is going to say to you,' said Tarquitius, 'and because I have known for four months that there is something in this house that everyone has agreed not to tell me, and because I am fifty-three years old and I find that I am afraid of a room.' He looked down the corridor at the closed door. 'I could come in. She is my mother. I could come in and sit down and say, "Say it in front of me."'

'You could.'

'I am not going to,' he said. 'I have thought about it for an hour and I am not going to, and I would like you to understand why, because you will otherwise decide that I am a coward, and you would be right, but not for the reason you think.' He shifted the lamp. 'If I go into that room she will tell me what she thinks I can bear. She has been doing it since I was a boy. She told me my father was dying for a year before he died, and she told me in a way that was designed to be borne, and I have never once heard the whole of anything from her while I was in the room.' He stepped back. 'She will tell you the whole of it because you are not her son. Go in. I will be in the study. When it is over, come and tell me whatever she says I may hear.'

'And if she says it may all be heard?'

'Then I will hear it,' said Tarquitius, 'in the morning, after the trial, when I have won, and when I am a man who can afford to be told the truth.'

---

The room smelled of camphor and old wool and the particular dry smell of a body that has been in the same bed too long. There were three lamps and a brazier and a chair by the bed, and the shutters were closed, and Tarquitia Priscilla was propped up on cushions with her hands on the coverlet.

Marcellina sat on a stool at the foot of the bed. She was in her nightgown with a shawl over it and her hair was down her back, and she looked at Antonia as she came in and did not say anything.

'Sit down,' said the old woman. 'Not the chair; the chair is too far. Sit on the bed. I want to hear you breathing.'

Antonia sat on the edge of the bed.

'You look like a woman who has not slept,' said Tarquitia Priscilla.

'I have not slept.'

'Good. Sleep is for people who have nothing to say.' Her blind face turned. 'Marcellina is here because I asked her to be here. I have thought about this for four months, since the day my son came home from a room above a bookseller's and asked me a question in the doorway of this room and I told him to go to bed.' She paused. 'I told him to go to bed. That is what I said. He stood in that doorway with his cloak on and his face the colour of a fish and he said, "Mother, is it true that there was a child?" and I said, "Go to bed, Lucius, it is the middle of the night."'

The brazier ticked.

'He has not asked me again,' she said. 'That is what this family does. We ask once, at the wrong hour, and when we are told to go to bed we go to bed, and we do not ask again for twenty-five years.' She moved her hands on the coverlet. 'There is wine on the table. Pour yourself some and pour nothing for me; I have had my wine and I am allowed one cup a day and I have had it.'

Antonia did not move.

'I am going to tell you three things,' said Tarquitia Priscilla, 'and then I am going to ask you for something, and then you will go home and be tried in the morning. The first thing is what I did. The second is what my daughter did. The third is what my son was told. I have arranged them in that order because it is the order of the guilt and because if I begin with my son I will not get to the end.'

---

The first thing was this.

In the spring of the year, when the household at Arretium was in mourning and the estate was in the hands of the heirs, a steward came to her and told her that the girl Cassia from the linen room was pregnant, and that the child was her son's.

'I had known for a month,' she said. 'I am blind, not stupid. I knew from the way the household spoke in the kitchen and the way the girl walked and the way my son did not go back to the Danube. I had known for a month and I had done nothing, because I was fifty-two years old and I had just buried a husband and there was a daughter in the house who counted everything and a son in Rome who was being destroyed at court, and I told myself that if I did nothing the problem would resolve itself.'

'It did not resolve itself,' said Antonia.

'It resolved itself in the way that problems resolve themselves when a woman does nothing,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'My daughter resolved it.'

She had come up from Rome in the last week of March, and she had gone through the household books, and she had found the entry, and she had come to her mother and said, 'The girl must go.'

'I said yes,' said the old woman. 'I want you to hear that. I did not say, "She is a child, and your brother loves her, and she is carrying your father's grandchild." I said yes, because my daughter had a plan and the plan was tidy and I was tired.' Her hands moved on the coverlet. 'The plan was this. The girl would be sent to Puteoli, to Bassa, who was a freedwoman of the family and a midwife and who could be relied upon. She would be delivered there, quietly, in the summer. The child would be disposed of, and the girl would be manumitted and given a sum and sent away from Italy, and my son would be told that she had gone and that the child had died, and in a year he would marry and it would be over.'

'That was the plan,' said Antonia.

'That was the plan,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'And the first part of it was carried out, and the middle of it was not what I was told, and the last part of it was a lie that I helped to write.'

Marcellina had not moved.

'The middle of it,' said Antonia.

'My daughter went to Puteoli in August,' said the old woman. 'She did not tell me she was going. She told me afterwards, in September, when it was done, and she told it to me as a thing well managed: the child had been born alive, and had been sold the same day to a dealer for two hundred and forty sesterces, and had been entered in the household book at Arretium in her brother's name, and the girl had been sold to a house in Puteoli for two thousand four hundred, and a box of records at Puteoli had been paid for and lost, and everything was tidy.' Her voice was perfectly flat. 'I said to her, "Aurelia, the child is your brother's." And she said, "The child is a slave, and my brother is a man who will be consul, and there will be other children in this house who are born in wedlock and are not entered in a book as a purchase."'

Antonia sat with her hands in her lap and her heart going slowly and heavily.

'And the money,' she said. 'Four thousand sesterces was missing from the household accounts that year.'

'My daughter took the difference,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'Two thousand four hundred for the child, two thousand four hundred for the girl, and four thousand in the account of the steward, and the four thousand went into my daughter's own purse, and she called it the cost of managing the affair.' She turned her blind face. 'I found that out later. When I found it out, I said nothing, because by then she had been managing that estate for two years and my son was in Rome and there was no one else.'

'Six years later,' said Antonia, 'there was a child in your household called Tertia.'

'Yes.'

'You knew.'

'I knew from the day she came into the house,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'The steward brought me the bill of sale, as he brought me every bill of sale, and I had it read to me, and I heard the words "female child, four days, purchased at Puteoli" and I heard the name of the house of the Tarquitii on the bill and I understood everything that had happened in the space of about four heartbeats.' She closed her eyes. 'And then I said, "Enter it in the accounts and put her with Sabbia's sister and see that she is brought up to work." Those were my words. I remember them. I have had twenty-five years to remember them.'

Marcellina made a small sound and put her hand over her mouth.

'You want to know why,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'Everybody wants to know why. My son will want to know why, and the girl will want to know why, and there is no answer that is equal to the question, so I will give you the small one.' She moved her hands on the coverlet. 'I was married at sixteen to a man of thirty-four whom I had met twice, and I came into this house with a dowry and a nurse and a set of rules, and the first rule was that nothing in this house was ever to be discussed at table. My husband was a correct man. He was correct in the Senate and correct with his tenants and correct with me, and in thirty-six years he never raised his voice to me and never once asked me what I thought about anything. I bore him three children and buried one, and I kept his accounts and his peace and his mother's memory, and in all that time the only thing I was ever permitted to decide was what was served at dinner.' She turned her blind face. 'I am not telling you this so that you will be sorry for me. I am telling you because it explains the thirteen years. When a woman has been trained for thirty years to keep the accounts and the peace, and a thing comes into the house that would destroy the accounts and the peace, she does not ask whether it is right. She asks how it may be managed. My daughter asked the same question. She asked it at twenty-nine and she asked it with a knife, and I asked it at fifty-two and I asked it with a pen, and the difference between us is only that she enjoyed it.'

'Did she?'

'She enjoyed it,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'She came back from Puteoli in September and she ate a very good dinner and she told me what she had done, and she was pleased with herself in the way that a woman is pleased when she has done a difficult thing competently. And I sat at the table and said, "You should not have taken the four thousand," and she said, "It was owed to me for the managing," and I said nothing more.' She closed her eyes. 'That was the whole of my resistance. One sentence about money. I did not say one word about the child.'

---

'I did nothing for thirteen years,' said the old woman. 'I want that in the account. Thirteen years. She was in the linen and then in the kitchens and then in the villa's office, because she could read, and she read the way her mother read, and it never once occurred to me that she was doing it in the same room in which her mother had done it. I did nothing and I said nothing and I watched her grow up in my house and I told myself that she was well fed and well treated and not beaten, and that was the whole of the mercy available in the world, and that I had done what any woman in my position would do.'

'And then you freed her,' said Antonia.

'And then I freed her,' said Tarquitia Priscilla, 'when she was nineteen, by the council's leave, at a cost of eleven hundred sesterces and a favour to the prefect's wife, and I gave her four thousand sesterces and a trade and sent her to Rome, and I told her nothing.' Her voice did not change. 'I told her that she had been bought as an infant and that her mother was dead. I told her that in this room, with the door open, sitting where you are sitting now, and she listened to me and she was nineteen and she said, "Was she kind?" and I said, "Yes." That was the only true thing I said to her in the whole conversation, and I do not know whether it was true.'

Antonia looked at her hands.

'And your son,' she said. 'What was he told?'

---

The second thing was a letter, and the third thing was the writing of it.

In September of that year, when the child was a month old and the girl was in the house at Puteoli and the household at Arretium was in mourning and in debt, Tarquitia Aurelia wrote to her brother in Rome.

She wrote it at the table in the tablinum in the afternoon, and she brought it to her mother and read it aloud, and asked whether it was right.

'I was in this room,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'I was in this room with a rug over my knees, exactly as I am now, and my daughter read me a letter that said that the girl had been delivered of a dead child at Puteoli and had gone, and that the household had done what was necessary, and that he was not to write to her or to speak of it again, because their father had died in the spring and the family could not survive another scandal in the same year.' She stopped. 'And I said, "It is well written. Say that the girl was paid and went willingly; it will be easier for him."'

The brazier ticked.

'That is the whole of it,' said the old woman. 'I sat in this room and I made the lie easier for him to believe. I did it because I could not think of any other way to keep the house standing, and because I had spent thirty years keeping the house standing, and because the house was the only thing I had ever been given to do.' Her blind eyes were wet. 'And he believed it, and he has believed it for twenty-five years, and he has been a man with a cupboard in his study and a letter in a strongbox and a woman in this city whom he mentions every year in the Senate without naming her, and I have watched him do it and I have said nothing, and I have told myself that the alternative was to tell him that his sister had sold his daughter, and that he would have destroyed her, and that the family would have been torn in two.' She turned her face toward Antonia. 'It is not a good reason. I have had twenty-five years and it is not a good reason. But it is the true one.'

Antonia sat on the edge of the bed with the three lamps burning and the brazier ticking and the whole of the last twenty-five years arranging itself, quietly and terribly, into a shape she could see.

'Aurelia,' she said. 'Is she alive?'

'She is at Tibur,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'She is seventy-nine, like me. She has a disease of the joints and she cannot walk and she is in a great deal of pain, and she has been in a great deal of pain for eleven years, and I have visited her once a year and I have not once in that time told her what I think of her.' She closed her eyes. 'I have not told her that I know about the four thousand sesterces. I have been waiting, I think, for a moment when it would do some good. There is no such moment. There never is. You wait for it and it does not come and then you are seventy-eight and your son is fifty-three and there is a girl in the fourth region who counts under her breath.'

---

'What do you want?' said Antonia.

The old woman was quiet for a moment.

'Two things,' she said. 'The first is small and the second is not, and I have no right to either.'

'Ask.'

'I have written a letter,' said Tarquitia Priscilla, 'to the girl. To Tertia. I wrote it in the summer, after my son came home from the bookseller's, and I have not sent it, because I am a coward and because I did not know whether she would want it, and because a letter from a blind old woman is not a thing a girl of twenty-five can refuse.' She put her hand under the pillow and brought out a folded paper. 'I am not asking you to give it to her. I am asking you to read it, and then to decide, and if you decide not to give it to her I will not ask again. It says what I have just said to you. It does not ask her for anything. It does not ask her to come to this house, and it does not ask her to forgive me, and it does not call myself her grandmother, because I have not earned the word.'

Antonia took the paper.

'And the second thing?'

'Marcellina,' said the old woman.

Marcellina looked up.

'The girl is to be married in the winter,' said Tarquitia Priscilla, 'to a boy with a great name and no money and no sense, and she is going to refuse him, and she is going to refuse him in public, and it is going to be the talk of the city for a month. I know this because I have known her since she was born and because she has been sitting on that stool for four hours without saying a word, which is what she does before she does something.' She turned her blind face toward her granddaughter. 'I want you to let her do it in your house. Not here. There will be a scene here, and her father will say things he will regret for the rest of his life, and she will be alone in a house with a man who cannot bear to be argued with.' She turned back. 'She has nowhere to go. If she goes to my daughter at Tibur she will be eaten alive. If she goes to her nurse she will be hidden in a back room and married off in a year to a man who is worse. She will come to you, because you are the only woman she knows who has ever refused anything, and I am asking you to take her in for the winter, and to teach her the things you know, and to let her find out what she is before she is somebody's wife.'

Antonia looked at Marcellina.

'And what do you want?' she said.

Marcellina's voice was steady and her hands were not.

'I want to be in a house where nobody has a cupboard,' she said.

---

At the door the old woman said, 'Cassia.'

Antonia turned.

'Tomorrow,' said Tarquitia Priscilla, 'when you stand in that basilica, they will ask you questions that have nothing to do with the boy. They will ask you what you charge, and how many men have been in your house, and whether you have ever taken money from a married man, and whether you have ever been in love. They will ask it politely, because they are senators, and it will be the same question each time, and the question is: what is she?'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'Do not answer it,' said the old woman. 'Every woman who has ever stood in that place has tried to answer it, and every one of them has lost something in the answering. Say only what is true and no more; be as dull as a ledger; make them ask the same question three times and let them hear themselves doing it.' Her blind face was turned toward the door. 'And when the advocate for the other side has finished, and the court is quiet, and they are all waiting for you to be clever — say nothing. Let the silence be theirs. You are the only person in that room who has nothing to explain, and if you can sit there for the length of a hundred heartbeats without explaining yourself, you will have won more than the case.'

'That is good advice,' said Antonia.

'I have had twenty-five years to think of it,' said Tarquitia Priscilla, 'and no one to give it to.'

---

Marcellina came with her to the door.

'I will come,' she said. 'Not tomorrow. Tomorrow you will be in a basilica and I would be one more pair of eyes on you. In three days, when the court has adjourned for the games, I will come, and I will bring nothing but a box of my own clothes, and I will be a woman of nineteen with a dowry and a name and no idea what to do with either.' She stopped in the corridor. 'Domina. May I ask you something, before you go?'

'Ask.'

'My grandmother says that you refused everything. She says you have been offered the house and the name and the quiet and you have refused all of it, and that you are going to be tried tomorrow by men who will never in their lives offer you anything.' Marcellina's hands were clenched at her sides. 'Why? Not why you refused. I understand why you refused. Why do you stay? You could have taken a ship nine years ago, or eleven, and gone somewhere with no Senate and no praetor and no man on the Palatine, and nobody would have known your name, and you would have been free in the way that nobody in Rome is free.'

Antonia looked at her.

'Because I built it,' she said. 'Every stone of it. Not the stones; the other thing. The book of contracts, and the wages, and the eleven women who can read their own contracts because I had them taught, and the fact that when a girl in this city is sold out of a house she has somewhere to go that is not a brothel and not a gutter.' She pulled her cloak around her shoulders. 'If I take a ship, all of that is a story that a man tells in a tavern about a rich woman who ran away. If I stay and I am tried and I win, then it is a thing that happened in Rome, in a basilica, in front of the Senate, and for twenty years every woman in this city who is told that she cannot will be able to say, "Antonia Cassia did."' She opened the door. 'That is worth more than Massilia. It is worth more than the house. And it is the only thing I have ever owned that nobody gave me.'

---

Antonia walked home at the fourth hour with the letter in her sleeve and the sky beginning to grey over the roofs, and Brennus behind her, and the streets full of the first carts and the first slaves and the first of the city's morning.

The house was awake when she came in. Myrrhine had the lamps lit and the water hot and the good dress laid out, and Cinnamus had the tablets in their bundles and Tertia's summary in the fair copy with a ribbon on it, and Meno had swept the step and the doorkeeper's bench was clean.

Antonia stood in the tablinum and looked at the four bundles on the table.

'Well?' said Myrrhine.

'There was a letter,' said Antonia. 'Written in September by a woman of seventy-nine and read aloud to her mother, and the mother said, "Say that the girl was paid and went willingly; it will be easier for him to believe."' She sat down. 'It was not the man on the Palatine who sold me. It was his sister, who is dying by inches at Tibur, and I am almost sorry that she is, because I have spent twenty-five years hating a man and it turns out that the man was only a fool and a coward and his sister was the one with the knife.'

'And does that help?' said Myrrhine.

'No,' said Antonia. 'It does not help at all.' She looked at the window. 'But I know now. And in an hour I am going to stand up in a basilica and be tried by men whose fathers bought and sold people like me, and I am going to be defended by a man who was told that his daughter died and believed it for twenty-five years, and I am going to know, while they are speaking, exactly what all of it was, and nobody in that room will know that I know.' She stood. 'Bring me water. And Myrrhine — after today, whatever happens, I want the household in the garden at the ninth hour, all of them, and I want to tell them myself.'

'Tell them what?'

'That the house is closing,' said Antonia, 'and that every one of them will have a deed, a sum, and a trade, and that it will be done properly and in writing and in front of witnesses, and that it will be the last thing I do as the woman who owns this house.' She picked up the bundles. 'And then I am going to go and be tried for my life, and I would like to have it done first, so that if they convict me they will know that I had already started giving it away.'
