# 21 | A House Divided

The dinner was on the twenty-fourth of December, the day before the Saturnalia, and it was the first dinner party Antonia had given in eleven years at which nothing was for sale.

She had the small dining room done, not the great one; the one with five couches and the wall painting of the garden that was old and had been repainted twice. She had the good silver out and the ordinary wine, because the good wine would have been a statement and the ordinary wine was better anyway. She had the cook make the food that the household ate on feast days and not the food that was made for guests: a kid roasted with coriander, lentils, bread, a cheese from the country, and a honey cake.

Tertia came at the ninth hour with the woman she lived with, who was called Sabina, and who was a midwife of thirty-four with a broad face and a direct manner and a willingness to say exactly what she thought, which she demonstrated in the first quarter of an hour by looking at the mosaic in the atrium and saying, 'That is very old. You should have it lifted; there is water under this floor and in ten years the whole of it will be gone.'

'It is on the list,' said Antonia.

'What list?'

'The list of things I am going to do when I have closed the house and have nothing else to do but look after my own property,' said Antonia. 'It has eleven items on it and they are all extremely boring, and I find that I am looking forward to them more than I have looked forward to anything in years.'

They ate. It was, Antonia thought afterwards, one of the strangest evenings of her life: four women at a table — herself, Myrrhine, Tertia, Sabina — in a room that for eleven years had been the scene of a business, eating a family meal on a family feast day.

Sabina asked questions all evening and did not apologise for any of them. She asked Myrrhine how she had come to the house and what she did and whether she was paid, and Myrrhine answered all of it, and by the end of the meal the two of them were deep in a conversation about the price of linen that went on for a quarter of an hour and excluded everyone.

Tertia was quiet. She ate, and she looked at the room, and she looked at Antonia, and at the end of the honey cake she said, 'I have read the letter.'

Antonia put down her cup.

'It took me eleven days,' said Tertia. 'I carried it in my sleeve for a week and then I put it on the table in my room and looked at it for four days, and then I read it in the morning with Sabina in the room, and it is four pages long, and the first three pages are an account of what happened and the last one is about me.' She turned her cup around. 'She says that she does not ask me to come to the house, and she does not call herself my grandmother, and she says that she has written it because she is seventy-eight and does not intend to die with it. And then she says one thing that I have not been able to stop thinking about.'

'What?'

'She says that when I was four days old and she had the bill of sale read to her, she understood everything that had happened in the space of about four heartbeats, and that what she felt was not horror. It was relief. Because it was tidier.' Tertia looked up. 'She has written down the worst thing about herself. Nobody has ever done that to me before. My whole life, people have been telling me things that made them look well.'

'That is what she is,' said Antonia. 'She is a woman who has been correct for sixty years and who has discovered, at the end, that correctness is not the same as goodness, and she has written it down because she is out of time to do anything else.'

'And the other one,' said Tertia. 'At Tibur.'

'What about her?'

'I am going to go,' said Tertia. 'In January. Not to forgive her and not to shout at her. I am going to go and stand at the foot of her bed and look at her for as long as I want, and then I am going to ask her three questions, and if she answers them I will leave, and if she does not answer them I will leave.' She pushed her plate away. 'Sabina says I should not go. She says that a dying woman in pain is the only person in the world who cannot be made to answer for anything, and that I will come away feeling as though I have been cheated.'

'You will,' said Antonia.

'I know,' said Tertia. 'I am going anyway. Because if I do not go, then in twenty years I will be a woman of forty-five who did not go, and I have had enough of my life being arranged by other people's silences.'

There was a small silence.

'Well,' said Myrrhine, 'that is the most sensible thing anyone has said at this table all evening.'

And then Tertia said, 'I have a question for you, domina, and it is not a fair one, and I am going to ask it now because it is the Saturnalia and because I have had wine.'

'Ask.'

'If I said that I wanted to be in this house,' said Tertia. 'Not as your daughter. As the woman who keeps your books, at a wage, with a room. If I said that in March, when the house closes, I wanted to be the one who closes it with you, and that I wanted to be the one who opens whatever comes next.' She met Antonia's eyes. 'What would you say?'

Antonia sat with her hands on the table.

'I would say that you should not,' she said.

'Why?'

'Because you have a place in the fourth region and eleven thousand sesterces and a name you have made for yourself, and because a woman who comes to work in the house of a woman who might be her mother will never find out whether she is any good, because everyone will be polite to her,' said Antonia. 'And because in three years, when you have decided that you hate me, you will have no way of leaving that is not also a way of leaving your mother, and I have been in that position and it is the worst position in the world.'

'That is a very careful answer,' said Tertia.

'It is a careful answer because it is the most important question anyone has asked me all year,' said Antonia. 'But there is a second half of it, and I am going to say the second half, and then you will know what kind of woman you are dealing with.' She put her cup down. 'The second half is that I would like it more than anything I can think of, and if you asked me in the spring, when I am tired and the house is empty and everything I have built is gone, I do not know whether I would be strong enough to say no to you. So the answer is: ask me now, while I am still strong, and I will say no. If you wait until March, I will say yes, and it will be the wrong yes, and you will spend the rest of your life in a house where nobody has ever been able to say no to me.'

Tertia looked at her for a long moment.

'All right,' she said. 'I will not ask in March.'

---

The Saturnalia itself was the last festival the house kept together, and it kept it properly.

For three days the household did what Roman households do at the Saturnalia: it pretended, with great formality, that the order of the world was reversed. The women drew lots for who would serve at table and who would sit. Meno drew the lot to sit at the head and spent the whole of the first evening in a state of profound discomfort, and then, on the second evening, discovered that he enjoyed it, and on the third became insufferable and was told so by the cook.

The baker's family came up from their new shop with eleven loaves and stayed for the day. Vibia Lucana came, and Celer, and Naso with his wife and his brothers-in-law, who had by then decided that Antonia was a very sound risk and were extremely polite to her. Marcellina presided over the household's own little ceremony of lamps and gave out the presents, and she gave Antonia a stylus with a silver case, which was the first present she had ever bought in her life with money she had earned, and she said so, and then had to leave the room.

Tertia came on the second day with Sabina and did not preside over anything and sat at the end of the table with her wine and watched the household, and at the end of the evening she said, to Antonia, in the doorway, 'They are not afraid of you.'

'No,' said Antonia.

'In my shop, the people who work for the builder are afraid of him. Not because he is cruel; because he is the one who pays. And in this house nobody is afraid of the one who pays, and one of them told you at dinner that your hair was a mess and you said thank you.' She pulled her cloak around her. 'I am not going to say that it is a good house. I have read the books and I know what it is. But it is a strange one, and I am beginning to think that strange is the best that can be said for anything in this city.'

The trial of Prima took four days in the middle of January, in the same basilica, before a different panel of judges, and Antonia sat behind her every day.

It was not the same kind of case. There was no great speech and no crowd of eight thousand; the hall was half full and the weather was bad. The prosecution was conducted by the prefect's office, not by an injured family, and it was conducted without much enthusiasm, and the charge was homicide, and the man who had died was named Gaius Titius Saturninus and he had been a hired man with four convictions and no family and a widow in the fourth region who did not appear.

The facts were not in dispute. Meno testified to the ladder and the crowbar and the jar of pitch. Pamphilus testified to the wound that had killed Brennus. The man who had been taken alive testified, from the court's custody, to the whole of it, including the name of the man who had paid him and the sum — eleven thousand sesterces, paid in two parts, in the wineshop by the Ostian gate — and he was believed, because he was a criminal who had nothing left to gain and because he wept.

The dead man had been on the ground, and the law on that point was not simple, and the prosecutor made the most of it.

Tarquitius spoke for an hour and a half. He spoke about a house at night, and four men over a wall, and eleven women, a cook, and a boy of fifteen. He spoke about what the law permitted a householder to do in defence of a house, and about how the law had always distinguished between a killing in a courtyard and a killing in a street. He spoke about what a woman could reasonably be expected to do at the second hour of the night with an iron bar in her hands and a man with a knife in front of her, and he said that the law had been written by men who had never been in that position and that it had never once been written for the person who was.

Then he said: 'The prosecutor has asked you to consider whether the force was proportionate. I ask you to consider proportionate to what. To a man on the ground? Or to a night in which one man had already been killed in that garden, and three others were still standing?'

The court took two hours to vote.

Prima was absolved, eleven to nine, with two of the judges adding a written opinion that the case should never have been brought.

She came out of the basilica into a small crowd, not eight thousand, perhaps three hundred, and it was a different crowd from November: it was women, mostly, and not the sort of women who came to Antonia's house, but shopkeepers and the wives of artisans and a group of seven or eight who worked in the fullers' yards, and they stood at the bottom of the steps and did not cheer. They simply stood and looked at her, and one of them, an old woman with a basket on her arm, said, loudly, 'That is the one,' and they all nodded, and that was all.

---

Tertia went to Tibur in the third week of January, and she came back the same day, and she came to the house in the evening.

Antonia was in the study. Tertia came in with her cloak still on and sat down in the chair without being asked, which she had never done before, and said, 'I want to tell you about it and then I do not want to talk about it again.'

'Tell me.'

'The house is small and it has a garden with a wall and a fig tree. There is a nurse and a cook and a man who carries her. She is in a room at the back with the shutters open because she says the dark makes the pain worse, and she is very thin, and her hands are —' Tertia stopped. 'Her hands are the worst of it. They are turned. She cannot hold a cup and the nurse holds it for her and she hates it.'

Antonia said nothing.

'She knew who I was before I said my name,' said Tertia. 'She looked at me for a long time and she said, "You have the Tarquitius jaw and you have come to look at me." And I said, "I have come to ask you three questions." And she said, "Ask them, and then go, because I am in a great deal of pain and I cannot be looked at for long."'

'What did you ask?'

'The first was: did you know that the child was alive when you sold it? And she said yes.' Tertia's voice was steady. 'The second was: did you ever think about it afterwards? And she said, "Every day, and it has been no use to you at all, and I am not going to pretend that it has been a kind of penance, because a penance is a thing a person does for themselves."' She looked up. 'And then I asked the third one, and I had not planned it; I had planned to ask her about the four thousand sesterces, because that was on my list. But I stood at the foot of her bed and I asked her instead: "What did my mother look like?"'

Antonia's hands were cold.

'And she said, "She was small and quick and she had a way of standing in a doorway with her hands folded, and she could read, which was a nuisance, and she was in love with my brother, and I did not think about her at all. I thought about the household."' Tertia's chin was up. 'And then she said, "Do not do what I did. Whatever it is that you are going to do with your life, do the other thing." And then she told the nurse to take me out, and I went.'

'And that was all?'

'That was all,' said Tertia. 'It took a quarter of an hour. I was in that house for a quarter of an hour and I have been twenty-five years getting there.' She stood up. 'I am not going back. I am not going to her funeral and I am not going to pretend to be sorry when she dies, and I am not going to be a good and forgiving woman, and if that disappoints you then it disappoints you.'

'It does not disappoint me,' said Antonia.

'Good,' said Tertia, and went to the door, and stopped. 'She said one thing more. When I was going out. She said, "Tell my brother that I did it for the family and that I have been repaid."' She looked back. 'I am not going to tell him. I am going to tell you, and you may do what you like with it.'

---

The house came apart in February.

It did not come apart in a scene. Antonia had expected a scene, and there was none. It came apart in a series of ordinary mornings, in the way that houses do.

Chloe went first, to Ostia, with the deed for the fuller's business and a letter, and she cried and said she would write every month and she wrote twice.

The two cooks went to the cookshop near the Macellum, and took the boy from the kitchen with them as an apprentice, and he came back every second day for a month to see Meno and then stopped coming, which was, Antonia thought, exactly right and exactly what should have happened.

Eucharis stayed until the last week. She had found a household at Neapolis, not as a woman of the house but as a music teacher to the daughters of a shipping family, and it was, she said, either the best thing that had ever happened to her or a slow death by respectability, and she had not yet decided which. She came to Antonia's study on her last evening with the contract in her hand.

'I have read it eleven times,' she said.

'You negotiated it yourself. You wrote two of the clauses.'

'I know. I have read it nine times to see whether there was a trap in it, and there is not, and I find that I am disappointed.' Eucharis put the contract down. 'I have been in this house six years and for six years I have known exactly what I was: a woman in a house who could be sold. And now I am a free woman with a contract and a room and a wage and I am going to Neapolis to teach the daughters of a man who has never in his life had to think about what a woman costs, and I am frightened, domina. I am more frightened now than I was when I was nineteen and had nothing, because when I had nothing there was nothing to lose.'

'That is how it works,' said Antonia.

'I know it is how it works. I would like you to tell me that it is worth it.'

Antonia looked at her.

'I cannot tell you that,' she said. 'I can tell you that on the other side of the fear there is a thing that no woman in your position has ever had, which is a bad month that is your own bad month, and a good year that is your own good year. And I can tell you that in eleven years I have been frightened every single day, and that I have never once wished I had stayed in a house where somebody else owned me.' She stood up. 'And Eucharis — when you are in Neapolis and the daughters are insufferable and the family is respectable and you are bored to the edge of madness, you will write and tell me, and I will write back and tell you that I am also bored, and we will both be lying, and it will help.'

Eucharis laughed and then, unexpectedly, put her arms around her, and Antonia stood in her own study being embraced by a freedwoman of twenty-six and did not know what to do with her hands.

---

Marcellina went to see her father on the eleventh day of February.

She had been in the house since November. She had worked in it: she had done the accounts with Cinnamus, and learned to keep the book, and stood behind Antonia in the basilica four days in January with a stylus and a tablet. She had also, in the way of a girl of nineteen who has never had a job, been extremely annoying; she had rearranged the library twice and had opinions about the kitchen, and she and Myrrhine had had two arguments, one of which had ended with Myrrhine saying, 'You are not the mistress here and you will not be the mistress here, and if you want to be useful you will learn to ask before you decide.'

She went on the eleventh day in a plain dress and no jewellery except a ring her grandmother had given her, in Antonia's own litter, with Brennus's replacement — a man named Faustus, who was not a replacement, and who was old and slow and bore it patiently when people called him by the wrong name.

She was gone four hours.

She came back at the ninth hour with her eyes red and her chin up and went into the small room off the library and sat down.

Antonia came in and sat on the stool opposite her and said nothing.

'He has agreed to the annulment of the betrothal,' said Marcellina. 'There is nothing to annul, as it happens, because the contracts were not signed; the boy's father had been putting it off since October because he wanted the dowry in cash and my father wanted to pay in land. So it is not a scandal and it will not be in the Acta, and Gracilis has already been seen at the house of another girl's father, and everybody in the city will have forgotten it by March.' She blew her nose. 'He was very calm. That was the worst thing. I had prepared for shouting, and he was very calm, and he said that he had been expecting it since November, and he asked me whether I had anywhere to live, and I said yes, and he said, "The house of Cassia," and I said yes, and he said, "Then I will not ask you to come home."'

'And then?'

'And then we sat in the study for an hour and did not say anything.' Marcellina's hands were in her lap. 'He asked me whether I was happy. He has never asked me that in my life. I said I did not know, which was the truth, and he said that he had never been happy either, and that he had decided at some point that happiness was not the point, and that he did not know any more whether he had been right or whether he had simply been a coward for fifty years.' She wiped her eyes. 'And then he said the thing that I have not been able to stop thinking about. He said: "I have a daughter I have never met who is twenty-five years old and lives above a bakery in the fourth region, and I have known for four months, and I have not gone, and I am not going to go, because she has asked for a month and because I gave my word. And I find that I have spent the whole of my life keeping my word to people who did not need it and breaking it to the people who did."'

Antonia sat very still.

'He was talking about me,' she said.

'No,' said Marcellina. 'He was talking about himself. He has been talking about himself for fifty-three years and he has only just noticed.' She looked up. 'I am going to go and see my grandmother. Tomorrow, in the morning. She is seventy-nine and she is blind and she has been in that house with the whole of it for twenty-five years, and I am going to sit with her and tell her about the trial and about Tertia and about the dress I have ruined with ink, and I am not going to ask her anything at all.' She stood up. 'And then I am going to come back here and learn to keep a book properly, because Cinnamus says I am careless and he is right.'

---

On the last day of February, two men came from the office of the curator of the public works and asked to see the owner of the house.

They were civil. They were extremely civil. They sat in the study with a plan on the table between them and explained that the emperor's building programme had been extended, that the new baths would require an aqueduct branch on the north slope of the Esquiline, and that a corridor of land would be required for it, and that the house of Antonia Cassia stood inside the corridor.

'Required,' said Antonia.

'Bought,' said the elder of the two men. 'The treasury does not expropriate; it purchases at valuation. The valuation has been made and it is a fair one; you may inspect it at the office of the curator, and you may appeal it to the prefect, and the prefect will hear you in his own time, which at present is about eight months.'

'And what is the valuation?'

He told her. It was, she saw at once, a fair valuation of a house on the Esquiline in the ordinary way of things, and it was less than half of what the house was worth with the business in it, and rather more than she had paid for it eleven years before.

'And if I refuse?'

'Then the matter goes to the Senate,' said the man, 'and the Senate will confirm the purchase, because the emperor is building an aqueduct, and no one in Rome has ever successfully refused an aqueduct.'

Antonia looked at the plan.

'There is something I do not understand,' she said. 'You have been extremely civil, and you have come in person, and there are two of you, and you have brought a plan. That is not how the treasury behaves. The treasury sends a clerk with a notice.' She looked up. 'Who sent you?'

The two men looked at each other.

'The curator instructed us,' said the elder, 'to say that he had instructed us, and that you would ask, and that the answer is that the corridor could be moved eleven feet to the north, and there is a house in the way, and that house belongs to the guild of the sandalmakers, and the guild has a stronger claim on the treasury than a private owner, and the moving of the corridor is a matter for the curator's discretion.'

'And why would the curator use his discretion in my favour?'

'He did not say,' said the man. 'He said that you would know.'

They went away. Antonia sat in the study with the plan on the table and the lamp burning, and Myrrhine came in and looked at her.

'Well?'

'He has moved an aqueduct eleven feet,' said Antonia.

'Why?'

'Because he is a man who has spent his whole life being correct,' said Antonia, 'and he has discovered that correctness is not the same as goodness, and he has decided to try the other thing, and he has begun with the only thing he has any power over, which is an aqueduct.' She put her hand flat on the plan. 'It will not save the house. The house is finished; I decided that in September and I have not changed my mind, and I would not keep it now if he moved the aqueduct a mile. But he has done it, and he has done it without telling me, and he has told two clerks to tell me that I would know why, and that is the first gift anyone has given me in twenty-five years that had nothing to do with money.'

Myrrhine sat down.

'You are going to have to decide what you are doing with him,' she said. 'Not this month. Not next. But there is a man on the Palatine who has moved an aqueduct for you and who has a daughter he has not met, and you are going to have to decide what he is to you, and you are going to have to decide it before you are both old.'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'Do you?'

'Yes,' said Antonia. 'And I have decided one thing, and it is the only thing I have decided, and you may hold me to it. I am not going to take the bargain.'

'Which bargain?'

'The one you told me not to take in September. The one where he saves me and I close the house and I am grateful to him for the rest of my life.' She looked at the plan on the table. 'If we are ever anything to each other it will be because we have both looked at the whole of it — the villa, the linen room, the child, the letter, his sister's knife, his mother's silence — and we have decided, with our eyes open, in the light, what we are going to be.' She folded the plan. 'And that conversation has not happened. It has been postponed eleven times this year by a trial, a death, an aqueduct, a girl of nineteen and a boy with a bucket. It is going to happen, and it is going to happen in the spring, and it is going to be the last thing I do in this house.'
