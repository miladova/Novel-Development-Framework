# 13 | Baiae

The letter came on the second day of October, and it was written by Sabbia, and it was four lines long.

*Domina. She will not last the winter. She has asked for you every day since the twenty-ninth of August, when you did not come, and she has said to me that she does not blame you, which is how I know that she does. If you want to see her alive you should come now. She cannot write and I cannot write better than this.*

Antonia read it standing in the atrium with her cloak still on.

'Saddle the carriage,' she said.

'It is the fourth hour,' said Cinnamus. 'You will be on the road in the dark and you will be at Puteoli by the second hour of the morning, and there will be no inn open and no gate open and the road through the Pomptine is bad in October.'

'Saddle the carriage.'

'Domina—'

'Cinnamus.' She turned. 'I did not go on the twenty-ninth. I have gone every year for twenty years, and this year the house was closed and there was a dead boy and a trial coming and I told myself that a woman with a dead boy in her house does not go to Puteoli in August to stand in an empty garden. And she sat in that room on the twenty-ninth and waited for me, and I was not there, and she is eighty and she is dying and she has sent me four lines because she cannot write more.' She took off her cloak and put it down on the bench. 'I am going to Puteoli. You may saddle the carriage or I will hire one in the street and go without a guard, and if you think I will not, you have not been paying attention for eleven years.'

Cinnamus went to saddle the carriage.

---

They took the Via Appia as far as Terracina and then the coast road, and they went in the rain, which began at Aricia and did not stop until the sea. Myrrhine sat opposite her with a rug over her knees and a basket of food she had made the kitchen put up in a quarter of an hour, and she did not say anything for the first two hours, and then she said:

'You will not be able to save her.'

'I know.'

'And you will try.'

'Yes.'

'And you will spend the winter paying for the physicians and the wax and the funeral and the ground, and you will do it because it is the only thing you can do, and it will not be about her at all.' Myrrhine looked out at the rain. 'It will be about the twenty-ninth of August and a garden and a woman who did not come.'

Antonia watched the rain on the window.

'Yes,' she said.

'Good,' said Myrrhine. 'As long as you know.'

---

Puteoli in October was a town that had put its shutters up. The great harbour was still working — the Alexandrian fleet had sailed and the African fleet was loading, and there were ships at the mole and stevedores on the quay and the whole machine of the grain trade grinding on — but the villas along the Lucrine road were closed, and the bathing beaches were empty, and the shopkeepers who had made their year in July and August were sitting in their doorways with their hands in their sleeves, watching the rain.

The house on the Lucrine road had a lemon tree in the courtyard and a leaking gutter and a smell of sickness in the back room.

Sabbia opened the door. She was sixty now, and thin, and she had been with Bassa for forty years, and she looked at Antonia for a long moment and then stepped aside without a word.

'How long?' said Antonia.

'The physician says a week. He said that three weeks ago.' Sabbia took her cloak. 'She has a growth in the belly and she will not have it looked at, and she has not eaten more than a mouthful a day since the ides of September, and she has been giving away her things. She gave her good cloak to the woman next door and her bronze mirror to the girl who brings the water, and she has been asking for you since the twenty-ninth of August, and she has been asking in a way that is not like her.' Sabbia's mouth worked. 'She has been asking like a woman who has something to say.'

---

Bassa was in the bed in the back room with the window that looked east, and she was very small in it.

Her face had gone the colour of old ivory, and her hands on the coverlet were like a bird's feet, and her eyes were open blue and entirely aware.

'You did not come,' she said.

'I did not come.'

'Why?'

'Because a boy died in my house in August,' said Antonia, 'and because the house was closed, and because there was a trial coming, and because I told myself that a woman with a dead boy in her house does not go to Puteoli to stand in an empty garden.'

Bassa lay still for a moment.

'That is a good reason,' she said. 'I have heard worse. I have heard a great many worse, from a great many people, over the years, and I have believed most of them, because it is easier to believe a reason than to be angry.' Her hand moved on the coverlet. 'Sit down. Not there; there is a stool by the bed that Sabbia sits on. Sit on the stool.'

Antonia sat.

'I am dying,' said Bassa.

'I know.'

'It is not quick and it is not interesting and it is not dignified, and I have delivered two hundred children into the world and I am going out of it through my own belly like a fish on a slab, and there is no justice in it and I do not want any.' She turned her head. 'I have been thinking about you since August. Not about the girl; about you. About a girl of sixteen in a hot room with her hair stuck to her face and her hands on the bed frame, and the way you looked at me when I told you the child was dead.' She closed her eyes. 'I have told that lie to eleven women in forty years. It is the only part of the trade that I have never been able to do properly. Some of them believed me and some of them did not, and you looked at me as though I were a door that had been shut, and I have seen that look every year when you come and stand in the garden, and this year you did not come, and I thought: she has found out, and she is not coming because she cannot look at me.'

'I found out in the summer,' said Antonia.

'I know. I heard. The whole town heard; you hired a man and he came and asked questions in the basilica, and the clerk in the records office is my cousin's husband, and he came and told me that a man from Rome had been asking about the year.' Bassa opened her eyes. 'You should have come to me first.'

'You lied to me for twenty-five years.'

'Yes,' said Bassa. 'And then I told you the truth six years ago and you walked out of this house and down to the sea and you did not write to me for three years, and when you came back you stood in the garden and did not come in.' She shifted, and her face tightened with pain. 'I am not going to argue with you. I have about a week and I am going to spend it saying what I have been keeping, and you are going to listen, and then you are going to leave and I am going to die, and neither of us is going to be forgiven, because that is not what this is.'

'Then say it.'

'Not tonight,' said Bassa. 'Tonight I am going to sleep, because I have been keeping myself awake for eleven days waiting for you, and I have not got the strength to say it twice.' Her hand found Antonia's wrist on the coverlet and closed on it with surprising force. 'Tomorrow. In the morning, when the light is good. And bring the Greek woman, the one you sleep in the same room with, because you will need somebody to hear it who is not you.'

---

She said it in the morning, with the shutters open and the light coming in grey off the sea, and it took an hour and a half, and Antonia did not interrupt her once.

She said that on the twenty-ninth of August, when the child was born, there had been a woman waiting in the corridor. She had come from Rome two days before and had been in the house since, and had given her orders in a voice that made Bassa's hands shake, and she had been perhaps forty, tall, thin, with a jewelled clasp at her shoulder and a slave with her who did not speak.

'She was not a slave,' said Bassa. 'I have been forty years in this trade and I know a slave when I see one, and I know a free woman who has been given orders all her life, and she was the second. She stood in my corridor with her hands folded and she said, "The child is not to remain in this house," and I said, "The mother is sixteen and she is bleeding and she has been told nothing," and she said, "The mother is not your concern. The child is."'

'What did you do?'

'I delivered the child,' said Bassa. 'She was born at the ninth hour and she was small and she cried, and I put her in a cloth and I held her for a moment. And then the woman held out her arms, and I gave her the child, and she did not look at her. She gave her to the slave who did not speak and she said, "The dealer is expecting us before dark," and they went out of that door and down the road and I never saw the child again.'

Antonia sat very still.

'You told me it was buried in the garden.'

'I told you it was buried in the garden, and then I went out and turned over a piece of earth at the bottom of my own garden with a spade, so that there would be a place, in case you asked to see it. And you asked to see it.' Bassa's eyes were wet and her voice was perfectly steady. 'I have stood in that garden with you, year after year, while you looked at a piece of turned earth that I dug myself, and I have said nothing, and I have taken your money for the wall, and I have let you buy the ground, and I have let you believe that you had a grave.'

'Why?'

'Because I was told what would happen to me,' said Bassa, 'and because I was sixty-one and I had a house and a freedom that could be taken back by a signature, and because I had been a slave for fifty years and I knew exactly what a signature could do.' She turned her head on the pillow. 'That is the whole of the reason, and it is not enough, and I know it is not enough, and I have been saying it to myself for twenty-five years in the hope that it would become enough, and it has not.'

'What was her name?' said Antonia.

'I do not know her name.'

'Bassa.'

'I do not know her name,' said Bassa again, 'and that is the truth, and it is the one thing in this whole business I cannot give you. She was called domina by her slave and she was never called anything else in my hearing, and I did not ask, because a midwife who asks is a midwife who is paid less.' She closed her eyes. 'But I know the dealer, because I had dealt with him before, and I know what he was told to write on the bill, because I heard her say it at the door. She said: "Enter it in my brother's name. He is the master of the household and it is his household that is buying."'

The room was silent except for the sea.

'Her brother's name,' said Antonia.

'She said it,' said Bassa. 'She said, "Enter it in the name of Lucius Tarquitius Priscus," and the dealer wrote it down, and that is how I have known all these years that the child went into that family and not into a brothel or a field.' She opened her eyes. 'And there is one thing more, and then I am going to sleep. Under the loose tile by the window, in the corner, there is a hole. I put a paper in it the day after they took her, and I have never taken it out, and I have never told anyone, not Sabbia, not the priest, not the woman who comes to wash me.' Her hand moved. 'It is the copy of the bill that the dealer left with me. I asked him for a copy because I said I must have something for my own book, and he gave it to me, and it has the name on it, and the price, and the date, and the mark of his office. I have been afraid of it for twenty-five years. Take it out of my house and take it to Rome and do whatever it is you are going to do with it, and do not tell me what it says.'

Antonia got up and went to the corner by the window and knelt and lifted the tile, and the hole beneath it was lined with a piece of leather that had gone hard, and inside the leather there was a folded paper.

She did not unfold it there. She put it in her sleeve and came back and sat down on the stool.

'Bassa,' she said.

'Do not,' said Bassa. 'Whatever it is. Not now.'

'I was going to say thank you.'

'That is worse,' said Bassa, and turned her face to the wall.

---

On the second afternoon, while Bassa slept, Antonia walked down to the harbour.

She had not meant to. She had meant to sit in the courtyard and write letters, and she got as far as the end of the Lucrine road and then kept walking, and Myrrhine walked beside her and did not ask where they were going, because she had understood from the first turn.

The house of Caesonius Verus had changed its name and its trade.

It was a boarding house now, for merchants and ships' officers, with a new portico and a new doorkeeper and a sign with a ship on it. The fountain in the courtyard was the same fountain; she could see it through the gate. The vine on the trellis had thickened and covered half the wall. The room over the kitchen, where she had slept for six years, had a new shutter.

'Do you want to go in?' said Myrrhine.

'No.'

They stood in the street for a while, and the doorkeeper looked at them, and Antonia looked at the fountain.

'It was a good house, as those houses go,' she said. 'He did not beat us. He did not sell us to the sailors. He had a rule that no man came into the courtyard after the second hour, and he kept it, and when a magistrate's son broke it he was thrown into the street by a doorkeeper who was paid extra for the throwing.' She turned away. 'There is a thing that respectable people cannot hear, and it is this: that house saved me. Not in the way they mean when they say a woman is saved. It taught me to read a man in three sentences, and to keep a book, and to know what a favour costs, and it gave me six years in which nobody owned my body except a man with a bad leg who wanted the accounts balanced. If I had stayed in the villa at Arretium I would have been a laundry woman at thirty, and dead at forty, and I would never have learned that I could do arithmetic.'

'You do not have to defend it,' said Myrrhine.

'I am not defending it. I am telling you what it was.' Antonia started back up the road. 'The bishop of the Christians says that a woman in such a house is a sinner and that the man who goes to her is a sinner, and that both may be forgiven. The philosophers say that the woman is a victim and the man is a beast. The law says that the woman is a thing and the man is a citizen. None of them has ever asked me what it was, and I have been in this town for two days and I have walked past that gate and I have found that I still do not know how to answer.'

---

The woman who had lived next door to Bassa for thirty years was called Vera, and she came in on the third day with a bowl of soup and stayed for two hours, and it was she who sat with Antonia in the courtyard while Bassa slept.

Vera was sixty-five and had been the wife of a rope-maker and was now a widow with four sons and a small income and no patience at all.

'You are the one who bought the ground,' she said.

'I am.'

'Bassa told me you come every year and stand in it.' Vera stirred her soup. 'I asked her once why you did it, and she said, "Because she thinks her child is there," and I said, "Is it?" and she said, "No," and then she did not say anything else for a year.'

Antonia looked at her.

'You knew.'

'I knew there was no child in that ground because I was in the house the morning she dug it,' said Vera, 'and she dug it alone with a spade at the fifth hour, and she was crying, and she was sixty-one years old and she had a bad hip and she would not let me help her, and she would not tell me why.' She put the bowl down. 'I am not going to be sorry for her in front of you. She was my neighbour and I liked her and she did a wicked thing and kept it for twenty-five years, and both of those are true, and I have been to the priest about it and he told me that the gods see everything and that I should mind my own business.' She looked at Antonia sideways. 'The girl. Did she live?'

'She lived.'

'Where is she?'

'I do not know yet. In Rome.'

Vera nodded slowly.

'Then find her,' she said, 'and when you find her, do not tell her that her mother is a rich woman with a house full of beautiful women and a trial for murder in November. Tell her that her mother is a woman who has been looking for her for twenty-five years and has made a great many mistakes and is not going to make this one.' She picked up her bowl. 'And when you have told her, bring her here, and I will tell her what her grandmother Bassa was like, because there is nobody else left who can, and a girl should have one old woman in her life who remembers the beginning.'

---

She died on the fourth day, in the afternoon, with Sabbia on one side of the bed and Antonia on the other, and at the end she said something that Antonia did not catch and Sabbia did, and Sabbia wept and would not repeat it.

The funeral was the next morning. She had been a freedwoman for forty years, and she had been a midwife for fifty, and the town came: the women she had delivered and the children she had delivered and some of the children's children, the physician, two of the town magistrates, the priestess of Isis in her white linen, the woman from the house next door who had been given the good cloak, the girl who brought the water and had been given the bronze mirror. There were eleven wreaths and a flute player and a man who was paid to make the lament and did it very well, and there was a procession down the Lucrine road to the burial ground outside the town wall.

Antonia walked in it. She was the richest woman in the procession, and she walked behind the bier with Sabbia and did not speak to anyone, and the town looked at her and made its own arrangements about who she was.

Afterwards she stood at the edge of the ground with the sea behind her.

'What will you do with the house?' said Myrrhine.

'Give it to Sabbia. And the ground at the bottom of the garden.' She looked at the turned earth, which had grown a lemon sapling in the twenty years since it had been dug, and which was, as it had always been, empty. 'Have a stone put there. Not a grave. A stone with no name, and a line from the poet, the one about the sea washing away the evils of men, and the date. She can decide what it means, or the woman next door can, or nobody can, and it will be there in a hundred years and no one will know why.'

'That is a strange thing to build,' said Myrrhine.

'It is what I have,' said Antonia. 'I have spent twenty years paying a wall around a lie. A wall and a stone with no name is at least honest.'

---

She read the paper in the carriage on the way back to Rome, at dusk, on the road between Formiae and Fundi, with the sea on her right and the hills going dark.

It was a bill of sale, four inches by six, in a dealer's hand, with the office mark at the foot. It said that in the ninth month of the year of the consulship of Domitian and Corbulo, one Caius Oppius Felix had sold to the household of Lucius Tarquitius Priscus a female child, age four days, of good health and no defect, for the sum of two hundred and forty sesterces; that the child was of slave condition, born of a slave woman of the household of the Tarquitii at Arretium; and that she had been given the name Tertia in the household accounts, being the third such purchase of that year.

At the bottom, in a different hand, in ink that had gone brown, there was one line more.

*Received, and the child delivered to the household at Arretium in the care of the slave Sabbia, on the sixth day before the Kalends of October.*

Antonia read it four times.

'Well?' said Myrrhine.

'Her name is Tertia,' said Antonia. 'She is twenty-five years old. She was bought for two hundred and forty sesterces and given to a woman named Sabbia and taken to a house in the country where I spent the worst year of my life, and she was entered in a book as the third purchase of the year.' She folded the paper. 'And she was freed seven years ago, by the council's leave, on the instruction of Tarquitia Priscilla, and sent to Rome with a trade.'

Myrrhine sat forward.

'Then she is in Rome.'

'She is in Rome,' said Antonia. 'There are perhaps four hundred thousand people in this city. I have a name that is also a number, a date, a household, and a woman who freed her and knows where she went.' She looked out of the window at the dark hills. 'In the morning I am going to send for Hilarus, and I am going to have him find her, and then I am going to have to decide what to do with the rest of my life.'

'And the trial?'

'The trial is in nine days,' said Antonia. 'And when I stand up in that basilica I will be a woman who has been told by a dying midwife that her daughter is alive in this city, and I will not be able to say so, because the court will hear it as a fiction invented for sympathy, and because the one man who could confirm it is the man who is going to defend me.' She leaned her head against the side of the carriage. 'So I will say nothing, and I will be tried for a boy who died of his own foolishness, and I will keep my face still, and when it is over I will go and find my daughter.'

'And if she does not want to be found?'

Antonia closed her eyes.

'Then I will have a name,' she said, 'and I will know that she is alive, and I will be a woman in a house on the Esquiline with an open door for the rest of my life, in case she ever wants to come through it. It is not much. It is more than I had in August.'

---

Before they left Puteoli, on the last morning, Sabbia came to her in the courtyard with a bundle in her hands.

It was the child's cloth. The one she had been wrapped in.

'I took it,' said Sabbia. 'That morning, when the woman took her. I took the cloth and I put it in my chest and I have had it for twenty-five years, and I have washed it twice and I have not used it for anything, and I have been afraid that somebody would find it and afraid that nobody would.' She held it out. 'It is nothing. It is a piece of linen that a slave woman in this house wove and a midwife cut up for baby clothes, and there is a stain on it that will not come out, and it is not worth a sesterce.' Her hands were shaking. 'But I held her in it, domina. For an hour, before the woman came. She had the cord around her neck when she came out and Bassa cut it and she started to cry, and I held her while Bassa was seeing to you, and she stopped crying when I talked to her, and she held my finger with her whole hand, which they do; the books say they cannot, but they do.'

Antonia took the cloth.

It was thin and worn and the colour of old paper, and there was a brown stain in one corner, and along the hem someone had sewn a line of small cross-stitches in dark red thread, which had been a good thread once, before forty years of washing.

'What was she like?' said Antonia.

'Small,' said Sabbia. 'Loud. She had a great deal of hair for a baby, and it was dark, and it was not the hair of a four-day child, and Bassa said that meant nothing, and it meant nothing.' She wiped her face with the back of her hand. 'And she had a mark. Here.' She touched her own left shoulder blade. 'On the back of the shoulder. Not a birthmark like a wine stain. It was like a small crescent, the size of a fingernail, and it was a little darker than the rest of her, and it will have faded, or it will not. You should know. Somebody should know.'

Antonia folded the cloth and put it in her sleeve with the bill of sale.

'Thank you,' she said.

'Do not thank me,' said Sabbia, and began to cry in earnest, and said, through it, the thing that Antonia had been waiting four days to hear and had not asked for: 'I should have run out of that door with her. I was thirty-nine years old and I could have run out of that door and down the Lucrine road and they would never have caught me, and I did not, because I was afraid, and I have thought about that every day for twenty-five years and there is nothing in the world I can do about it now except tell you that I have thought about it.'

Antonia stood in the courtyard of a house on the Lucrine road with a piece of linen in her sleeve.

'You held her,' she said. 'For an hour, you held her, and she stopped crying, and that is not nothing. It is the only thing anyone in that house did for her that had no price on it, and I am going to tell her so, if I ever get the chance.'

---

They left at the fourth hour and were in Rome by the evening of the next day, and the city was in its October mood, which is the best month it has: the heat gone, the light gold, the markets full, the rich coming back from the coast with their servants and their seasickness and their stories.

At the gate, Meno said, 'There are three things, domina. The praetor's brother-in-law has been to the urban prefect. There is a letter from the house of Vibia Lucana. And a man has been here twice asking for the atriensis and would not give his name, and Cinnamus has put him off, and he says the man is a clerk from the Palatine.'

Antonia stood in her own atrium for a moment with the smell of the house around her: wax and wine and the dust of a hundred sandals and, underneath it, the fig tree.

'Bring me wine,' she said, 'and then bring me Cinnamus, and then send to Hilarus, and tell him I want a woman found in this city, and I want her found in ten days.'

'In ten days, domina? The trial is in eleven.'

'I know,' said Antonia. 'I would like to know who my daughter is before I am tried for my life, and I would like it very much if those two things could happen in that order.'
