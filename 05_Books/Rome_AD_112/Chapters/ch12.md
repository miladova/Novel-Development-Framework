# 12 | The Ledger of Favours

Vibia Lucana's house on the Caelian had eleven rooms and a garden in which nothing grew but roses, and in the first week of September she asked Antonia to come and eat with her alone.

That was unusual. Vibia never ate alone with anyone; she said it made her feel like a woman in a play. But she asked, and Antonia went, and they ate in the garden under a vine with a lamp on the table and a jug between them, and Vibia talked for an hour about a wedding she had been to, and a senator's wife who had worn a dress that was eleven years old and had pretended it was new, and a rumour about the prefect's daughter, and Antonia listened and laughed in the right places and waited.

When the plates had gone and the slaves had withdrawn, Vibia said, 'I want to buy a house.'

'You have a house.'

'I want to buy a house in my own name, with my own money, that my husband's family cannot touch, and I want to put it in writing so that no one in Rome can take it from me, and I want you to tell me how.' Vibia poured the last of the wine. 'Also I want to sell the factory at Puteoli, and I want to keep the proceeds in a place where my late husband's brother cannot see them, and I want to stop paying his allowances. He has had eleven thousand a year from me for six years and he has spent it on horses and a boy from Capua, and I am tired of it.'

Antonia put down her cup.

'You cannot do any of that,' she said.

'I know. That is why I am asking you.'

'No, listen to me. You cannot do any of it, and it is not because the law forbids it. It is because your husband's will left you a usufruct and not a title, and because your dower went into the estate and the estate is administered by your husband's brother as trustee for your son, and because your son is nineteen and has been told since he was twelve that his mother is an eccentric who must be humoured.' Antonia counted on her fingers. 'The house you want to buy will be bought in the name of a man, because a woman cannot hold property in her own name under the law as it stands, and the man will be a friend, a freedman, or a trustee, and in ten years or in ten days he will discover that he holds the title and you do not. The factory at Puteoli is in the estate's name. The allowances are in your husband's will. And your brother-in-law is not going to give up eleven thousand a year because you have discovered that you dislike him.'

Vibia was silent for a moment.

'Then what am I to do?' she said.

'You are to do what every woman in this city does who has money and no power over it. You are to make yourself so useful to the man who holds the title that he cannot afford to cheat you, and so dangerous to the man who administers the estate that he does not dare.' Antonia leaned back. 'Your brother-in-law has a daughter who is to be married in the spring. He has no money for the dowry because he has spent it on horses. You will offer to pay the dowry — out of the factory's profits, which are in the estate, which he administers, which he has been using for six years to pay himself an allowance that the will does not authorize. You will offer it in front of witnesses, generously, in the temple of the family gods, with a notary present. He will accept, because he cannot refuse in front of witnesses and because he needs the money. And in the act of accepting he will have admitted that the profits of the factory are yours to give, and from that day forward a competent jurist can argue that his administration of the estate is a fiction.'

Vibia stared at her.

'That would take a year,' she said.

'It will take two.'

'And in the meantime?'

'In the meantime you will buy your house in the name of my atriensis,' said Antonia, 'who is a freedman of mine, and who has a wife and no debts and a horror of lawyers, and who will sign a deed of trust that I will draw up myself and that my own advocate will witness, and if I ever cheat you, Vibia, you will be able to take the deed to the urban prefect and ruin me and every woman in my house.' She picked up her cup. 'I am not offering to do it because I am noble. I am offering because you stood in a crowd in the Forum of Trajan and told me the truth when it would have been easier to tell me a joke, and because in this city a woman who tells the truth in a crowd is worth more than a house.'

Vibia looked at her across the table for a long moment.

'You are a strange woman,' she said. 'I have known you eleven years and I have never once been able to decide whether you are the most honest person in Rome or the most dangerous.'

'Both,' said Antonia. 'It is the same thing in this city. The honest ones are dangerous because everyone knows what they will do.'

'And the dangerous ones?'

'The dangerous ones are honest about one thing,' said Antonia, 'and it is usually not the thing you think.'

The counterstrike came three days later, and it came in the form of a tablet with a copy of a copy in the hand of a clerk.

Hilarus brought it himself, in the morning, to the house, which he had never done. He came in through the garden door and stood in the study with the tablet in his hands.

'The woman Doris,' he said. 'The one who was sold in June to the baker and bought back by Sestius in July. She is not going to testify.'

'Why not?'

'Because she is dead,' said Hilarus. 'She died on the fourth day of September, in the household of the praetor, of a fever, according to the physician who signed the certificate, and the physician is the same man who has been engaged to swear that your house poisoned the boy. I have it from a slave in the praetor's household who is frightened and who wants money, and I have paid him, and he says that the woman was not ill on the second of September and that she was buried on the fifth, in the ground outside the Colline gate, without a stone.' He put the tablet down. 'I cannot prove it. There is no body to look at and no one will ever be charged. But it changes the case, and it changes what I am going to say to you now, which is that you should settle.'

'I will not settle.'

'Domina—'

'Three charges,' said Antonia. 'The poisoning of Cirrus, which is a lie and which I can disprove with a box and a physician and a slave. The keeping of a disorderly house, which is true and which is not a crime that anyone has been convicted of in eleven years except in the provinces. And the corruption of the young, which is not a crime at all and which is the only one that will be read aloud in the Senate.' She turned the tablet around. 'They will not convict me of the first. They will convict me of the third in the court of public opinion, and that is the whole of the case, and I am not going to settle, because a woman who settles has admitted that there was something to settle.'

Hilarus looked at her.

'Then you will need a weapon that is not evidence,' he said.

'Yes,' said Antonia. 'That is why you are here.'

---

The fire had been in the third year of the emperor's reign, in the ninth region, in a block of tenements off the Via Lata, and nineteen people had died in it, and the tablet in Antonia's strongbox was the sworn statement of a contractor of the vigiles named Gaius Albinus.

He had been drunk when he made it, and terrified, and he had made it in her presence at a table in a back room of a cookshop with the door locked, and he had wept twice, and at the end he had asked her what she was going to do with it, and she had said that she did not know, and he had said, 'If you use it, I am a dead man,' and she had said, 'Then I will not use it unless I must.'

He had died the following year, of an abscess in the jaw.

What the tablet said was this: that on the night of the fire the watch had been directed to the warehouse next to the tenements before the tenements themselves, and that the direction had come from a man in a good cloak who had spoken to the curator of the watch and had shown him a token, and that the man had been paid by the brother of the aedile, and that the aedile that year had been Marcus Sestius Verus, whose brother owned the warehouse, and who had bought the ground the tenements had stood on eleven days after the fire at a tenth of its value.

Antonia had never used it. She had thought about it every year, in the way that one thinks about a knife in a drawer.

She did not use it now, either — not directly, and not in writing. Instead she sent for Cinnamus and gave him four instructions, and she gave them in the flat voice she used for the accounts, and he wrote them all down and did not comment on any of them.

The first was to find out, quietly, whether Marcus Sestius had a brother-in-law named Publius Calpurnius who had lent him money four years ago and had never been repaid.

The second was to find out whether that same Publius Calpurnius had a daughter of marriageable age.

The third was to have a copy made of the tablet of Gaius Albinus — not the whole of it, and not in a hand that could be traced, but the first eleven lines, which named the hour, the street, the direction of the watch, and the sum paid — and to leave that copy, in an envelope of thin leather, with the doorkeeper of the house of Publius Calpurnius, with no letter, on the morning of the twelfth day of September.

The fourth was to have a message carried to the praetor's household on the same morning, by a boy from the street who could not be traced back to the Esquiline, saying only this: *The woman on the hill has a copy of the tablet. She has had it for nine years. Ask your brother-in-law what he intends to do about it.*

'Domina,' said Cinnamus, when he had written it all down, 'may I ask what this is intended to achieve?'

'It is intended to start a family quarrel in a house that has been united against me for eleven years,' said Antonia. 'Calpurnius has been owed money by Sestius for four years and cannot press for it because his daughter is to be married and he needs Sestius's name at the wedding. When he learns that Sestius's name is going to be in a basilica in November attached to a fire that killed nineteen people, he will do one of two things. He will either come to me to buy the tablet, or he will go to Sestius and demand his money before the case opens.' She set down her stylus. 'If he comes to me, I will give him the tablet for nothing and tell him to take it to the urban prefect, and Sestius will spend the winter defending himself instead of prosecuting me. If he goes to Sestius, then Sestius will have to choose between paying four hundred thousand sesterces he does not have and explaining to his brother-in-law why the woman on the hill has a document about a fire.'

'And if he does neither, and simply waits?'

'Then nothing happens,' said Antonia, 'and I have spent nothing, and lost nothing, and I will go into that court with the truth and an advocate and be acquitted or not, and I will have learned something about the man, which is always worth the price of a leather envelope.' She looked up. 'You disapprove.'

'I do not disapprove, domina. I am counting.'

'Counting what?'

'The number of men in this city who will have heard, by the Saturnalia, that you keep documents about fires in a strongbox,' said Cinnamus. 'Eleven, by my reckoning. And of those eleven, four will be men who have something to hide and who have never done you any harm, and they will now have a reason to want you ruined, and they will be quiet about it, and they will be in the basilica in November, in the second row, listening.'

Antonia was silent for a moment.

'Yes,' she said. 'That is the price.'

'It is a high price.'

'It is a cheaper price than being convicted,' said Antonia, 'and I have been in this city long enough to know that the only real question about a price is whether you can pay it in the month it falls due.'

---

Publius Calpurnius came to the house on the fifteenth day of September, at the ninth hour, in a litter with the curtains drawn.

He was a thin, careful man of fifty with the look of a man who has spent his life in the second rank of a family that has always been in the first, and he came into the atrium and stood with his hands behind his back and said, 'I have received a document.'

'I know,' said Antonia. 'I sent it.'

He blinked. Whatever he had prepared, it had not been that.

'You sent it,' he said.

'I did. Sit down. There is wine, and it is good, and you have had a difficult week.'

He did not sit down. He took the envelope out of his sleeve and held it.

'This names my brother-in-law,' he said. 'It says that the watch was directed away from a building in which nineteen people died, on the instruction of a man paid by my brother-in-law, and it is signed by a contractor of the vigiles who is dead.' He looked up. 'Is it genuine?'

'Yes.'

'And you have had it for nine years.'

'Nine years.'

'And you have produced it now,' said Calpurnius, 'three weeks before the trial of a charge that my brother-in-law has spent the summer preparing, in an envelope with no letter, delivered by a boy who cannot be found.' He put the envelope on the table. 'What do you want?'

'Nothing.'

'Everybody wants something.'

'I want your brother-in-law to spend the winter explaining a fire,' said Antonia, 'and I want my house open in the spring, and I want the woman Doris, who died in his household on the fourth day of September, to be remembered by somebody besides me. That is all. I am not asking you for money and I am not asking you to testify. I am giving you a document because you are the only man in Rome who has both a reason to use it and the standing to be believed.'

Calpurnius stood very still. Then he sat down.

'I have lent him four hundred thousand sesterces over six years,' he said. 'He has never repaid a sesterce. My daughter is to be married in the spring and I have been selling land to pay for it, and last month I asked him for the money, and he told me that a man who presses his friends is a man who has no friends.' He picked up the cup and drank from it. 'I am not a brave man. I have been in the Senate for twenty-two years and I have never made a speech that anyone remembers. I have four children and a modest estate and a wife who is cleverer than I am, and my whole life has been arranged around not being noticed.'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'And now you have put this in my hands,' said Calpurnius, 'and if I use it, I will destroy my wife's brother and my wife will never forgive me, and my daughter's wedding will be a small quiet affair in the country, and I will be a man who did a thing.' He turned the cup around. 'And if I do not use it, I will have it in my strongbox for the rest of my life, and I will take it out every year and look at it, and I will be a man who did not.'

'Yes,' said Antonia.

'Why did you send it to me and not to the prefect?'

'Because the prefect would have buried it,' said Antonia, 'and because a man who has been cheated for six years by a man he is obliged to admire is the most dangerous creature in Rome. He is not angry. He is certain.'

Calpurnius looked at her for a long moment. Then he put the envelope back in his sleeve and stood.

'My wife's brother,' he said, 'is going to be told tomorrow morning that I have the document, and that I intend to take it to the urban prefect on the first of October unless he repays me in full and withdraws his name from the charge against your house.' He straightened his tunic. 'He will refuse. He will refuse because he cannot pay and because a man like him would rather be ruined than be seen to give way. And then I will take it to the prefect, and I will be a man who did a thing.' He went to the door. 'Domina. If I do this, and it goes badly, I will lose everything I have, and I will remember that it was a woman on the Esquiline who put the envelope in my hand.'

'You will remember it either way,' said Antonia. 'That is what documents do.'

---

The other thing that happened in September happened at Tibur, and Antonia did not learn of it for nine days.

Two men came to the house of the physician's sister on the eighth day of the month, in the afternoon, and asked for the woman Berenice, and were told that there was no such woman in the house, and went away. They came back on the tenth with a magistrate's man and a written authority, and searched the house, and found nothing, because Berenice had been moved on the seventh to a farm belonging to Naso's cousin, six miles further up the valley.

On the eleventh, the physician's sister sent a message to Rome by a boy on a fast horse, and the message reached the house at the fourth hour of the morning, and Antonia read it in the study by lamplight, and then sat for a long time with her hands in her lap.

'They found her,' said Myrrhine.

'They found the house. They did not find her.' Antonia folded the note. 'They will find her. There are six farms in that valley and they will visit all of them, and a farm is not a city; there is nowhere to hide a woman in a farm.'

'Then bring her back.'

'And put her in this house, where every man in Rome knows she is, three weeks before the trial?' Antonia shook her head. 'No. She stays where she is, and we send word to the farm that if men come asking, she is the wife of the cousin's steward, and she has a name and a marriage and a child, and there is a document to prove it, and the document will be in her hand before the men get there.' She stood up. 'And Myrrhine — send to Hilarus. Tell him I want to know what happened to the woman Doris, and I want to know it in a form that a court will hear, and I want it before the first of October.'

'That will cost.'

'Everything costs,' said Antonia. 'I have spent the summer learning the price of things. It turns out that I had it wrong for more than a decade: I thought I was buying safety. I was buying time, and time runs out, and then you pay again.'

---

The crack in her own household came four days later, and it came from Prima.

Antonia had known for years that Prima kept money of her own. It was not a secret; half the women kept money, in a box, under a tile, in a hole behind a loose brick in the bath, and Antonia had never asked about any of it, because a woman with nothing of her own is a woman with no reason to stay.

What she had not known was that Prima had been taking it from one man for nine years.

His name was Quintus Valerius Asiaticus, he was a merchant of the wine trade with an office on the Tiber and a wife in the country, and he had been coming to the house since the fourth year. He was fifty, fat, good-tempered, and generous, and he had given Prima, over nine years, a sum that came to something over two hundred thousand sesterces, which he had entered in his own books as the purchase of a share in a ship.

'He did not buy me,' said Prima. 'He gave me money because he wanted to. There is a difference, and I have had nine years to think about what it is, and it comes down to this: he could stop at any time and I would still be in this house.'

Antonia stood in the doorway of Prima's room.

'Why are you telling me this now?' she said.

'Because his wife's brother came to see me,' said Prima. 'On the sixth day. He sat in the garden with me for an hour and he said that his sister had found the entry in the book, and that there would be a divorce, and that in a divorce the wife's family will look for everyone who has taken money out of the estate, and that I am a woman with two hundred thousand sesterces and no name to protect me.' She was folding a dress, and she did not stop folding it. 'He offered me a choice. I could give evidence in November that this house is a place where men are drugged and robbed, and he would see that I was left alone. Or I could refuse, and he would bring an action against me for the money in the spring, and I would lose it, and I would be in the street at thirty-one with nothing.'

Antonia came into the room and sat down on the chest.

'And what did you say?'

'I said I would think about it.' Prima put the dress down. 'And then I came home and I have been thinking about it for four days, and I have decided, and I am telling you before you hear it from someone else, because whatever else I am, I am not a woman who takes a bribe behind a locked door.'

'What have you decided?'

Prima looked at her.

'I have decided to keep the money,' she said. 'I am going to give the money to Cinnamus to hold, in the house's name, in a trust, with a deed, and I am going to tell the wife's brother that if he brings an action I will produce the letters his brother-in-law wrote to me and read them aloud in the basilica, and that his sister will hear them, and that he will be a man who ruined his own sister's marriage to save two hundred thousand sesterces.' She folded her arms. 'I am not going to testify for him, and I am not going to testify for you. I am going to be a woman who was in the house and who says that she saw the boy take the box himself, because I did, and that is all I am going to say, and if that is not enough for you, then I am sorry, and I will go.'

Antonia sat on the chest in the small room and looked at her.

'You were with me that night,' she said. 'On the floor. You sat with him for four hours because you did not want him to be alone in a strange house, and you told me about the man in the Argiletum, and I have never told anyone that and I never will. And now you are telling me that you will not stand up and say what happened.'

'I am telling you that I will stand up and say exactly what happened,' said Prima. 'What I will not do is stand up and say everything else. I will not be the woman who says that the house pays wages and the wine is free and the girls are free to leave, and I will not be the woman who says that Antonia Cassia is a good woman. Because you are not a good woman. You are a woman who has done good things and terrible things in the same afternoon for eleven years, and I have watched you do both, and I am thirty-one years old and I have two hundred thousand sesterces and I want to be alive at forty.'

There was a long silence.

'That is honest,' said Antonia at last.

'I have been in this house seven years,' said Prima. 'You have never once lied to me. I am not going to begin.'

Antonia stood up.

'Give the money to Cinnamus in the morning,' she said. 'Have the deed drawn in your own name and a copy kept in the temple of Castor. And Prima — when the case is over, whether I am acquitted or exiled, I want you to take that money and buy something that cannot be taken from you. A house, a shop, a field. Not a ship, not a share, not a loan to a man with a good name. Something with a door and a lock, and a deed in your own name, and a woman in it who is not obliged to be pleasant to anyone.'

Prima looked at her.

'Why?' she said.

'Because I have spent eleven years telling the women in this house that they are free,' said Antonia, 'and I have just discovered that I have been paying them enough to be free and not enough to be safe, and that is the difference between me and the men I have spent my life despising.'

---

Myrrhine heard all of it, of course. Myrrhine heard everything, and she came to the study that night with the accounts and a face like a wall.

'Prima is right,' she said. 'Cinnamus is right. Vibia is right. Half the house will leave before the winter and the other half will stay because they have nowhere to go, and every one of them will be asked, in a basilica, in front of the whole city, what she does for a living and how much she is paid for it.' She put the tablets down. 'And you have spent the whole of September arranging other people's lives and you have not once sat down and asked yourself what you will do if you lose.'

'I will go into exile,' said Antonia.

'Where?'

'Massilia. There is a woman there who runs a shipping office; I lent her money in the sixth year and she has never defaulted. She will give me a room and a desk.' Antonia turned her cup. 'I have thought about it. I think about it every night at the second hour. I have a plan, and a route, and a sum of money that is not in the house and not in Naso's bank, and I have had it for nine years, and I have never told anyone, and now I have told you.'

Myrrhine sat down.

'Why now?'

'Because you are the only person in the world who has never asked me for anything,' said Antonia, 'and because I am going to ask you to come with me, and I want you to know what you are agreeing to before you say yes.'

Myrrhine looked at her for a long moment.

'I have been waiting sixteen years for you to ask me something,' she said. 'Sixteen years. I have watched you give money to women who left and letters to girls who went to Narnia and a contract to Eucharis and a trust to Prima, and I have sat in this house and kept your accounts and slept in a room down the corridor from yours and never once asked for anything, because I decided when I was twenty-five that if I had to ask, I did not want it.' She stood up. 'And now you have asked me to go into exile with you, and I am going to say yes, and then I am going to go to bed, and tomorrow I will be the woman who keeps the accounts again, because that is what this house requires.'

'Myrrhine—'

'No,' said Myrrhine. 'You have had a very difficult summer and you are about to be tried for murder in a basilica, and I am not going to have the conversation that we should have had sixteen years ago in the same month. When it is over. When you are acquitted, or when we are in Massilia, and there is nothing left to be afraid of. Then you will ask me properly, and I will not say yes immediately, because I want to be asked twice.' She picked up the tablets. 'And Antonia. Whatever happens in November — do not make the bargain with him.'

'What bargain?'

'The one you are going to make,' said Myrrhine. 'The one where he saves you and you close the house and you are grateful to him for the rest of your life. I have watched you refuse that bargain twice this summer and I have been proud of you, and I know that in November, when the court is full and the witnesses are finished and the verdict is being counted, you will be offered it a third time in a voice you cannot refuse, and you will take it, because you will be tired, and because it will look like peace.' She stopped in the doorway. 'It will not be peace. It will be the same silence he has been living in for twenty-five years, and you will be living in it with him, and the house will be closed and the women will be gone and you will be a woman of forty-two with a great fortune and a name that is spoken carefully in rooms you are not in.'
