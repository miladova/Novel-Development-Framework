# Epilogue | The Garden

Rome, six years later.

In the summer of the year when the emperor Hadrian came back from the east with the news that the provinces beyond the Euphrates were to be given up, the north slope of the Esquiline was a garden.

It had taken four years and a great deal of argument. The aqueduct had been abandoned in the autumn after the fire and the site had sat empty for a year with a rope around it, and then the treasury had sold the land to the city for a sum that was entered in the accounts as a nominal price because nobody could think what else to do with it, and the city had built a wall and laid a path and put in eleven fig trees and a fountain at the corner which did not work properly and was never repaired.

In the middle of the garden, on a low stone base, there was a bronze tablet.

It said that the site had been cleared after a fire in the consulship of two men whose names nobody remembered, that eleven houses and four workshops had been destroyed and forty-one people had died, and that the ground was given to the people of Rome for a garden in the fourth year after the fire, in the consulship of Lucius Tarquitius Priscus and Publius Calpurnius, by the favour of the emperor Caesar Nerva Traianus Augustus and of the senate and people.

There was no other inscription. There was no mention of a woman, or of a house, or of anything that had happened on that slope before the fire.

The children played in it.

---

Antonia was forty-eight years old and she had a house on the Aventine with four rooms and a courtyard and a fig tree, and she was, by the reckoning of the prefect's office, a person of some consequence in the city.

She had spent six years travelling to eleven towns in Italy, twice, with a clerk and a strongbox and a letter from the Augusta, and she had looked at the accounts of the imperial fund for the maintenance of children, and she had found that in four of the towns the money was being stolen by the clerk of the local council, and in one town by two men who had been appointed by the provincial governor, and in one town by nobody at all, which had astonished her and which she had reported in writing with a certain amount of pleasure.

Four men had been removed. One man had been prosecuted and had gone into exile at Massilia, and had written to her from Massilia, of all things, to complain about the climate. The fund had lost eleven per cent in the first two years and four per cent in the third, and in the fourth year it had lost nothing at all, and the empress had sent her a letter of four lines in her own hand, and Antonia had kept the letter in the chest with the other things.

She was not respectable. That had not changed and she had stopped expecting it to. She was not invited to the dinners of the senatorial families, and when she went to the baths the women of the old houses were civil to her in a way that was worse than rudeness, and the aedile in the fifth year had asked her opinion on the price of bread and had then, in the Senate, forgotten to mention that he had asked.

But four men had been removed from their posts, and eleven towns had kept their money, and there were four hundred children in Italy who were being fed at the state's expense and whose names were in a book that Antonia Cassia had caused to be kept honestly.

She had decided, in the second year, that this was what she had instead of a name.

---

Metrodora's school was in a building in the sandalmakers' street with a yard and two rooms and a door painted green.

She had taken the four hundred thousand sesterces in the December after the fire, in the courtyard on the Aventine, with a deed that Antonia's own advocate had drawn and that said, in the fourth clause, that the lender had no claim upon the borrower, the school, its property or its income, and that the sum was to be repaid at no interest when and if the borrower chose, and that if she never chose, it was a gift.

Metrodora had read the deed four times. Then she had said, 'There is no clause about you coming to the school.'

'There is not.'

'Then I will put one in,' said Metrodora, 'in my own hand, under the fourth clause, and it will say that the lender may come on the seventh day of any month and shall not be asked to teach anything.'

In the sixth year there were eleven girls in the school in the mornings — the daughters of shopkeepers, two slaves whose owner had agreed to it because a girl who could add was worth more, and three freed girls from the fourth region — and four in the afternoons who were older and who came to learn to write a contract.

Metrodora was thirty-one. She had married in the third year, a surveyor named Gaius who was quiet and who thought that she was the most interesting person he had ever met, and who had asked Antonia, at the wedding, whether it was true that she had once put four hundred people into a chain at a fire, and had been extremely disappointed to be told that it had only been about a hundred and fifty.

She called Antonia nothing at all. She had never found the word. In six years she had called her *domina* perhaps eleven times, and each time it had been in a moment of stress or hurry, and once, in the fourth year, when Antonia was ill for a fortnight and would not admit it, she had called her *mater* in the doorway of the sickroom and then had gone very red and had said, 'There is no bread in the house,' and had gone out to buy some.

That was the whole of it. It was enough.

---

Marcellina had married in the fifth year, and she had married a man of no family at all.

He was a physician of thirty-four named Hermias, who had been trained at Alexandria and who had a practice among the equestrians and no money and a great many opinions, and who had met her at Antonia's table on a seventh day and had argued with her about the treatment of a burn for an hour and a half, and who had afterwards walked her home and told her that she was entirely wrong and that he would prove it.

She had said, at the wedding, that she had spent nineteen years in a house where nobody argued, and that she had discovered at twenty that she could not be happy in a room where everybody agreed with her.

They lived in a house on the Caelian with a courtyard and a great many children — their own two, and Verana, who was twenty-one and had married a potter and lived four streets away and came on Sundays, and Brennus, who was eighteen and was apprenticed to a shipping agent at Ostia and who came home when he needed money.

Verana and Brennus had never been formally adopted. There had been a great deal of legal discussion about it in the first year, and in the end Tarquitius had written a letter from Asia explaining that the adoption of free children required the consent of the council and a good deal of paper and that, in his opinion as a jurist, the whole of it was unnecessary, because Marcellina had already done the only thing that mattered, which was to go to Lugdunum and fetch them.

Tarquitia Priscilla had died in the spring of the fifth year, at eighty-four, in her own bed, and had left Marcellina the house on the Palatine and the masks and the whole of the estate except a sum of eleven thousand sesterces, which she had left to a freedwoman named Metrodora, formerly of the household at Arretium, with a note that said only: *for the school*.

She had never met her granddaughter. She had never asked to. Metrodora had taken the money and had put it into the lease of the green door, and she had not gone to the funeral, and she had not said anything about it at all, and Antonia had not asked her to.

Tarquitia Aurelia had died at Tibur in the summer of the fourth year, alone, of the disease of the joints and a fever. There had been eleven people at the funeral, of whom nine were servants. Nobody from the family had gone.

---

The others were scattered and were not lost.

Meno had gone to Tibur in the May after the fire and had kept a garden gate for five years and had written eleven letters, of which four had been about the widow with the bees and seven had been about the roof. He had died in the winter of the fifth year, in his own bed, at seventy-three, and Antonia had paid for the stone and had gone out to Tibur for the funeral, and the widow with the bees had come, and had cried, and had said that he had been a difficult man and that she had been very fond of him.

Cinnamus had the shop in the Subura and had made a success of it, and had taken on two clerks and a boy, and had come to the seventh-day dinners for six years and had never once failed to bring the accounts. He had taught Marcellina to keep a book properly and had told her, on the day she married, that she was now competent and that she should never trust a man who told her otherwise.

Prima had the house on the Aventine with the two rooms and the yard, four streets from Antonia's, and had let the second room to a family of dyers and had bought a share in a fuller's business with the money, and by the sixth year she had four women working for her and a lease in her own name and a reputation in that quarter for being extremely hard and entirely fair. She came to dinner on the seventh day and she and Myrrhine argued about the price of wool. She never spoke about the Argiletum and nobody asked her to.

Eucharis had taught music in Neapolis for six years and had written nine letters, of which nine had been complaining about the family and two had been about a man. In the fifth year she had married him, and he was a ship's captain of forty with a house at Puteoli and a bad temper and a good laugh, and she had brought him to Rome in the sixth year and had stood in the courtyard on the Aventine and said, 'This is my husband, and he knows everything, and if anybody is polite to me about it I will leave.'

Vibia Lucana had her courtyard at Ostia and her shipping office and her roses, and she had kept the promise she made in the portico of the Basilica Julia on the first day of the trial: once a month, on the eleventh day, there was a dinner in the courtyard at Ostia for the women who had nowhere to go, and there were eleven of them in the first year and thirty in the sixth, and Antonia went when she was in Italy and sent money when she was not.

The baker's family had rebuilt their shop on the north slope in the second year and had a good trade from the garden, and the baker's wife had named her fourth daughter Antonia, which Antonia had not known about until the child was two years old and had been brought to the house on the Aventine to be looked at.

---

In the autumn of the sixth year a letter came from the east.

It came in a packet of eleven, with the seal of a legion and a name on the outside in a hand she knew, and it had been six weeks on the road, and it was from Antioch.

She opened it in the courtyard on the Aventine, in the afternoon, with Myrrhine sitting opposite her, and she read it twice before she said anything.

'He is dead,' she said.

Myrrhine put down her cup.

'In the summer,' said Antonia. 'The eighth day before the Kalends of August. A fever, in Antioch, four days. He had been with the emperor's staff since the spring; he was fifty-nine years old and he had gone east because the emperor asked for jurists and because he had spent two years in Asia and knew the province.' She turned the letter over. 'It is from his secretary. The secretary is sixty-one and has a club foot and has been with him for thirty years, and he wrote this himself in the inn at Antioch while the body was being prepared, and he says that he died in the afternoon and that he was in a great deal of discomfort for the last day and that he was clear in his mind until the end.'

'Antonia—'

'He says that there are four boxes of papers and a strongbox and that they will come by the military post in the spring, and that the strongbox is not to be opened except by the person named in his will, and that the person named in his will is me.' She folded the letter. 'He has left me something. In the strongbox. And there is a letter with it that I am to read first.'

Myrrhine looked at her.

'He was in your life for twenty-five years and then for six,' she said, 'and he has left you a strongbox and a letter, and you are going to have to decide whether to open them.'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'When?'

'In the spring,' said Antonia, 'when they come. Not before.' She put the letter in her sleeve. 'There is nothing in a strongbox in Antioch that will not keep until the spring.'

---

It came in April, in four boxes and a strongbox, on a wagon with a military escort, and the escort was a young man of twenty-four who had been told to deliver it into the hands of Antonia Cassia and who was extremely correct about it.

The strongbox was iron, with two locks and a lid that had been sealed with the boar's head and the two letters. She broke the seal in the courtyard, with Myrrhine and Metrodora and Marcellina present, because she had asked them to be present, and because she had discovered in the last six years that she did not want to open anything alone.

Inside there was a bundle of papers tied with a thread, and a smaller box, and a letter.

She read the letter aloud.

*Antonia. You are reading this in the spring, and I have been dead since the summer, and I find that I am not able to write this as I would wish, because I have written it nine times and burned ten of them and this one is not elegant.*

*There are two things in this box. The first is a bundle of letters: thirty-one of them, one for each August since the year we met, which I wrote and did not send and did not burn. I have kept them because I discovered, at some point in my forties, that a man who burns a letter is a man who is arranging his own feelings for his own comfort, and that keeping them was more honest and considerably more painful. You may read them or burn them. They are not good. They are the letters of a man who was correct for thirty years and who was afraid.*

*The second thing is my will, in a copy made by my own hand, and the original is with the Vestals and will be opened in Rome in the autumn.*

*I have left the estate to my two daughters in equal shares. Marcellina is named in the will in the ordinary way. The second share is left to Metrodora, freedwoman, formerly of the household at Arretium, whom I have described in the will as my daughter by a slave woman of that household, born at Puteoli in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Corbulo.*

*I have thought about this for six years. I have thought about whether it is a gift or a violence, and I have concluded that it is neither, because it is not for her. It is for the record. There will be a document in the temple of Vesta, and in a hundred years, when the whole of this is nothing, there will be a girl in this city who is told that she cannot be anything because of her mother's condition, and there will be a copyist who finds the will of Lucius Tarquitius Priscus in a box and reads that he acknowledged a daughter by a slave woman in the year of his consulship. That is all it will do. It will not give her anything she does not have. But it will be in the record, and she will be in the record, and it will not be possible afterwards for anyone to say that she was not.*

*I have not asked her permission, and that is the only thing in this letter that I am ashamed of. I have spent my life doing things to people for their own good and calling it care. I am doing it once more, and I am doing it because I am fifty-nine and going to the east and I do not expect to come back, and because a man who is dying may leave a record, and a woman who is living must decide what to do with it.*

*You will decide. That is the last thing I have to ask you, and I have no right to ask it, and I am asking it because in six years you have never once done what I expected and I have come to rely on it.*

*L. Tarquitius Priscus.*

---

They read it in the courtyard in the spring.

Metrodora sat with her hands in her lap and did not move for a long time.

Then she said, 'He has put me in the will of a Roman senator as his daughter.'

'Yes,' said Antonia.

'Without asking me.'

'Yes.'

'And in the autumn the will will be opened in Rome, and there will be eleven men in the Forum who will read it out in the Acta, and by the winter every man in this city will know that Metrodora who keeps a school in the sandalmakers' street is the natural daughter of the consul Lucius Tarquitius Priscus and a slave.' She was very pale. 'And every girl in my school will be told by some woman in the market that her teacher is a senator's bastard, and there will be a version in which I am a fortune-hunter and a version in which I am a tragedy, and I will not be able to say a word in my own defence, because the whole of it is true.'

'Yes,' said Antonia.

'And you have known about this since April and you have not told me.'

'I have known since the letter came, which was eleven days ago,' said Antonia. 'I have not told you because I wanted you to hear it with the paper in front of you, in this courtyard, with witnesses, and not in the street.' She put the will on the table. 'There is a thing I can do. I am named in the will as the person to whom the strongbox was entrusted, and the will is not yet opened; the original is with the Vestals and it will be opened in the autumn. I have an advocate and I know the prefect of the archives, and I can have the clause about you struck out before it is read, on the grounds that it names a person who has not consented, and it would be irregular and it would take two months and eleven favours, and it could be done.'

Metrodora looked at her.

'And if it is struck out,' she said, 'what happens?'

'You are not in the will. You are not in the record. You are a freedwoman of the household of the Tarquitii who keeps a school, and nobody in Rome will ever have a reason to look at you twice, and in a hundred years the copyist will find the will of Lucius Tarquitius Priscus and it will say nothing at all about a daughter, and nobody will know.'

Metrodora sat in the courtyard for a long moment.

'No,' she said.

'Think about it.'

'I have thought about it. I have had nine days to think about it while you were deciding how to tell me.' She put her hands flat on the table. 'I have spent six years building a school and a name and a life out of nothing, and I have done it without a father and without a mother and without a name that anyone gave me, and I am not going to give it up to protect the sensibilities of a city that has never done one thing for me.' She looked up. 'Read it. Let them read it. Let the whole of Rome read it and let them say what they like. I have a school and a husband and a name in the register of the temple of Castor and eleven girls in the morning, and every one of them is going to learn to read, and in twenty years there will be a woman in this city who is somebody's clerk because Metrodora taught her, and the fact that my father was a senator will be a thing that people say about me at dinner, and it will not be the first thing or the second thing or the tenth.' She pushed the will back across the table. 'And I am not going to take the money.'

'It is a great deal of money,' said Antonia.

'I know what it is. I have read the schedule; it is four hundred and eleven thousand sesterces and a house at Arretium that I have never seen.' Metrodora stood up. 'I am going to take the house at Arretium and I am going to sell it, and I am going to put the money into the school, and the school will be in my name and not his, and there will be no plaque and no inscription and no thanks.' She picked up her box. 'And I am not going to read the thirty-one letters. You may read them or burn them. I have had one father for six years and he was a man who wrote me a letter every August and burned it, and I do not need thirty-one more.'

She went out.

Myrrhine, who had not said a word for a quarter of an hour, said, 'That is a remarkable woman.'

'She is,' said Antonia.

'She is exactly like you.'

'I know,' said Antonia. 'It is the most frightening thing that has ever happened to me.'

---

She read the letters in the summer, all thirty-one of them, in the garden on the Esquiline, sitting on a bench in front of the fountain that did not work.

They were not good. He had been right about that. They were the letters of a man who had been taught to write like a magistrate, and each of them began with the date and the year and an account of the weather, and then, in the middle of the second paragraph, the thing that had been in him for a year would come out in one sentence and he would retreat into the weather again.

The first was from the year she was sold. It said, in the middle of a paragraph about the harvest in Etruria: *I have been told a thing that I do not believe and I have decided to believe it because the alternative is to go to Puteoli and find out.*

The eleventh, from the year she opened the house on the Esquiline, said: *I am told that you have bought a house on the Esquiline and that you are doing very well, and I have ridden past the end of your street four times this year and have not turned into it, which I record here because there is no one else to whom I would record it.*

The twenty-third, from the year of the fire, said nothing about the fire at all for two paragraphs and then: *There was a fire on the Esquiline in April and nine houses were destroyed and I have spent the summer signing orders about it and I have discovered that a man can be of use at fifty-seven and that it is not the same as being admired.*

The last was written in July, in Antioch, three weeks before he died, and it was four lines long.

*The heat here is very great and the flies are worse than in Dacia. I have been reading the rescripts of the province and there are eleven that I cannot in conscience sign, and I have written to the emperor about them, and he will do what he does, which is usually the right thing and always a year too late.*

*I find that I have nothing to add. I have written thirty-one of these and each of them was supposed to say the same thing and none of them has said it. I am fifty-nine years old and I have been correct for the whole of it, and I find, at the end, that I would rather have been in a courtyard on the Aventine with a woman who does not forgive me and a daughter who calls me nothing at all, eating a fish that was cooked too quickly.*

*I hope you are well. L.T.P.*

Antonia sat in the garden with the letter in her hands.

Then she folded it with the others and tied the thread and put the bundle in her sleeve, and got up and walked to the middle of the garden, where the bronze tablet was, and stood in front of it for a while.

Two children were playing on the path: a boy of about seven and a girl of about five, and they had a hoop and a stick and they were arguing about whose turn it was. Beyond them the eleven fig trees stood in a line along the wall, and at the corner the fountain that did not work had a little green water in the bottom of it, and beyond the wall the whole of Rome went on in the sun.

The girl won the argument. She took the stick and hit the hoop and it went off down the path, wobbling, and the boy ran after it, shouting.

'Domina.'

She turned. Myrrhine was coming across the garden with a basket on her arm.

'It is the seventh day,' said Myrrhine. 'There is a fish, and I have made it too quickly, and Vibia is coming up from Ostia, and Marcellina has sent word that she will be late because of the children.' She stopped beside her. 'Are you all right?'

'Yes,' said Antonia.

'You have been standing in front of that tablet for a quarter of an hour.'

'I know,' said Antonia. 'It does not say anything about me. In a hundred years there will be a garden with a fountain that does not work and a plaque about a fire, and there will be nobody in this city who knows that I was ever here, and I find that I do not mind at all.' She took the basket. 'What is in it?'

'Bread, and the fish, and the lentils, and a jar of the wine that the proconsul sent from Asia before he died, and I have been saving it for a year and I have decided that tonight is the night.'

They walked out of the garden together, through the gate in the wall, into the street.

'Myrrhine,' said Antonia.

'Mm.'

'There is a thing I have never said to you, and I am going to say it badly, and then we will go home and eat the fish.'

'Say it.'

'For twenty-two years,' said Antonia, 'I have been a woman who was building something, and I have never once been able to say what it was for. Not the house. Not the money. Not the women in it or the book or the trial or the fund or the school. I have said to a great many people that it was for safety, and it was not for safety, and I knew it was not for safety at the time.' She stopped in the street. 'It was for this. A courtyard, and a fish cooked too quickly, and a woman coming up from Ostia, and a girl who calls me nothing at all, and seven people at a table on the seventh day of the month, and a garden where the house used to be, with children playing in it who have never heard of me.'

Myrrhine looked at her.

'Yes,' she said. 'I know. I have known for about ten years.'

'Why did you not say?'

'Because you were not ready to hear it,' said Myrrhine, 'and because I have discovered that there are two kinds of silence, and one of them is a thing that has to be borne and the other is a thing that is waiting for the right day.' She shifted the basket on her arm. 'This is the right day. Now come home; the fish will be cold, and I am not going to cook another one.'

---

In Rome, power is inherited, and fortunes are made, and some wounds never heal.

But a house is not the walls of it, and a name is not the register that holds it, and on the seventh day of the month, in a courtyard on the Aventine, there is a table with seven places at it, and a lamp, and a woman who was sold for two thousand four hundred sesterces in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Corbulo, and who was, in the end, worth considerably more.
