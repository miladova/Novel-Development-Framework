# 3 | The Weight of a Name

The room was small for a man of his standing, and that was deliberate. Lucius Tarquitius Priscus received visitors in a study off the peristyle with a single window, a table of old oak, two chairs, and a bronze bust of his father on a bracket at eye level, so that whoever sat opposite him sat also opposite the dead consul. The floor was black and white in a pattern that had been fashionable a hundred years before. There was no carpet. There was no cushion on the visitor's chair.

He was standing when she came in, and he did not sit until she had.

'Cassia,' he said.

'Tarquitius.'

Twenty-four years. He had been twenty-nine. She had been fifteen and had come into a room with a jug in her hands and her eyes down.

He was a tall man, narrow, with the thin-lipped, high-nosed face that appears on coins and in the histories of families that have produced more magistrates than children. His hair had gone the colour of iron and was cut short in the old style. His hands were beautiful and he used them when he spoke, sparingly, like a man who had been taught that gesture was a form of extravagance. He wore a plain tunic and no rings. Twenty-four years had put a fold in his neck and a shadow under his eyes and had not touched the essential thing about him, which was that he looked at the world from a height.

'Sit down,' he said.

'I am sitting.'

'You are sitting in the chair I offer my clients.' He went to the window. 'I have thought about this conversation for some time. I had intended to begin with the portico.'

'Begin with the portico.'

'Very well.' He turned. 'You have nine feet of public street under your portico on the north side. Your permit allows three. The six feet were granted by a clerk who has been dead eleven years, in a document that has been removed from the file. I have the file. I have had it since the spring. I could require demolition by the Kalends of October, and I could fine you the value of the land for twenty years, which would be one hundred and eighty thousand sesterces, and I could do it in a way that would appear in the Acta as a routine correction, and every man in Rome who owes you money would read it and understand that you were finished.'

Antonia waited.

'That is what I could do,' he said. 'I have not done it.'

'Why not?'

'Because I am not a fool, and because in the fourth region there are four hundred encroachments belonging to men whose fathers sat with my father in the Senate, and if I begin with the woman on the Esquiline I will be asked why I did not begin with the portico of the Aquilii, which is eleven feet, or the shopfronts of the Vitellii, which are in the street entirely. A curator who is seen to be settling a private account is a curator who will not be curator for long.' He came back to the table. 'So I will make you an offer instead. It is an honest one. I would like you to consider it as such.'

'I am listening.'

'Sell me the house. Not the business. The building, the land, the portico. I will pay you its assessed value, which is one million two hundred thousand, and I will pay it in cash, and the sale will be entered in the usual way, and the encroachment will become a thing that I have inherited and must regularize, which I can do quietly and at my own cost.' He sat down. 'You will keep your fortune, your women, your clients. You will move to another house, of which there are eleven for sale in the city at this moment, and in a year the whole thing will be a curious anecdote.'

Antonia looked at him for a long moment. The bust of his father looked at her from its bracket with its bronze eyes, which were not quite symmetrical; one had been repaired.

'Why do you want the house?' she said.

'Because it is on the north slope of the Esquiline, at the corner of the Vicus Patricius, and because in four years the new bath complex will have doubled the value of every foot of that street, and because I am not a man who pretends that he does not notice such things.' He said it flatly, as though the admission cost him nothing, and perhaps for him it cost nothing. 'And because I do not care to live in a city where a house of that kind stands at the gate of my hill.'

'There it is.'

'There it is,' he agreed. 'You asked. I have answered.'

'You have answered half.' Antonia put her hands on the arms of the chair. 'You do not want the house. If you wanted the house you would have bought one of the other eleven and let me keep mine, and you would have had the pleasure of watching me grow poor. What you want is for me to sell it to you. To come to your table and take your money and sign my name under your name and go away quietly. You want me to agree.'

The room was silent. Outside, in the peristyle, a fountain made the smallest possible sound.

'Yes,' he said.

'Why?'

'Because twenty-four years ago you were in my father's house,' said Tarquitius, 'and you took something from it, and you have been spending it ever since, and every year that you spend it in public I am required to watch.'

Antonia felt the blood go out of her face and come back. She had known he would say something. She had rehearsed eleven answers on the way up the hill in the litter, and not one of them had been for this.

'I took nothing,' she said.

'You were given nothing. There is a difference, and it is the difference the whole city lives on.' He leaned back. 'I am not going to argue with you about the past. I have no interest in it. I am interested in the autumn. In the autumn I will be examined by the Senate as a candidate for Asia, and men who dislike me will ask whether a man who cannot keep order on his own hill is fit to govern a province. I do not intend to be asked that question with your portico in the room. So. One million two hundred thousand, in cash, on the Kalends of September. You will be richer than you are now and I will be at peace, and in twenty years, when we are both dead, no one will remember either of us.'

'And if I refuse?'

'Then I will do what I am required to do as curator,' Tarquitius said, 'and it will be correct, and it will be legal, and it will be merciless. And you will know, when it happens, that it happened because you would not take a fair price, and that the fault was yours.'

Antonia stood up. She had not meant to stand up; her body had decided before her mind. He rose too, because he was well bred, and for a moment they were both standing with the table between them, and the distance between them was the width of a table and twenty-four years.

'You have made me an offer of one million two hundred thousand sesterces for a property worth two,' she said. 'You have told me that if I refuse, you will destroy me legally, and you have described the destruction in detail, and you have called it correct. And you have asked me to believe that you are doing this because of what I took from your father's house.' She heard her own voice, level, cold, and it was the voice she used in the worst moments of her business, and she was proud of it and appalled by it. 'Let me tell you what you are doing. You are fifty-three years old. You have one daughter and no son. In the winter you will go to Asia for a year, and while you are away the city will go on without you, as it always does, and when you come back there will be a new curator and new consuls and men who were children when you were consul, and they will not remember what you said in the Senate. You know this. You have known it for years. And the one thing in this city that still makes you feel that you are the centre of it is the fact that there is a woman on the Esquiline who will not bend, and that as long as she does not bend, you have an enemy, and a man with an enemy is a man with a place in the world.'

His face did not change. That was the frightening thing about him. His face did not change at all.

'Get out of my house,' he said, pleasantly.

'I am going.'

'You will not be offered again.'

'I know.'

'And you will send my mother no more books.'

'No,' said Antonia. 'I will send her better ones.'

She was at the door when he said, in a different voice, quieter, almost careless: 'You have a girl in that house. Seventeen. Bought in March.'

Antonia stopped. She turned.

'I have nineteen,' she said.

'I mean the singer.' He was looking at the window again. 'Lucius Sestius's son was in the Forum yesterday telling four men that he had heard her and that she will be worth ten thousand by the summer. He said it in the tone of a man describing a horse he intends to buy.' He turned. 'I am telling you this because I am curator of the public works and because it is nothing to do with me, and because you will not believe that I am telling you for any other reason. Do with it what you like.'

'Why?'

'Because I have been in this city for fifty-three years,' said Tarquitius, 'and I know what happens to a girl who is spoken of that way in the Forum before she is spoken of in a house. Good afternoon.'

---

He watched her cross the peristyle. She did not hurry, and she did not look back, and at the gate she stopped and spoke to the doorkeeper for a moment, and the doorkeeper laughed, and she went out into the street and the gate closed.

He stood at the window for a long time.

She had grown into a woman who could look at him. That was the thing he had not been prepared for. When he had thought about her — and he thought about her more often than he would have admitted to any man living, and always in the same three or four attitudes, like a set of bad statues — she had been fifteen, with her hair down, holding a jug, in a corridor that smelled of lavender and lamp oil. He had thought of her as a girl he had once been kind to. A girl he had made, in a sense: the money he had given her had been the beginning of everything she was.

There was a version of the story in which he was generous. He had gone over it so many times that it had become smooth, like a coin handled for twenty-four years. In that version he had loved her, or something like love, and his father had found out, and there had been a scene, and he had done what a son must do, and afterwards — afterwards he had arranged the manumission, and the money, and the introduction to the household of the merchant at Puteoli, and had told himself that this was mercy.

There was another version. He did not go over that one.

His secretary came in with the morning's letters and he took them and did not read them. On the table was the book she had sent his mother: Varro, in a bad fourth-century copy, with the owner's mark of a Greek library on the flyleaf. He had wanted to throw it in the fire. His mother had had it read to her twice.

'Domine,' said the secretary. 'The men from the fourth region are waiting.'

'Let them wait.'

'There are eleven of them, and one is the builder Caius Sentius, who has come three times.'

'Let him come a fourth time.' He turned. 'Did you find out what I asked you?'

The secretary hesitated. He was a careful man of forty with a club foot, freed by Tarquitius's father, and he had been in the house since before the events in question, which meant that he knew enough to be careful.

'The woman Bassa is still alive, domine. She lives at Baiae, in a house on the Lucrine road that was bought for her in the year —' he consulted his tablet, though he did not need to '— in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Corbulo. She is old and she is not well. She has a granddaughter who keeps house for her.'

'And the child?'

'There is no record of a child, domine.'

'There is always a record. There is a record of every slave born in Italy, because somebody paid tax on it.' Tarquitius turned from the window. 'Find the record. Do not send a slave; go yourself. Do not use my name. If the record does not exist, find out who was paid not to make it.'

'Domine.' The secretary bowed and went out.

Tarquitius sat down at the table and put his hands flat on the oak, one on either side of the bust of his father, and looked at nothing.

---

At the sixth hour he went to the baths, because he went to the baths at the sixth hour every day of his life and because a man who does not appear at the baths for a month is a man the city has begun to talk about.

The thermae of Titus were full. He went through the usual sequence: the apodyterium, the tepidarium, the exercise ground where he did not exercise but stood and was seen to have come, and then the caldarium, where he sat on the highest bench with a strigil in his hand and the steam so thick that nobody could see anybody's face.

Two men came in and sat on the bench below him, and did not know who he was, and talked about the woman Cassia for a quarter of an hour.

He listened to all of it. It was a small masterpiece of ignorance. She was, according to the elder of the two, a freedwoman of a Greek family who had made her fortune out of a shipping fraud at Puteoli. She was, according to the younger, the mistress of a senator whose name he did not know but who was certainly in the Senate that year. She had, according to both, four hundred thousand sesterces in a strongbox, a house full of women who were all in love with her, and a book in which the private business of half the city was written down and which she had used eleven times to destroy men who crossed her.

'She was in the basilica in November,' said the younger. 'My cousin was there. He said that she stood up and told the whole court what she cost when she was sixteen, and there was not a man in that hall who was not crying.'

'That is what she wants you to think,' said the elder. 'A woman like that does not tell the truth in a court. She tells the thing that will be believed, and she has been doing it for twenty years, and she has got very good at it.'

Tarquitius sat on the bench with the steam around him and the strigil in his hand.

He wanted to say: she was not good at it at fifteen. She was fifteen and she could read Latin badly and she laughed at a joke about a boundary stone.

He said nothing at all, and after a while the two men got up and went into the cold room, and he sat on the bench until his hands had begun to shake, which was the heat.

---

Afterwards he heard the men from the fourth region.

They came in one at a time, with their plans and their pleas, and he sat at the oak table with the file open, and he was, for two hours, exactly what he had spent his life training to be. The builder Caius Sentius had built a shopfront four feet into the street and had a lease going back forty years and a wife who was ill and three sons in the trade, and Tarquitius listened to all of it and said, 'You will pull down the four feet before the Kalends of August, and you will rebuild the front of the shop at your own cost, and you will keep the lease, and I will not fine you, because you came when you were called and you did not lie to me about the four feet.'

The man wept. Tarquitius looked at the table while he did it.

The second was a widow who had inherited a portico from a husband who had inherited it from a man who had built it in the reign of Vespasian, and who had no papers at all, and who had four children and a brother-in-law who wanted the property. Tarquitius gave her the portico. He gave it to her because the brother-in-law had come to him three weeks earlier with a purse, and because he had a memory for such things, and because the law, on the point, was on the widow's side, and because he liked the shape of the thing: a woman with no papers, keeping what was hers.

The third was a corn merchant named Felix who had built an upper storey over the pavement, and who had lied about it, and who had tried to have the surveyor transferred, and who had offered the surveyor's clerk a hundred thousand sesterces. Tarquitius made him pull down the storey, fined him the value of the land for ten years, and reported him to the prefect of the corn supply.

'Domine,' said the secretary, when the man had gone, 'the fine is heavy.'

'It is the fine the law provides.'

'It is the fine the law provides for a man who does not have friends in the Senate.'

'He does have friends in the Senate,' said Tarquitius. 'That is why the fine must be the fine the law provides. If I am easy on a man with friends, then every man without friends will know that the law is a thing that is bought, and then I will be the curator who sold Rome's streets, and there will be a poem about me by the winter.' He closed the file. 'And he lied to me. Write that down. Not in the record. In your own book.'

'You keep a book of men who lie to you, domine?'

'I keep a book of everything,' said Tarquitius. 'It is the only inheritance my father left that was worth having.'

---

That morning, before any of it, the salutation had been at the first hour and had lasted two. Forty-one men had come to the house to pay their respects, and he had received them in the atrium standing, because a man who receives clients sitting looks like a man who needs them. The first eleven had been senators and had been given eight minutes each. The next twenty had been equestrians with petitions and had been given three. The last ten had been freedmen, and had been given the pleasure of seeing him, which costs nothing and is worth a great deal.

His daughter had stood at the far end of the atrium through the whole of it, with her nurse, as she had stood every morning since she was fourteen, because a daughter of the house should be seen to be part of the house.

Tarquitia Marcellina was nineteen. She had her grandmother's bones and her father's height. She was dressed that morning in a plain white gown with a border of blue, and she had braided her hair herself, and she was holding a small ivory box, and she came to him when the last client had gone and said, 'Father, I have a petition.'

'You have a petition.'

'Yes.' She held out the ivory box. 'It is the money for the shrine of the Lares of the crossroads. The tenants of the two insulae have not paid their collection this month, and the man who collects has taken his share out of it and says that he is owed it for the carrying. I have counted it. There is eleven sesterces missing. I would like to pay it from my own allowance, and I would like to be permitted to go to the shrine myself and give it to the priest, because if I send a slave they will say that the lady of the house thinks herself too grand for the crossroads.'

He looked at his daughter, who was nineteen and had just handed him a lesson in the management of tenants, and who did not know that she had done it.

'You may pay it,' he said. 'You may not go yourself.'

'Father—'

'You may not go yourself because the crossroads of the Vicus Cyprius is not a place where a girl of this family walks without an escort, and because if you go with an escort the whole street will believe that the family is making some statement, and the priests of the crossroads will begin to expect it.' He handed back the box. 'Send the nurse. Send her twice. Make her stay and be seen to be bored.'

Marcellina took the box. Her mouth had set in a way he recognised, because it was his own mouth, and because he had set it in that exact fashion at her age, in that exact room, at his own father.

'And the betrothal?' she said.

'What about it?'

'It is in the winter.'

'It is in the winter.'

'I have met him four times,' said Marcellina. 'He is courteous. He is not clever. He has a great name and no money and he plays at dice with men who cheat him. He will spend my dowry in three years and then he will be sorry, and I will be in a house in the Subura with his mother, and I will be sorry, and neither of us will be able to say anything about it because we will have been seen at the games together.'

Tarquitius was silent. He had not known that she knew about the dice, and he was irritated with his secretary.

'He will improve,' he said.

'Will he?'

'Men improve when they have something to lose. You are something to lose.' He stood. 'This is not a matter for the atrium, Marcellina. If you wish to discuss it, come to my study at the ninth hour and we will discuss it as we would discuss any contract, and I will show you the accounts of his father's estate and you will see why the match is a good one.'

'I have seen the accounts,' she said. 'The nurse's brother is a clerk in the prefect's office. They are terrible.'

'Then you will see why it is a good match,' said Tarquitius. 'A man whose accounts are terrible and whose name is great will be grateful to the woman whose dowry saved him. Gratitude is the most reliable form of affection available to a marriage. I have seen it succeed a hundred times.'

'And the other kind?'

'What other kind?'

Marcellina looked at him. She had her grandmother's eyes, which were pale and direct.

'I have heard that there is another kind,' she said, 'and that people will ruin themselves for it, and that it is in songs, and that when it happens in this city it is always a scandal and sometimes a death. And I have lived in this house for nineteen years, father, and I have never once seen it.'

'You have seen a great deal of Rome for a girl of nineteen,' said Tarquitius, 'and none of it has been seen by you. Go and do your accounts. The eleventh sesterces will be found in my study at the ninth hour, and you will enter it in the book as a gift, not as a payment, because a gift is remembered and a payment is expected.'

She went. He watched her go, and thought, with a clarity that was almost pain: she will be married in the winter, and he could not protect her, and the boy would spend the dowry, and in ten years she would be a woman who had learned to say pleasant things in rooms where she was not listened to. It was the way of the world. It had been his mother's way. It would be his daughter's way.

And yet.

He went to the tablinum and worked until the sixth hour, and then he walked in the garden, and then he came back and stood at the window of the small study, and it was then that they told him Antonia Cassia had arrived.

Now, hours later, the light was going. He rang the little bell and told the slave to bring wine, and to bring it to the room where his mother sat.

---

Dinner in the house of the Tarquitii was at the eighth hour in summer, in the small dining room, with the three of them: his mother, his daughter, himself. It had been the same room and the same hour for thirty years. The walls were painted with scenes from the Aeneid in a style that had been old when his grandfather was young, and the couches were of the same pattern, and the silver was the silver that had come to the family with his grandmother, and had been repaired in eleven places.

His mother ate with her fingers because she was blind and would not be fed. Marcellina read to her while they ate, in Greek, from the book she had chosen, which tonight was not Varro but a volume of letters, and she read badly, and her grandmother corrected her without malice and without stopping.

'There is a thing I have been meaning to say,' said Tarquitius, when the plates had been taken.

His mother went on chewing.

'The woman Cassia came today.'

'I know. I heard her in the peristyle.' Tarquitia Priscilla wiped her fingers on the napkin. 'She walks like her mother. Did you know that? Her mother walked with her weight forward, as though she expected the floor to give.'

'Her mother was a slave in a fuller's shop at Baiae,' said Tarquitius.

'Her mother was a Bithynian who had been sold twice before she was twenty, and she had a way of holding her head that her daughter has, and you never noticed it because you have never in your life looked at a slave's mother.' His mother turned her blind face toward him. 'What did you offer her?'

'A fair price for the house.'

'And she refused.'

'She refused.'

'Of course she refused.' Tarquitia Priscilla held out her cup and a slave filled it. 'Lucius, I am going to say something to you, and you will not like it, and I am eighty-seven years old and I have been saying things you do not like since you were four.'

'I am fifty-three, mater.'

'You are a boy who has been left in charge of a very old house,' she said. 'You have spent your life being correct. It has been your whole armour. You were correct when you were quaestor and correct when you were consul and correct in the law courts and correct with your wife, God rest her, who was a stupid woman and deserved better than correctness. And there is one thing in this world that has never been correct, and it happened in this house, and it was not her doing, and you know it.'

Marcellina had stopped eating. She was looking at her plate with great concentration.

'We are not discussing this at table,' said Tarquitius.

'We have never discussed it at table. We have never discussed it anywhere. That is the thing about this family: we do not discuss, and then we die, and the masks go up in the cupboard, and the next generation inherits a silence and does not know what it is for.' His mother's voice did not rise. 'When I am dead you will have no one in this house who remembers, and you will find that a great relief for about a year, and after that you will find that you have become a man who talks to a bust.'

'Marcellina,' said Tarquitius. 'Leave us.'

'No,' said his mother. 'She is nineteen. She is to be married and taken out of this house in the winter, and she will sit at another table with another family's silences, and it would be a mercy if she knew, before she went, that the silences in this house were not her fault and were not her inheritance to keep.'

There was a long silence. The lamp on the sideboard had begun to smoke at the wick and a slave moved to trim it, and the shadow of the slave went up the painted wall and over the burning ships of Troy.

'Father,' said Marcellina, quietly. 'What happened in this house?'

Tarquitius looked at his daughter, and at his mother, and at the wine in his cup.

'Nothing that concerns you,' he said.

He said it pleasantly. He had been saying it pleasantly for twenty-four years, to himself, in the dark, in the middle of the night, when the whole of Rome was asleep and he was lying on his back in a house six hundred years old with the masks of dead men in the next room, and he had almost succeeded in believing it.

Almost.
