# 19 | The Small Room Off the Library

They sat in the small room off the library, where a girl with a remarkable voice had slept on a stool in the spring, and where a lamp had once burned all night while a woman sat with her hands in her lap and did not write on a tablet.

There was a table, two stools, a chest, and the lamp. Antonia had had the room cleaned that afternoon and the shutters closed and a brazier brought in, and she had put the box on the table and the bill of sale beside it and the folded letter from the Palatine beside that, and then she had sat down and waited for an hour.

Tertia sat opposite her with her hands in her lap and her box of tablets on the floor by her feet, because she had brought her box, because she had come from the shop, and because she did not know what else to do with it.

'I am going to tell you all of it,' said Antonia, 'and I am going to tell it in order, and I am not going to stop in the middle to ask you whether you are all right, because you are not all right and asking would be for my comfort and not yours. If you want me to stop, say stop. If you want to leave, leave, and the door is not locked and nobody will follow you. Do you understand?'

'Yes.'

'Then I will begin with a house above the Arno in the year you were conceived,' said Antonia, 'and I will tell you the whole of it, including the parts that make me look well and the parts that do not, and at the end of it you may ask me anything and I will answer it, and I will not lie to you, not even a little, not even kindly.'

She told it.

It took an hour and a half. She told it in the flat voice she used for the accounts, because the flat voice was the only one she trusted, and she told it in order, and she did not soften it: the linen room and the green glass window, the word *eleutheria*, the steward who told her to ask for a paper. The winter. The words *I will look after you*. The pregnancy and the eleven days in which she did not tell him. The villa in mourning and the sister from Rome and the man who came with the papers. Puteoli and Bassa and the room with the stain on the ceiling in the shape of a boot. The cry she heard and was told was the door. The turned earth at the bottom of a garden. Six years in a house with a fountain in the courtyard, and the book she learned to keep because the proprietor could not add. The hundred and ten thousand sesterces. The room above a laundry on the Aventine and the first thing she bought, which was a lock.

She told about the summer: the delator, the dealer's book, the eleven weeks. She told about Bassa dying and the paper under the tile.

When she had finished, the brazier had burned low and the lamp had been trimmed twice, and it was the second hour of the night.

Tertia had not moved.

'Show me the paper,' she said.

Antonia pushed it across the table.

Tertia picked it up and held it close to the lamp and read it four times, and Antonia watched her daughter read her own bill of sale: *a female child, age four days, of good health and no defect, for the sum of two hundred and forty sesterces; of slave condition, born of a slave woman of the household of the Tarquitii at Arretium; given the name Tertia in the household accounts, being the third such purchase of that year.*

'Two hundred and forty,' said Tertia.

'Yes.'

'That is less than the builder paid for his cart.' She put the paper down carefully, aligning it with the edge of the table. 'I have been thinking, in the shop, all afternoon,' she said, 'about what I would ask you. I made a list. I have it in my box; I wrote it on a tablet at the third hour and I have been adding to it all day. There are eleven questions on it.' She looked up. 'None of them is the question I am going to ask you now.'

'Ask it.'

'Why did you not look for me?'

Antonia sat with her hands in her lap.

'Because I was told you were dead,' she said, 'and because I believed it for twenty years, and because I have no other answer, and I know that it is not the answer you want.'

'It is the answer I expected,' said Tertia. 'I have been sitting in that shop since the sixth hour asking myself what a woman like you would say, and I made a list of the things you might say, and that was the first one.' Her voice was steady. 'And here is the thing, domina. I cannot tell whether it is true.'

'It is true.'

'You say it is true. You are a woman who has been in a court for six days and who has been acquitted, and you have spent your whole life being believed by people who did not want to believe you, and you have just told me a story in which you are the injured party in every single part of it.' She put her hands flat on the table. 'In your story, you did nothing wrong. You were fifteen and you were in love. You were sixteen and you were lied to. You were twenty-two and you bought your freedom and you were twenty-five and you built a house. Every person in your story is either a monster or a fool, and you are the only one who is neither, and I am supposed to sit here and weep and be your daughter.'

The brazier ticked.

'That is fair,' said Antonia.

'Is it?'

'It is fair and it is not the whole of it, and I am going to give you the rest now, because you have asked for the truth and not for a story, and because a woman who is told a story in which she is the injured party will spend the rest of her life being injured.' Antonia turned her cup slowly. 'I did nothing wrong when I was fifteen, and almost everything wrong afterwards. When I was twenty-two and I had money I did not go back to Arretium to look for a grave. I did not write to your father, not once, because I was told that he had ordered me sold and I was afraid. When I was twenty-six and I had a house and a strongbox, I could have paid a man to go to Arretium and look at a grave and ask a question, and I did not, because a part of me did not want to know. When I was thirty and I was rich and I had a hundred friends in this city, I could have bought the whole of the estate at Arretium and gone through the household books, and I did not, because by then I had a position and a reputation and a house full of women who depended on me, and because looking would have cost me something.'

'What would it have cost you?'

'Everything I had built,' said Antonia. 'In this city a woman who keeps a house like mine survives by being a woman with no past. The moment I began to ask questions in Arretium, the whole of it would have come out: the villa, the son of the house, the child. It would have been in every dining room in Rome within a month, and my clients would have gone elsewhere, because a man will go to a whore but he will not go to a woman whose story he has to think about.' She looked up. 'So I did not look. For twenty years I did not look, and I told myself that it was grief, and it was not grief. It was arithmetic.'

Tertia sat very still.

'Thank you,' she said at last. 'That is the first true thing anyone has ever said to me about my own life.'

---

'There is more,' said Antonia.

'Of course there is more.'

'The letter.' She pushed it across the table. 'It is from your father's mother. She is seventy-eight and blind and she lives on the Palatine, and she wrote it in the summer and gave it to me last night, and she told me that I might read it and decide whether to give it to you, and I have read it, and I am giving it to you.'

Tertia did not take it.

'My father,' she said.

'Yes.'

'You have not told me his name.'

'No,' said Antonia. 'I have told you the whole of it except his name, and I have been waiting for you to ask.'

'Why?'

'Because when I say it, everything in your life will rearrange itself, and I would like it to happen while you are looking at me and not while you are reading a letter.' Antonia put her hands on the table. 'His name is Lucius Tarquitius Priscus. He is fifty-three years old. He was consul in the year of the consulship of Nerva and Virginius Rufus. He is the curator of the public works, and in the winter he is to be a candidate for the proconsulship of Asia, and he stood up in the Basilica Julia on the sixth day of this month and defended me for two hours and eleven minutes in front of the whole of the city, and he did it knowing that it would cost him the province and probably the rest of his career, and he did it because he had discovered that the woman he was defending was the mother of his daughter.' She stopped. 'He was in that court every day. You were in the gallery every day. You have been in the same room with him for six days and neither of you knew.'

Tertia's face had gone entirely white.

'I saw him,' she said. 'I saw him — I wrote his name on my tablet, the first day, because I write down the names of everyone who speaks. Lucius Tarquitius Priscus, for the accused.' Her hands were shaking. 'I have been copying that name for nine days. It is on the charge sheet and in the Acta and at the bottom of the household register that freed me, and I have written it twenty times with my own hand and I never once —'

'No,' said Antonia. 'Nobody would. There is nothing in the world that would make you look at a name you have copied a hundred times and think: my father.'

'I have been in his household's books since I was four days old,' said Tertia. 'That is what the paper says. He owned me. He owned me for nineteen years and I never saw him, because I was at Arretium and he was in Rome, and I was a line in a book in a room I never entered.' She was very quiet. 'And when I was freed, the register said that I was freed on the instruction of Tarquitia Priscilla, and I thought: a kind old woman. I thought: somebody in that house noticed me.'

'She noticed you on the day you arrived,' said Antonia, 'and she did nothing for thirteen years, and then she freed you and gave you four thousand sesterces and a trade and sent you to Rome, and she has been sitting in that house ever since with the knowledge of it, and she has written you a letter, and it is on that table.'

Tertia picked up the letter. She did not open it.

'What does it say?' she said.

'It says what I have just told you. It does not ask you for anything. It does not ask you to come to the house and it does not ask you to forgive her, and it does not call herself your grandmother.' Antonia looked at the folded paper. 'It is a confession, and it is the only one that anybody in that family has ever made, and she made it to me last night because she is seventy-eight and because she is afraid of dying with it. Read it or do not read it. Burn it or keep it. It is yours and nobody will ask you about it again, not her and not me.'

Tertia folded the letter and put it in the sleeve of her dress with the bill of sale.

'And the other one,' she said. 'The one who sold me.'

'She is at Tibur, and she is seventy-nine, and she is dying of a disease of the joints and in a great deal of pain. Her name is Tarquitia Aurelia and she is your father's sister. She had you sold on the day you were born and she took four thousand sesterces out of the household accounts, and she bought a house with some of it, and she has lived in it for twenty-five years, and nobody has ever asked her to explain herself.' Antonia's voice was flat. 'I am not going to tell you what to do about her. If you want to go to Tibur and stand at the foot of her bed, I will send you in my own carriage with two men. If you want her to die without ever hearing your name, that is also permitted, and it is not a sin, and I have thought about it myself for four months and I have decided that I do not want to see her face. But you are not me, and it is not my decision.'

---

Tertia sat with her hands in her lap for a long time.

Then she said, 'I have eleven questions on a tablet and I am going to ask them now, and some of them are not fair, and I am going to ask them anyway.'

'Ask.'

'Was I wanted?'

'Yes.'

'By whom?'

'By me, from the moment I knew I was carrying you, and I was fifteen years old and I had no idea what a child was,' said Antonia. 'I had a plan. I worked it out in a garden at Puteoli over six weeks, and it involved a room above a shop and a lamp and a basin and the two of us, and it was a stupid plan and I have never in twenty-five years been able to think about it without wanting to put my head in my hands. But it was a plan, and it had you in it.'

'Did he want me?'

Antonia was silent for a moment.

'He wanted to do the right thing,' she said. 'Which is not the same, and it is more than most men in that position would have offered. He arranged for me to be sent to a midwife of his family's, with a trade and a sum and a manumission to be registered after the birth. He wrote to me three times. And then he was told that you and I had both died, and he believed it, and he has believed it for twenty-five years.' She stopped. 'I do not know what he would have done if he had known. Nobody will ever know. That is the thing about a lie: it does not only take away the truth. It takes away the person you would have been if you had known it.'

'Did he love you?'

'He was twenty-nine and I was fifteen,' said Antonia, 'and I loved him, and I do not know what he felt, and I am not going to invent it for him or for you. He was kind to me. He taught me to read Latin properly and he laughed when I found a hole in his argument, and he said that if I had been born a man he would have made me a jurist, and he said, "I will look after you." That is all I know. It is not enough to build a father out of, and I would not do it if I were you.'

Tertia nodded slowly.

'When is my birthday?'

Antonia stopped.

'Nobody has ever known that,' said Tertia. 'The household at Arretium kept a day for me; the sixth day before the Kalends of October, because that is the day I was delivered to them. Sabbia kept it. When she died, nobody kept it, and for seven years I have had a day in October that is not my birthday and I have not told anyone, because when you tell somebody that you do not know your own birthday they look at you in a particular way.' She turned her cup. 'You know it.'

'I know it,' said Antonia. 'You were born on the twenty-ninth day of August, at the ninth hour of the morning, in a house on the Lucrine road at Puteoli. It was very hot. There was a bird outside the window and I remember being angry with it.' She put her hands together. 'I have kept that day for twenty-five years. I have gone to that garden every August and stood in it. This year I did not go, and she died while I was not going.'

Tertia was quiet.

'The twenty-ninth of August,' she said. 'I am a late summer child.'

'You are a late summer child.'

'It is a strange thing,' said Tertia, 'to be given a birthday in a room at the second hour of the night by a woman I have known for eleven days as my employer.' She said it without irony. 'I am going to have it recorded. In the register, when I change my name. Metrodora, freedwoman, born the twenty-ninth of August in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Corbulo.' She looked up. 'Was the bird a gull?'

'I do not know,' said Antonia. 'It was a bird, and it would not stop, and I have hated gulls for twenty-five years, which is unjust and I know it.'

'It is completely unjust,' said Tertia. 'Gulls are the most honest creatures in Puteoli; they eat what they find and they do not pretend to be anything else.' And then, quite suddenly, she laughed — one short, hard, surprised laugh, and put her hand over her mouth as though she had done something wrong, and Antonia sat in the lamplight and looked at her daughter laughing and thought that she would remember it for the rest of her life, and that it had cost two hundred and forty sesterces and twenty-five years.

---

'Do you hate him?'

'I have hated him for twenty-five years,' said Antonia. 'I hated him on a pallet in a house in Puteoli and I hated him in a basilica and I hated him every time a man in this city said his name and I had to keep my face still. And this summer I found out that the hatred was built on a letter that his sister wrote and his mother helped to compose, and I have spent four months finding out what that does to twenty-five years of hating, and the answer is: not very much. It does not give me back the years and it does not make him innocent. It just means that the man I have hated does not exist, and I have not yet decided who is standing in his place.'

'And now?'

'Now I am a woman of forty-one who is going to close her house in March,' said Antonia, 'and who has a great deal of money and no family and a trial behind her, and who has been sitting in this room for a fortnight waiting for a woman of twenty-five to come through the door and tell her what happens next.'

Tertia looked up.

'That is what I wanted to ask,' she said. 'The eleventh question. What do you want from me?'

---

Antonia sat back.

'I have thought about that every day since August,' she said, 'and I am going to answer it properly, because it is the only question that matters and because I have had four months to prepare the answer and I still do not have it right.'

She turned her cup around once.

'I want you to come to dinner on the seventh day of every month. I want to know what you are reading and whether the builder pays you properly and what you think of the new prefect. I want to be a woman of forty-one who has somebody to leave her money to. I want, when I am old and difficult, to have a person in the world who will tell me that I am being a fool, and I would like it to be you, because you are the only person I have ever met who might do it.'

'That is a great deal,' said Tertia.

'It is a great deal and it is not the answer,' said Antonia. 'The answer is that I do not want anything from you that you do not want to give, and that I am not able to say the second half of that sentence honestly, because I want it so much that I can feel it in my hands. I have been a woman who takes what she wants for twenty-five years. It is how I got the house and the money and the trial and the whole of it. And I am telling you, here, in this room, that I am not going to do it to you.' She looked at her daughter. 'So this is what I propose. You will go home tonight and you will think about it for a month, and you will not see me and I will not write to you, and if at the end of the month you want nothing to do with me, you will send me a note and I will not trouble you again. And if you want something — dinner, or money, or a name, or a woman to sit with you in a room while you decide what to do with your life — you will write and tell me what it is, and it will be yours, and there will be no conditions, and you will never have to hear the story again.'

Tertia sat with the letter in her sleeve and the bill of sale in her hand.

'A month is a long time,' she said.

'It is the shortest time I could offer you and still be honest,' said Antonia. 'Two weeks would be me hurrying you. A year would be me punishing myself, and you would be sitting in your rooms in the fourth region waiting for a woman on the Esquiline to finish being noble, and that is not fair to either of us.'

Tertia stood up.

'I am going to take the box,' she said. 'The linen. I have been thinking about it since you said it and I want to see it.'

Antonia took the chest from under the table and opened it and took out the folded cloth.

It was thin and worn and the colour of old paper, and there was a brown stain in one corner, and along the hem there was a line of small cross-stitches in dark red thread.

Tertia took it in both hands. She stood in the lamplight in the small room off the library and looked at it for a long time, and then she turned it over and found the stain with her thumb, and Antonia watched her daughter's face do something that she had no name for.

'It is very small,' said Tertia.

'You were very small.'

'I have been in this city for seven years,' said Tertia, 'and I have had a good life, and I have eleven thousand sesterces and a trade and a friend who is a midwife and a builder who is fair to me, and I have been happy in a way that most people are never happy, and I have told myself a hundred times that I did not need to know.' She folded the cloth. 'I was wrong. I have needed to know every single day of my life and I did not know it until tonight.'

She put the cloth in her box of tablets. Then she said, 'My name is not Tertia. I have been Tertia since I was four days old because I was the third child bought into a household that year. Nobody in the world has ever given me a name. I took the builder's name because that is what freedwomen do and it is not mine either.' She looked up. 'What was my mother's name? Your mother. The one who died in Baiae.'

Antonia was silent for a moment.

'Her name was Metrodora,' she said, 'and she was a Bithynian, and she was sold twice before she was twenty, and she died of a thing in the throat when I was six, and I have not said her name out loud in thirty-five years.'

Tertia nodded.

'Then that is what I will be,' she said. 'Not Cassia. Not Vipsania. Not a name out of a book. I will be Metrodora, and I will have it registered in the town records as a free woman's name, and in a hundred years there will be a woman in this city with that name on her tomb and nobody will know where it came from.'

She picked up her box, and went to the door, and turned.

'On the twenty-fourth day of December,' she said. 'The day before the Saturnalia. I will come to dinner on that day, and I will bring the woman I live with, because she is a midwife and she has been asking me questions for three days that I cannot answer, and because she will want to look at you.' She opened the door. 'And domina — I am not going to call you anything else yet. I am not being cruel. I do not have a word for you. I have been a woman with no mother for twenty-five years and I do not know what the word is, and I am not going to use one until I do.'

'That is fair,' said Antonia.

'I know it is fair,' said Tertia. 'I am trying to be fair. It is the only thing I have ever been good at.' She went out.

---

Antonia sat in the small room off the library for a long time after the door closed.

The brazier had gone out. The lamp was low. The chest was open and the bill of sale was gone and the letter was gone and the piece of linen was gone, and on the table there was nothing but the lamp and the cup and her own two hands.

At the fourth hour Myrrhine came in with a fresh lamp and stood in the doorway.

'Well?'

'She is going to be called Metrodora,' said Antonia, 'and she is coming to dinner on the twenty-fourth of December, and she is bringing a midwife, and she is not going to call me anything at all.'

Myrrhine came in and set the lamp down and sat on the stool opposite her.

'That is better than I expected,' she said.

'It is better than I deserved.'

'Yes,' said Myrrhine. 'It is. And in the morning you are going to get up and go to the Palatine, because he asked you to come on the Ides and bring the girl, and the girl has said she will not be called anything by anybody for a month, and you are going to have to tell him that.' She reached across and took Antonia's hand. 'And then you are going to come home and we are going to start closing this house, and it is going to take four months and it is going to be the hardest thing you have ever done, and I am going to be here for all of it.'

Antonia looked at her.

'I am going to hold you to that,' she said.

---

At the sixth hour, when the sky was beginning to grey, she went up onto the roof.

The house was quiet below her: the last of the lamps, the smell of the night's fire, the baker's boy asleep in the passage with his mouth open. She lay on the tiles in her good dress and looked at the sky, which in November is not brown but a deep, clean, cold black with actual stars in it.

She thought about a girl of fifteen in a linen room with the sun coming through green glass, who had copied a Greek word onto a scrap of wood and kept it in a box with a piece of blue glass.

She thought: in March I will be a woman of forty-two with a fortune and no house and a daughter who calls me nothing at all, and in twenty years I may be a woman of sixty-two with a daughter who calls me something, and there is no way to get from one to the other except by living every day of the twenty years.

She thought about the words *I will look after you*, which she had believed in a corridor in November when she was fifteen, and which had turned out to mean a cart at the fourth hour of the morning and a piece of bread from a kitchen woman who wept.

And then, because she was a woman who had been in business for eleven years and because her mind had a way of going back to the accounts, she thought about the men who had been standing at the corner of the street for four days.

She had noticed them on the second day. Two men, then three, then four, at the fountain and at the corner by the wineshop, in the plain tunics of men who did not wish to be noticed, and they were not the prefect's men, because the prefect's men wore a badge, and they were not thieves, because thieves do not stand in the same place for four days.

She had told Meno. Meno had told Brennus. They had put a second bar on the garden door and taken turns at the gate.

In the morning she would have to decide what to do about it, and in the morning she was going to the Palatine to tell a man on the Ides that his daughter was going to be called Metrodora and that she would not be called anything by anybody for a month.

It would keep until the morning.

She lay on the tiles and looked at the stars until the sky began to be grey at the edge, and then she went down and slept for two hours, and dreamed of gulls.
