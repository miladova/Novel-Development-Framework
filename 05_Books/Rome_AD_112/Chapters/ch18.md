# 18 | The Verdict

Before dawn, Myrrhine came into her room with a lamp and a basin and said, 'Get up. You are not going to lie there and think.'

Antonia got up.

She washed in cold water. She sat on the stool while Myrrhine pinned her hair, and the two of them were reflected in the bronze mirror, one behind the other, and Myrrhine's hands were perfectly steady and her face was the face she wore when she was frightened and had decided not to be.

'Which dress?' said Myrrhine.

'The blue.'

'The blue is unlucky. You wore it to the dedication and it rained and a man was crushed in the crowd at the gate.'

'The blue,' said Antonia, 'because it is the dress of a woman who is not asking for anything.'

Myrrhine put the pins in.

'If it goes badly,' she said, 'what happens?'

'If it goes badly I am convicted of the first charge and the penalty is exile from Italy with the loss of a third of my property, and I will not be in a cell; a woman of my rank is not put in a cell. And the second charge carries the closing of the house and the sale of the women, which would be done by the court and not by me.' Antonia looked at her hands. 'And then I would go to Massilia and you would come with me, and we would have two thirds of a fortune and no house and no name and a very quiet life, and I would spend the rest of it being a woman who was once something in Rome.'

'That is not so bad,' said Myrrhine.

'No,' said Antonia. 'That is what frightens me. It is not so bad at all. I have been lying awake for six days discovering that I could survive almost anything, and that the only thing I could not survive is being a woman who could survive anything.' She stood up. 'Bring the tablets. And Myrrhine — if it goes badly today, there is a letter in the strongbox for you, and the strongbox is in the wall behind the second press, and the key is in the box of the four things, and I want you to open it before the magistrate's men come.'

Myrrhine's hands stopped.

'You have never told me about the box of the four things,' she said.

'No,' said Antonia. 'I am telling you now.'

---

She walked to the basilica.

She could have taken a litter; Cinnamus had ordered one, and it was at the gate, and she looked at it and said, 'I will walk,' and Cinnamus said, 'Domina, it is a mile and a half,' and Antonia said, 'Then we will be late.'

They walked down the Vicus Cyprius and along the Subura and past the Argiletum and into the Forum in the grey light of a November morning, with Brennus in front and Cinnamus behind and Myrrhine at her side and eleven women of the household walking behind her in their good clothes, and the city came out to look at them.

It was not a hostile crowd. It was worse than hostile: it was curious. They stood in doorways and on steps and at the corners with their arms folded and their children in front of them and watched a woman walk down her own street to be tried, and some of them called out, and some of them said nothing, and one old woman at the corner of the Vicus Patricius said, quite loudly, to nobody in particular:

'She paid for my son's funeral.'

Antonia did not turn her head.

At the door of the basilica, the clerk of the court met her and took her through the side entrance, and in the dark passage behind the platform she stopped and put her hand against the wall.

'Domina?' said Cinnamus.

'One moment.'

She stood there with her hand on the wall for the length of about eleven heartbeats.

Then she took her hand away and went out into the hall.

---

The court sat at the second hour, and the voting took until the fifth.

The procedure was this. Each of the twenty-two judges came forward in order of rank and put a tablet into the urn: a tablet marked with an A for absolution, or a C for condemnation, or, if he had not made up his mind, an N, which was not permitted by the law and which had never in the memory of the court been used, and which every man on that platform knew was the only honest answer for at least four of them.

They came forward one at a time. The hall was so quiet that the tablets could be heard going into the urn.

Antonia stood at the table with her hands folded. She did not count. She had told herself in the morning that she would not count, and she did not count for the first eleven, and then she counted the twelfth, and after that she counted every one.

She had been in the basilica for four hours. She had slept perhaps three hours in the night and had eaten because Pamphilus had stood over her. She was aware of a great many small things at once: the smell of the crowd, which was the smell of the city in November, wool and smoke and wet stone; the way the light came through the high windows and lay in bars across the marble floor; a man in the third row with a cough who had been coughing since the first hour; the fact that her left hand was cold and her right hand was not.

The fourteenth tablet went in. The fifteenth.

At the sixteenth, a man in the front rank — a consular of sixty-five, with a good name and a bad reputation — paused at the urn for a moment, turned his tablet over in his fingers, and looked at her.

Antonia looked back at him.

He put the tablet in.

At the nineteenth, Tarquitius leaned over and said, without moving his lips, 'Do not count. Whatever you are doing with your fingers, stop it.'

'I am counting.'

'Stop,' he said. 'If you are counting and you are wrong by one, you will spend the next hour of your life in a state that no woman should be in when the verdict is read, and I need you to be standing up straight when it is read, because half of them will decide how to look at you afterwards based on how you look at that moment.'

She stopped counting.

It did not help.

---

While the urn was carried up to the table, Antonia looked at the men who had voted.

The fourth man on the left had a brother who was still in her debt and would be for two more years, and he was looking at his hands.

The seventh on the right had been in her house nine times and had once, in the fifth year, sat in the small room by the garden for an hour and told her about his son, who was seventeen and could not read, and had wept; and he was looking at her now with an expression of pure hatred, and she understood that he had voted for absolution and that he would never forgive her for it, and that for the rest of his life he would tell a story in which she had been acquitted because she was clever and not because he had been afraid.

The old man at the end of the second row, the one who owed her nothing, was writing something on his tablet, and when he had finished he sat with it in his hand and looked at it, and Antonia thought: he has written his reason. He will keep it. In twenty years, when the whole of this is a paragraph in somebody's history, there will be an old man's tablet in a chest in a house on the Caelian with three lines on it in his own writing, and only the cockroaches will read it.

The two friends of Cirrus were in the third row, on the prosecution's side, and they had been called as witnesses on the first day and had not helped. They looked young and ill, and one of them had been crying; not, Antonia suspected, for their friend, but because they had been in a house on the night when a boy died and had gone home and slept, and because the whole city had heard them described in the Acta as drunk, and because they were twenty-three and would be that in every room they entered for the rest of their lives.

The presiding magistrate counted the tablets himself, with two clerks, at the table in front of the platform, and it took a quarter of an hour, and then he stood up.

'Judges of this court,' he said. 'On the charge of causing the death of Marcus Sestius Cirrus by poison: thirteen for absolution, nine for condemnation. The accused is absolved.'

The hall made a sound like a wave breaking.

'On the charge of keeping a disorderly house and of corrupting the young: eleven for absolution, eleven for condemnation, and the court being equally divided, the accused is absolved by the law's presumption.'

The sound went on. Somebody in the gallery was shouting. Somebody else was crying, and it was not, Antonia thought, a woman; it was a man of about fifty in an equestrian stripe, in the fourth row, who had come to her house eleven times in six years and who was now, finally, weeping.

'Silence,' said the magistrate, without much hope. 'Silence, and hear the opinion of this court, which is not a verdict and has no force in law, and which I have been instructed to read.'

The hall quieted.

'The court observes,' he read, 'that the accused has been absolved on all charges, and that the absolution is not to be understood as an approval of the house she keeps. The court further observes that the law of this city permits such a house, that the accused has kept it with an honesty unusual in her trade, and that the evidence in this case has disclosed no crime except the sale of a noxious substance by a man who has been dealt with, and the death of a young man of good family who was, by his own act, the author of it. The court observes that the prosecution was brought in good faith by a father, and that it was conducted in a manner which this court does not commend.' He paused. 'The court recommends that the accused close her house, not because the law requires it, but because the city has voted, in this hall, in the sight of Rome, and it is better that a woman of her ability should be occupied in some other way.'

He put the paper down.

'The court is dismissed. The accused is discharged. The bond of one hundred thousand sesterces will be returned to her household.' He looked at Antonia for the first time, directly, and he was an old man with a tired face. 'Domina. A word with you, if you please, before you go.'

---

They took her into the small room behind the platform, where the magistrates robed, and the presiding magistrate sat down heavily and rubbed his face.

His name was Gnaeus Domitius Tullus and he was sixty-nine and he had been consul twenty years before, and he said, 'Sit down. There is wine; it is bad, and it is what the court provides.'

Antonia sat.

'You are going to be told a great many things in the next four days,' he said. 'You are going to be told that you have won a great victory. You are going to be told that the city is with you, and there will be a crowd in the Forum tonight, and there will be a man selling your image in clay, and in a month there will be a woman in the fourth region who will name her daughter after you, and in three years there will be a play.' He poured wine for himself and not for her. 'It is all nonsense. You have not won a victory. You have survived a case that should never have been brought, and you survived it because you are clever, and because you had the best advocate in Rome, and because the prosecutor's household killed a witness in a manner that even a Roman court could not ignore, and because eleven men on that platform have been in a house like yours and were afraid.'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'Do you? Then let me tell you the part you do not know.' He put the cup down. 'The emperor has been informed of this case from the beginning. He has read the Acta, and the charge, and he has had my report on it this morning, and he has sent me a message, which I am to deliver to you personally and not in writing.'

Antonia's hands were cold.

'What is the message?'

'That the emperor does not trouble himself with the morals of the city, which he says are a matter for the courts and the gods, and that he does not intend to make a martyr of a woman who pays her taxes and has never been convicted of anything.' The magistrate looked at her. 'And that he would be grateful if, in the spring, the house on the Esquiline were a house in which no further young men died, because he is about to begin the greatest building programme in the history of the city, and he does not intend to spend his year explaining a brothel to the Senate.'

'That is not a condition,' said Antonia.

'It is not a condition in law. It is a condition in fact.' The old man turned his cup slowly. 'You are going to close the house. I know you are; I have heard from my wife's cousin that you have been drawing up deeds for the women for a month. So I am not asking for anything. I am telling you what I know, so that you understand that the recommendation of this court was not a recommendation, and that the emperor's gratitude has a shape, and that the shape is: no more noise.'

Antonia sat in the small room with the bad wine and the smell of robes and old sweat.

'And the praetor?' she said.

'Marcus Sestius is outside this room, in the hall, and there are two men of the urban prefect's office waiting for him with a warrant in the matter of a fire in the ninth region,' said Domitius Tullus. 'I have known about that warrant since the day before yesterday. I have been sitting on that platform for six days with two pieces of paper in front of me, and I have been very careful not to let one of them fall on the other.' He stood up. 'I am sixty-nine years old and I have done a great many things in this city that I am not proud of. I am going to retire in the spring, and I am going to write a book about the law of boundaries, and no one will read it.' He went to the door. 'Domina. For what it is worth. When you told that court what you cost, the whole hall was with you, and I was with you, and I have been in that chair for eleven years and it is the first time in my life that a witness has made me ashamed of my own household. That is all. It is not much. But I am seventy and I will not have another chance to say it to anyone.'

---

They came out of the basilica at the sixth hour into a crowd that filled the whole of the Forum.

Antonia had not expected it. She had expected two hundred people and a few shouted insults. There were, she thought afterwards, perhaps eight thousand: the whole of the lower Forum and the steps and the portico of the Castors and the space in front of the Temple of Vesta, and they had been waiting since the voting began, and when she came out on the steps the noise came up like a blow.

She stood at the top of the steps with her household behind her: Cinnamus with the four boxes, Meno with his stick, Brennus, Myrrhine, Marcellina in her grey dress, nine women of the house in their good clothes, and the baker and his wife and their children, who had come because they had insisted on coming.

'What do I do?' she said.

'Nothing,' said Tarquitius at her shoulder. 'Stand still for a moment and let them look at you, and then walk down the steps and go home, and do not make a speech.'

She stood still.

The noise went on for a long time, and in it she could distinguish things: her name, chanted by a group of young men near the Arch of Tiberius; a woman's voice, high and clear, shouting something that might have been *the baker's family*; a man near the front who was shouting *whore* over and over, and being shouted down by the people around him; and a group of four men in the fourth row who were not shouting at all, but standing with their arms folded and their faces closed, watching her.

She walked down the steps.

Halfway down, a woman pushed through the crowd and put something into her hand: a small clay figure with a house on the base, and the roof coming off in her fingers. Antonia looked at it and then at the woman, who was perhaps thirty and dressed in the plain wool of a shopkeeper, and who said, 'I have one at home. I bought it on the second day. My husband said it was foolish.'

'It is foolish,' said Antonia.

'Yes,' said the woman, and then she said, 'My mother was sold twice,' and went back into the crowd.

---

At the bottom of the steps, at the edge of the Forum, the urban prefect's men were waiting, and they took Marcus Sestius Verus by the arm in front of three thousand people.

He did not resist. He was standing with his jurist and two of his clients, and he had been watching Antonia come down the steps, and when the men came up to him he turned and looked at them and then, over their shoulders, at her.

The crowd had gone quiet in patches.

Sestius said something to the prefect's man, quietly, and the man shook his head, and Sestius took off his own ring and gave it to his jurist, and then he walked away between the two men through the crowd, which opened for him and closed behind him and did not shout at all.

Antonia watched him go.

She had wanted this in September. She had arranged it in September, with a leather envelope and a boy from the street, and she had told herself that it was not revenge but arithmetic, and she had believed it, mostly. And now she stood at the bottom of the steps of the Basilica Julia in the middle of a crowd that had come to see her acquitted, and watched a man of fifty-eight in a good toga being taken away between two guards, and found that she felt nothing at all except a kind of enormous tiredness.

'He will be acquitted,' said Tarquitius.

'What?'

'The fire. He will be acquitted of the fire; there is no body and the contractor who signed the statement is nine years dead, and his sister's husband will not testify, and in a year he will be a man with a grudge and no office.' He was looking at the crowd. 'But he will never be praetor again, or consul, or anything at all, and every man in this city will know why, and that is worse for him than exile. You have not killed him. You have made him a man who is spoken of in the past tense.'

'Is that better?'

'It is what you did,' said Tarquitius, 'and it is done, and you will spend the rest of your life deciding whether it was worth it, and so will I.' He turned to her. 'I am going home. I have a daughter who has left a letter on my table and a mother who has not spoken to me in four days, and I have been in a basilica for six days defending a woman I have spent twenty-five years hating, and I find that I would like to sit down in a dark room.'

'You defended me,' said Antonia.

'I did.'

'Why?'

He looked at her for a long moment.

'Because you were right,' he said, 'and because I have spent my whole life being correct, and being correct is the cheapest thing in the world; it costs nothing and it earns everything and it has never once in fifty-three years required me to do anything difficult.' He pulled his toga straight. 'That is the first honest answer I have given in twenty-five years, and I am too tired to say another, and there are eleven things I have to say to you that cannot be said in the Forum. Come to the house on the Ides. Not the back door. The front gate, in the morning, with your name given to the doorkeeper.' He turned away. 'Bring the girl.'

---

She went up to the room at the top of the west wing before the household gathered.

The strongbox was in the wall behind the second press, and she opened it and took out the four things and put them on the table in the light of the lamp: the ring of iron keys, too small for any door in the house; the lock of dark hair tied with a thread that had once been red; the scrap of papyrus with a name and a date in a hand that was not hers; and the small clay figure of a woman with her hands over her face.

She stood looking at them for a while.

Then she took the clay figure and put it in her sleeve, and she took the papyrus with the name and the date and the lock of hair and put them in a small box of their own, and she put the box in the chest with the piece of linen that had a cross-stitched hem and a brown stain in the corner, and she shut the chest.

The iron keys she left on the table. They had belonged to a woman in Arretium who had died in the year she was sold, and she had kept them for twenty-five years without ever knowing why, and this evening, for the first time, it seemed to her that she did know, and that the reason was not a good one, and that she was finished with it.

She went down to the garden with the keys in her hand and gave them to Cinnamus.

'What are these?'

'Keys to nothing,' said Antonia. 'Melt them down and put the bronze in the household account as a credit of eleven sesterces, and enter it in the book properly, in your own hand, with the date.'

Cinnamus looked at the keys and then at her.

'Domina,' he said, 'I have kept your books for more than a decade and I have never once entered a thing in them that I did not understand.'

'Then this will be the first,' said Antonia, 'and it will do you good. It is the only entry in that book that will ever be worth nothing at all, and every other one of them has cost somebody something.'

---

The house was full that night.

Not with guests. There had been no guests for a month and there would be no guests again. It was full of its own people: the eleven women who had stayed, the eight who were not the business, the baker's family, the two men of Naso's who had come to guard the silver, Vibia Lucana, who had arrived at the ninth hour with three baskets of food and had taken over the kitchen, and Celer, who had come up from Tusculum at the news and who was standing in the peristyle looking as though he had been struck on the head.

Antonia came down at the ninth hour into the garden, where every lamp in the house had been set on the walls and on the tables, and the whole household was waiting, and she stood on the step by the fig tree with a tablet in her hand.

'I am going to read you a list,' she said.

They were quiet.

'The house closes on the Kalends of March. Not before; there is too much to do and I am not going to put nineteen people into the street in December to make a point. Between now and then, each of you will have a deed. It will be drawn by my advocate and registered in the temple of Castor and a copy will be kept by Cinnamus and a copy by you, and it will say what you are to have, and no one — not me, not a magistrate, not a creditor, not a husband — will be able to take it from you.'

She read the list.

She had worked on it for a month with Cinnamus, and it took a quarter of an hour, and she read it in the flat voice she used for the accounts, and she did not look up until she had finished.

Eucharis: the twelve thousand she had paid, returned, and the contract of the room over the garden for three years at no rent, and a letter to a household at Neapolis where a freedwoman with a harp could name her own terms.

Prima: two hundred thousand sesterces held in trust, and the house on the Aventine she had found, bought outright in her own name, with a deed that had been registered three days before, and the key, which Antonia gave her across the garden in front of everyone, and which Prima took without a word and put in her sleeve.

Chloe, who had gone and had come back in October because her sister's husband was a fool: a sum and a place in a fuller's business at Ostia.

The two cooks: a share in a cookshop near the Macellum, and the name of a man who would sell them the lease.

The laundry woman: passage to Narbo, where her brother lived, and a letter.

Meno: a cottage at Tibur belonging to Naso's cousin, and two hundred sesterces a year for life, and, because he had asked for it in September and had been told to wait, a new stick with a bronze ferrule.

Brennus: three hundred thousand sesterces, and a letter to the magistrate of Lugdunum, and the name of a man in the vigiles at Rome who would write to the governor of Gaul on his behalf, and nothing whatsoever guaranteed, because a man who has been sold away from his children nine years before cannot have them given back by a deed.

'There is a thing I should say,' said Antonia, when she had finished. 'I have been told all day that I have won. I have not won. A boy is dead, and a woman called Doris is dead, and a man I have known for eleven years was taken away in the Forum this afternoon, and I did most of it myself, and I would do most of it again, and those two sentences will be true for the rest of my life.' She looked at them. 'What I have won is this: not one of you will go out of this house in March into a place I have not chosen with you. That is all. It is not much. It is more than I had, and it is more than most women in this city will ever have, and I intend to spend the next four months making sure that not one word of it is undone.'

She put the tablet down.

'Now eat,' she said. 'Vibia has taken over the kitchen and she is a tyrant and there is a great deal of food.'

---

They ate until the second hour of the night.

At the end, when the lamps were low and the children had fallen asleep on the benches and Meno was asleep sitting up with the new stick across his knees, Antonia went out to the gate to say goodbye to Vibia, and found Tertia Vipsania standing in the street with a box under her arm.

She had not been at the house since the day in court. She had not sent word. She was in her working dress with her hair pinned and her stylus behind her ear, and she had been crying, and she had stopped.

'You said the day it was over,' said Tertia.

'It is over,' said Antonia.

'I heard the verdict at the fourth hour. I was in the fourth region, in the shop, with the builder, and a man came in and told us, and the builder gave me the afternoon off, and I went and sat in my room.' Her voice was steady and her hands were not. 'And then I waited until it was dark, because I did not want to come up this hill in the daylight and have people look at me, and then I came, and I have been standing at this gate for an hour because I do not know what to say to the doorkeeper.'

'Come in,' said Antonia.
