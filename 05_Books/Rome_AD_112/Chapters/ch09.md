# 9 | The Dedication

It rained on the morning of the dedication, which the whole city took as an insult and then, by the ninth hour, as a blessing, because the rain stopped and the stone came up cool and the smell of the streets was washed away for the first time since June.

Antonia went. She had not intended to go. She had intended to spend the day in the house with the shutters closed, doing the accounts of the grain contracts, which had come back from Ostia with three signatures and a clause that she did not like. But on the morning itself she stood at the window of her bedroom and looked down at the street, where the whole hill was already moving, and she thought: if I do not go, they will say I was afraid.

There were eleven thousand people in the streets between the Esquiline and the Forum that morning. There were litters and chairs and donkeys and a man with a trained bear and three separate processions of priests going in different directions, and the vigiles standing at the corners with their buckets and their poles and their expressions of men who expected trouble and intended to be early to it.

Antonia went in a plain litter with Myrrhine and two of the women and Brennus walking beside it, and they came down the Vicus Cyprius into the flood of the city, and it took them an hour and a half to travel a distance that could be walked in a quarter.

The new forum was not finished. It had been eleven years in the making, and it had swallowed a hill and a market and a temple and part of the old Greek quarter, and the emperor had paid for it out of the Dacian spoils, which was the only reason the city had tolerated the noise. But the great basilica was done, and the two libraries, and the column with its spiral of carved soldiers, and the open square where the equestrian statue stood in the middle of everything like a man who has arrived at a party and knows that the room is looking at him.

The ceremony was in the square, in front of the basilica, and the space was so large that most of the people in it could not see the ceremony at all, and did not mind, because the point of a crowd is to be a crowd.

Antonia stood with Myrrhine at the edge of the equestrian order's section, where she had a right to be, because her fortune was above the census, and where she was tolerated, because a woman of her kind is tolerable at a public ceremony if she stands at the edge and does not attempt to be seen. She wore a dark blue dress and the carnelian ring and nothing else, and she stood with her hands folded and her face composed, and for the first hour she watched the crowd instead of the ceremony.

The crowd was the city in its own shape. There were senators in their broad stripes and their careful dignity, and equestrians with their narrow stripes, and the people in their tunics, and the women in their rows with their veils and their children, and in the upper part of the square, where the slope came down from the old quarters, the whole mass of the poor, wedged in and cheerful.

There were slave women carrying wine and cakes in baskets, calling their prices. There were pickpockets, and there were men who watched the pickpockets and would take a share later. There was a girl of about seven sitting on her father's shoulders, with a garland of laurel that was too big for her, holding on to his hair. There was a man in a toga that had been mended at the hem, who stood very straight and mouthed the words of the ceremony as they were spoken, and who Antonia realised, after a while, was a freedman who had learned the words from a book because he had never been allowed to hear them.

When the emperor came out onto the steps of the basilica the noise went up like a physical thing, and Antonia found that her eyes had gone wet, and she was furious with herself, and she was not the only one.

He was a heavy man, forty-nine, with a broad face and grey hair and the particular look of a soldier who has been promoted beyond his expectations and has decided to be worthy of them. He did not look like a god. That was the thing that people said about him afterwards, in the crowd, going home: *he does not look like a god, he looks like a man who is good at his job*. He stood with the consuls and the prefects and the priests of the city, and he spoke for eleven minutes, and the speech was about stone and water and the duty of the state to the city, and it was not a great speech, and the crowd loved it.

'He is not much of an orator,' said a man behind Antonia, to his neighbour.

'No,' said the neighbour. 'But he pays on time.'

---

When it was over, and the incense had been carried away in its silver dishes and the priests had gone off in their procession to the Capitol, Antonia did not go home.

There was a distribution of money at the gates of the basilica — three hundred sesterces to every citizen on the roll, which was the emperor's way of saying that the Dacian gold had been well spent — and the crowd had to be managed in lines, and the lines had to be managed by the vigiles, and the whole of the great square was a slow-moving river of people who had been given money and were determined to enjoy it.

Antonia stood with Myrrhine at the foot of the column and watched the queue.

'We should go,' said Myrrhine.

'In a moment.'

'You have been saying that for an hour.'

'I am watching something,' said Antonia, and it was true. She was watching the queue, which was the truest thing in Rome, because a queue is a society with the decoration removed. There were men in the queue who had been born free and men in the queue who had been freed the year before and men in the queue who were technically the property of the man in front of them and who had been sent to collect his money. There were women with babies and old men with sticks, and there was a blind beggar being guided by a dog, and there was a group of Jews from the Trans Tiberim who had come across the river for the distribution and who stood together, separate from the line, waiting until the crowd thinned, as people do who have learned that being first is not always a good idea.

And at the head of the queue, at the table where the money was given out, there was a clerk with a list, and a freedman of the prefect's office beside him, and a soldier, and beside the soldier there was a man whose whole function, Antonia realised after a while, was to look at the faces of the people who came to the table and decide which of them were citizens and which of them had borrowed a toga.

'It is the most honest thing in the city,' she said. 'A list of names, and a man with a stick, and a queue. Everything else in Rome is a performance. That is an account.'

'You are in a sentimental mood,' said Myrrhine. 'It is the rain.'

'It is the emperor,' said Antonia. 'He was not a god, and he did not pretend to be, and for a whole morning the city was governed by a man who was good at his job, and everyone in the square felt the same thing at the same time, and it was not love. It was relief.' She turned. 'And it will not last, and in twenty years the Senate will be back to arguing about blood, and the man after him will be worse, and my daughter's children will be told that this was the golden age and will not believe it.'

She stopped. She had said it out loud, in the middle of the Forum, with eleven thousand people around her.

Myrrhine looked at her.

'You said my daughter,' she said.

'I said nothing.'

'You said my daughter, and the man in front of us in a brown cloak heard you, and he has already decided that you meant something else, because no woman in a dark blue dress in the equestrian section says my daughter in the Forum of Trajan in the year of the consulship of—'

'Myrrhine.'

'—and we are going home,' said Myrrhine, 'now, and you will talk about the weather, and I will not ask you anything, and when we are behind the door of your own study you will tell me what you have done, and if you do not tell me I will put laudanum in your wine and find out from Cinnamus.'

'Cinnamus does not know.'

'Cinnamus knows everything,' said Myrrhine. 'Cinnamus knew before you did. Cinnamus has probably known since June and has been keeping it in the third stack, which is where he keeps the things that are neither.'

---

They were ten paces from the edge of the square when a woman in a green dress stepped out of the crowd in front of them and said, 'Antonia.'

It was Vibia Lucana, and she was flushed and her hair was coming down and she was holding the hand of a small boy of about six, who was eating a honey cake and had it all over his face.

'This is my sister's son,' said Vibia. 'I have lost my sister. She was in the crowd by the fountain and now she is not, and I have been walking in circles for a quarter of an hour and he has eaten three cakes and I have begun to be afraid that I will be arrested for kidnapping a child I am related to.'

'He is not troubled by it,' said Antonia.

'He is six. Nothing troubles him except the absence of cake.' Vibia looked at her, and her face changed. 'You have been crying.'

'I have not.'

'You have the face. I have seen that face at eleven funerals and one wedding.' Vibia glanced at Myrrhine, and something passed between them, and then Vibia said, in a different voice, 'I have heard a story about you and the curator of the works in the portico.'

'There was a rainstorm. There were four hundred people in the portico.'

'There are always four hundred people in every story,' said Vibia. 'That is what makes them believable.' She shifted the boy's hand in hers. 'I am going to tell you something as a friend, and then I am going to go and find my sister and pretend I never said it. You cannot be seen with him. Not once, not ever, not in the rain. Not because of what it means; because of what it will be made to mean. There is a kind of man in this city who cannot bear the thought of a woman with a house and a fortune and no husband, and those men have been looking for a way to explain you for eleven years, and now you have given them one. The explanation is not that you are clever. The explanation is that you have a protector on the Palatine, and everything you have built is his, and every woman who works for you is his, and the whole of your life is a front for a man's appetite.' Vibia's voice was quiet and hard. 'I have seen it happen to a woman in Ostia. She kept a shipping office for nineteen years. A rumour like this got into her business and she was dead in three years — not ruined. Dead. She took a fever because she could not afford a physician and her friends had all been told that she was somebody's mistress and had stopped calling.'

The boy pulled at her hand and said something about a dog.

'Thank you,' said Antonia.

'Do not thank me. Go home.' Vibia turned, and then turned back. 'And Antonia — the thing I said I would ask you for, one day. I have not decided what it is yet. But I want you to remember that I stood in a crowd and told you the truth when it would have been easier to tell you a joke.'

'I will remember.'

'Good,' said Vibia, and went off into the crowd with the boy, calling her sister's name.

---

It was after the ceremony, when the crowd had begun to break up into its smaller currents, that the rain came back — not the morning's steady rain but a sudden violence out of the north, the sort that Rome gets three or four times in a summer and never expects. The whole square turned at once and moved for the colonnades. Antonia, with Myrrhine and Brennus, was carried by the crowd into the portico of the basilica as though by a river, and there she stopped, because the portico was full and there was a wall, and there was nowhere to go.

She was standing with her back to a marble column and water running off the edge of the roof in a sheet, and Myrrhine was pressed against her shoulder and saying something that could not be heard, and a senator's wife was complaining about her hair, and a small boy had lost his mother and was being passed overhead from hand to hand toward the outer edge, where a woman was screaming his name.

And Lucius Tarquitius Priscus was standing four feet away, with his toga over his arm and his hair wet, and he was looking at her.

They looked at each other for a moment. The noise of the rain on the roof was enormous.

Then he came across the four feet — it took him some time, because the crowd was a wall — and stopped in front of her, and said, in a voice pitched to be heard by her and not by anyone else, 'This is unfortunate.'

'It is rain,' said Antonia.

'It is a portico full of the entire Senate,' said Tarquitius, 'and I am standing in it, and you are standing in it, and there are forty men here who are already composing the sentence they will use at dinner.'

'Then you should go and stand somewhere else.'

'There is nowhere else. The alternative is the street, and I am not going to be seen running in the street in a wet toga at my age.' He looked at the rain. 'We will talk about something that no one can misrepresent. Tell me about your grain.'

Antonia's mouth twitched, once, despite her.

'You have heard about my grain,' she said.

'I have heard about your grain from eleven men, all of whom told me a different thing, and one of whom told me that you were ruining the city and another of whom told me that you had saved it.' He watched the water coming off the roof. 'I have also read the contract. The prefect of the corn supply sent me a copy because I am a jurist and he wanted an opinion on clause nine.'

'And what is your opinion of clause nine?'

'It is unenforceable. You have written a clause that requires you to deliver at a price fixed by the prefect in the event of a declared shortage, and there is no definition of a declared shortage anywhere in the document, and if the prefect declares one and you refuse to accept his price, the case will go to the courts and you will win, because the clause is void for uncertainty.' He turned his head. 'You knew that when you wrote it.'

'I knew it when I dictated it.'

'Then clause nine is not a promise. It is a performance of a promise, designed to be shown to the prefect and to be litigated if it is ever tested, and the whole of the city will believe you for a year, and in a year the shortage will be over and no one will ever test it.' He said it without heat, as a man states the price of a fish. 'It is very cleverly done. I have seen worse men do worse things with better paper.'

'It is not a performance,' said Antonia.

'No?'

'If the prefect declares a shortage, I will deliver at his price, and I will not litigate, and I will lose two hundred thousand sesterces on the transaction, and I will do it because I have written the clause and because a woman in my position cannot afford to be shown to have written a clause she did not mean.' She looked at him. 'You cannot understand that, because you have never in your life had to be believed by people who did not wish to believe you.'

The rain was easing. The crowd had begun to move.

'You are wrong about that,' said Tarquitius, and there was something in his voice that she had not heard in twenty-five years, and it was gone almost before she heard it. 'I am going to ask you something, and you will answer it, and then we will not speak again until it is necessary.'

'Ask.'

'The publicani's tablets. Naso will produce them if you tell him to. The case is in October. If the tablets come out, the prosecution succeeds, and Atilius is exiled, and the Senate is reminded, for one season, that the law applies to governors.' He looked at her. 'If they do not come out, the case fails, and the provincials of Lusitania are told again what they have been told all their lives, which is that the law is a thing that happens in Rome and not in the provinces.'

'And what do you want from me?'

'I want you to tell Naso to produce them,' said Tarquitius, 'and I want you to want it, because the only thing in this city that I cannot buy, bully or argue into existence is a reason for a woman like you to help a man like me, and I am asking you to have one.'

'There is one.'

'Tell me.'

'Because the governor's brother-in-law is Marcus Sestius,' said Antonia, 'and because Sestius has spent the summer telling the city that my house should be closed, and because I would like him to spend the autumn explaining three receipts with his brother's name on them to a bench of consulars.' She smiled, and it was not a warm smile. 'You asked for a reason. I have given you a reason. It is not a noble one, and it is the only kind I have ever been able to afford.'

Tarquitius looked at her for a long moment. The rain had almost stopped. The light had come back, and the wet marble of the square beyond the portico was shining like something polished.

'That is a better reason than mine,' he said.

'What is yours?'

'I want to win,' said Tarquitius. 'I have wanted to win since I was a boy of nine in a house where my father had already won everything, and I have spent forty years finding out that winning is a thing that happens to you once and then becomes a habit that no longer feeds you.' He straightened his toga. 'I will tell the prefect that the tablets will be produced. You will tell Naso. And Antonia —'

She had not heard him say her name in twenty-five years. She had thought, in the way one thinks such things, that he had probably forgotten it, that she was to him a category, "the woman Cassia," a name on a bill.

'— the boy Cirrus is telling a story about your house,' he said. 'He is telling it in the baths and at dinners, and the story is that he was thrown out by a doorkeeper while you laughed, and that the singer is available at a price, and that you are the kind of woman who keeps a girl for a man and then sells her to his betters. Two of those things are lies and one of them is a slander and none of them will matter until the winter, when he will begin to say them under oath.'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'Do you know that his father has begun to collect evidence of the disorder of your house? He has a slave who was in your kitchen for a month in the spring.'

Antonia turned her head. 'Which slave?'

'A woman called Doris. She is not in your house now; she was sold in June to a baker in the fourth region, and Sestius bought her back in July and has her in his household, and she has been talking for a fortnight.' He said it evenly. 'She says that there are men in your house every night, that the women are paid wages, that wine is served to slaves, and that on the night of the Ides of May a young man was thrown into the street by a doorkeeper and that you stood in the doorway and laughed.'

'That is not what happened.'

'It is close enough to what happened to be sworn to,' said Tarquitius. 'A lie that is nearly true is the only kind that survives a cross-examination. You know that; you have been in court.'

The crowd had begun to move out into the square, and the sunlight was coming through the portico in long bright bars, and there were people pressing past them now from both sides, and a man with a tray of cakes shouting, and a woman calling for her son.

'Why are you telling me this?' said Antonia.

Tarquitius looked at her.

'Because in October I will be prosecuting a governor,' he said, 'and I will need to be a man whose judgement is respected, and it will not be respected if it is known that I warned the keeper of a disorderly house about a charge before it was laid.' He turned to go. 'So this conversation did not happen. You heard it from a clerk. You have never spoken to me in your life. Good day, domina.'

He went out into the light, and the crowd swallowed him, and Myrrhine said, at Antonia's shoulder, in a voice like a knife:

'Everyone saw.'

'I know.'

'Everyone saw, and half of them heard, and by the ninth hour of the night there will be a story, and the story will not be that the curator of the public works warned you about a charge. The story will be that you have been his mistress for twenty years and that the house on the Esquiline is where he goes on the seventh day.' Myrrhine took her arm. 'We are going home. Now. And you will not speak to anyone on the way, and if you smile at a single man I will personally put you in a litter and sit on you.'

---

They went home through streets that were steaming. The whole city was out on the doorsteps, and the whole city had an opinion, and Antonia sat in the litter with her hands in her lap and listened to it through the curtains.

'A woman in blue, in the portico of the basilica, talking to the curator—'

'—the one from the Esquiline, the one with the house—'

'—they say the emperor looked at her—'

'—they say she has a senator's ring—'

It was extraordinary, she thought, how quickly a city could build a thing out of nothing, and how much pleasure there was in it, and how little of the pleasure was malice. They were not being cruel. They were making the day into a story, because a day is long and a story is short, and because the alternative — that nothing had happened in the portico but two people out of the rain — was unbearable to a crowd that had stood in the wet for four hours.

---

On the Palatine, the story arrived at the seventh hour, in the form of a steward who came to the door of the small room where his mistress sat with a rug over her knees and said that there was a thing being said in the city that she might wish to hear.

Tarquitia Priscilla heard it out without moving.

'And what,' she said, when he had finished, 'is my son doing about it?'

'Nothing, domina. He came home at the fifth hour and he has been in his study since, and he has sent for the file on the Lusitania case, and he has not spoken to anyone.'

'Good,' said the old woman. 'That is what a man does when he has done something foolish and intends to behave as though he has not.' She was quiet for a moment. 'Caius. How long have you been in this house?'

'Thirty-one years, domina.'

'Thirty-one years. You came the year of the fire in the fifth region.' Her blind face turned. 'Do you remember, in the year after my husband died, a woman called Sabbia who was in the household at Arretium?'

The steward was silent.

'I remember her, domina.'

'Was there a child with her, when she came back from Campania?'

The silence went on for a long time. In the courtyard below, someone was watering the plants, and the water made a sound on the hot stone.

'There was a child, domina,' said the steward. 'She was entered in the household accounts as purchased at Puteoli, in the name of the master, and she was nursed by Sabbia's sister, and she was given the name Tertia, because she was the third child bought into the household that year.' He paused. 'She is not in the household now. She was freed seven years ago on your instruction, by the council's leave, and given a sum, and sent to Rome with a trade.'

'I know,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'I remember what I instructed. I am asking whether you remember what I did not instruct.'

'Domina.'

'Bring me the household list for the year she was bought,' said the old woman. 'The original. Not the copy. And Caius — you will bring it to me yourself, tonight, after the household is asleep, and you will not enter it in any book that you have brought anything to me at all.'

The steward bowed and went out.

Tarquitia Priscilla sat with her hands in her lap.

She had known, in the way that a blind woman knows things, that the story of the portico would come to her before the week was out, and she had known what she would do when it came, and she had known it for twenty-five years, and she had not done it, and now she was seventy-eight years old and her son was fifty-three and there was a girl of nineteen in the house who had begun to ask questions.

'Well,' she said, to nobody at all. 'It has taken a long time.'

---

At the gate Meno took her cloak and said, quietly, 'There is a young woman in the garden, domina. She came at the ninth hour. She says she is the daughter of the house of Tarquitius.'

Antonia stopped.

'She said that?'

'She said, "Tell her it is the girl from the fig tree."' Meno's face was entirely without expression. 'I put her in the garden and told her to sit on the bench, and I told Prima to sit where she could see her, and Prima is there now, and the girl has been crying for an hour.'

---

Marcellina had been at the dedication. She had stood in the women's section with her grandmother's nurse and two of her father's clients' wives, and she had seen the ceremony, and she had seen the emperor, and she had seen, at the edge of the equestrian section, a woman in a dark blue dress who stood with her hands folded and watched the crowd instead of the ceremony.

Then the rain had come, and the crowd had moved, and Marcellina had been carried with it into the portico of the basilica, and she had been twenty feet away when her father came through the crowd and stopped in front of Antonia Cassia and spoke to her for the length of time it takes to say eleven sentences.

She had heard three of them. She had heard *this is unfortunate*, and she had heard something about grain, and she had heard, at the end, in a voice pitched low, the word *domina*.

She had stood in the portico with the rain coming off the roof and the crowd pressing and her heart going like a bird in a sack, and she had understood in that moment, with a clarity that was like a door opening in a dark house, that her father was not a man with a secret. He was a man with a whole second life, going on in the same city, on the same days, in the same rain, and she had been nineteen years in his house and had never been told.

So she had gone home, and then she had gone out again, and she had come to the Esquiline in the rain, and she had sat in the garden under the fig tree for an hour, and she had cried, and she was ashamed of the crying and could not stop it.

'You said you would not come back,' said Antonia.

'I know what I said.' Marcellina's face was blotched and her hair was coming down. 'I have been in that portico. I heard him say domina to you, in the voice he uses for the gods and the dead. He has never in his life said my name in that voice.'

'What do you want me to tell you?'

'The truth,' said Marcellina. 'All of it. Not three things. I am not a child and I have been in a house with a cupboard in it for nineteen years, and in the winter I am to be married to a man who will spend my dowry, and I have decided, this afternoon, in the rain, in that portico, that I am not going to spend my life being told three things.'

Antonia looked at her for a long moment. Then she sat down on the bench, and put her hands in her lap, and looked at the wall.

'If I tell you the truth,' she said, 'you will have to carry it, and it will be heavy, and you will not be able to put it down, and the person it will hurt most is a man you love, who is not a monster and is not a good man, and who has spent twenty-five years building a wall around a room with nothing in it.' She looked up. 'And if I tell you, you will be in my debt, because that is how it works with secrets: the person who gives you one owns a part of you forever. I am telling you this now, before you ask, so that afterwards you cannot say I did not warn you.'

'I understand,' said Marcellina.

'You do not,' said Antonia. 'Nobody understands that until it happens to them.' She was silent for a moment. 'But you are nineteen and you are alone in this city and you have asked, so I will tell you one thing tonight, and it is the only one you are ready for.'

She told her about the linen room.
