# 4 | Debts Called In

Naso came on the fourth day after the Ides, and he came in the morning, which was a bad sign. Men who bring good news come at night, when there is wine.

He would not sit in the study. He walked up and down it, three paces and turn, and the lamp on the table watched him go.

'He has written to the urban prefect,' he said. 'Not about you. About the fourth region generally, and about the inspection of private structures, and about the need for a schedule of demolitions by the Kalends of October. It is a routine letter. It has been copied to eleven men, of whom I am one, because I am a banker with property on the Esquiline and therefore a person who ought to be informed.' He turned. 'Do you know what it is like to receive a routine letter from a man who is going to be proconsul?'

'Tell me.'

'It is like being told, very politely, that your name is on a list.' Naso stopped walking. 'My partners have read it. My wife's brother has read it. Two of them came to me yesterday and asked whether the house of Cassia is a good risk, and I said yes, and they asked me to say it again with a number attached, and I could not.'

'How much?'

'Two million by the Kalends of September.' He held up his hand. 'I know what I said in the spring. I am not withdrawing the loan; I am moving the date. If I do not have the money by September I will have to sell my share of the Ostian warehouse to a man I do not like, and I would rather be thought a coward by you than be ruined by my own brothers-in-law.'

Antonia stood by the window with her back to him.

'You are not a coward,' she said. 'You are a man with partners. I have been a man with partners. It is an illness that makes you say things you would not otherwise say.'

'That is a generous reading.'

'It is an accurate one,' she said, 'and it costs me nothing, which is why I can afford it.'

She turned around. She had been awake since the fourth hour, which was when the letter from Ostia had come, and she had spent the two hours before dawn doing arithmetic on a wax tablet, and the arithmetic had been good.

'I can have it by September,' she said. 'Not from the house. From the grain.'

Naso's face did something complicated.

'You have been listening to the Alexandrian,' he said.

'I have been listening to everyone. That is what I am for.' She sat down at last and pushed the tablet across the table. 'Read it.'

He read it. He read it twice. Then he sat down, which was the first thing he had done that morning that resembled a man at his ease.

'The granaries are oversold,' he said. 'That is the whole of your argument. There is a rumour that they are oversold, and the rumour has been in the city for a month, and the price has gone up eleven per cent on the rumour alone, and if the Alexandrian fleet comes in on time the price will fall, and everyone who bought on the rumour will be ruined, and you will be one of them.'

'The fleet will not come in on time.'

'You cannot know that.'

'I can know that the corn merchant Felix has been buying contracts for grain he does not have,' said Antonia, 'because he has been doing it for two years, and because last month he was fined by the curator of works for a building offence and lost nine hundred thousand sesterces in a single afternoon, and because a man who has just lost nine hundred thousand sesterces and who is already selling grain he does not own will not stop. He will double. That is what men like Felix do. They do not reduce; they double, because the alternative is to admit that they are finished, and there is no man in Rome who will admit that before dinner.'

Naso looked at the tablet.

'You want to buy his contracts,' he said.

'I want to buy his contracts at a discount, from a man who is desperate, in the last week of August, when he has realised that he cannot deliver and that the prefect of the corn supply is asking questions.' Antonia poured wine for both of them, which was itself a statement. 'Then I will hold them. I will not sell in the panic. When the price peaks — and it will peak, because there is less grain in Italy than the price says there is — I will deliver, and I will deliver to the city at a price that is high but not obscene, and the prefect will remember that when everyone else was hoarding I delivered, and that is worth more than the profit.'

'And the profit?'

'One million eight hundred thousand, if I am right. Two million two hundred if the harvest in Africa is as bad as the reports.' She drank. 'If I am wrong, I will lose the house and I will go to Puteoli and keep a shop, and you will have to explain to your brothers-in-law that you lent money to a woman who tried to corner the corn supply of Rome, which is a crime, and which will be in all the courts by the winter, and you will be ruined in a way that will be remembered for a generation.'

Naso sat very still.

'You are telling me this,' he said, 'so that when it happens I cannot say I was deceived.'

'I am telling you this so that you understand what you are being asked to do, which is not to lend me money. It is to hold your nerve while I do something that could destroy us both.' She set down her cup. 'I have never lied to you, Gaius. Not once in eleven years. There are men in this city who think that is a virtue. It is not. It is a business practice, and it has made me a great deal of money, and it is the reason you are sitting in this room instead of sending a slave.'

Naso looked at her for a long moment. Then he picked up his cup and drank the whole of it, which was not his habit.

'August,' he said. 'Not September. My brothers-in-law will not last until September.'

'August.'

'And if Felix will not sell?'

'Then I will find the man who holds his paper,' said Antonia, 'and I will buy it from him, and Felix will never know I was there until it is too late to stop it. There is always a man behind the man. That is the first thing I learned in this city, and it is the only thing I have never had to revise.'

She went to Ostia on the eleventh day after the Ides, at dawn, in a covered carriage, with Cinnamus and Brennus and two men she did not know well enough to like and trusted completely, which was a combination she had learned to look for in guards.

The road out of the city ran past the cattle market and the rubbish tips where the gulls came inland to feed, and then out into the flat country where the market gardens were, and then down through broken ground to the river. It was hot. The dust went into the throat and stayed there. Twice they passed carts turned over at the side of the road with men standing around them in the particular attitude of men who have decided that the day is finished.

Ostia in the morning smelled of tar and fish and the sweet rot of grain. The harbour was a forest of masts. The Alexandrian ships lay at the far mole, high out of the water, their crews asleep on the decks under awnings; and along the quays the stevedores were unloading the African fleet, which had come in three days before, and which was carrying about two thirds of what it had been contracted to carry, because two of the ships had been lost off Sicily and one had put in at Syracuse with a broken mast and a cargo that had heated in the hold.

Antonia stood at the end of the quay and watched the sacks come off. She counted, because she could not help counting, and the number did not please her any more than the report had. There was grain in Ostia. There was not enough grain in Ostia.

'It is worse than the letter said,' Cinnamus murmured.

'It is worse than the letter said and better than it will be in a month,' said Antonia. 'Where is the warehouse?'

'Beyond the temple of Vulcan, on the north side. It is the one with the new shutters.'

The warehouse of the corn merchant Felix was a long brick building with a tiled roof and a door of oak, and it was shut, and there was a man sitting on the step in front of it with his head in his hands.

He looked up when they came. He was perhaps forty-five, with the ruined complexion of a man who had once been florid and had lately stopped eating, and he was dressed in a tunic that had been expensive.

'The warehouse is closed,' he said.

'I am not here for the warehouse,' said Antonia. 'I am here for the paper.'

Felix got to his feet.

'Who are you?'

'My name is Cassia. I have a house on the Esquiline.' She stood in front of him, and she was shorter than he was and somehow it did not seem so. 'You were fined by the curator of the public works a month ago for building over the pavement, and you have been buying grain on the Alexandrian market for two years with money you did not have, and last week you sold three contracts for delivery in September to a man named Sura who is a client of my banker's, and there is not enough grain in Italy to fill them.'

Felix's face went through several things. The last of them was a kind of relief.

'You are here to ruin me,' he said.

'I am here to buy them.'

'At what price?'

'Sixty per cent of what you paid.'

'That is robbery.'

'That is sixty per cent more than you will get in September, when Sura discovers what you have done and takes you to court, and the prefect of the corn supply discovers it at the same time and decides whether you are a fool or a criminal, and the difference between those two things in this city is about four days.' She let that settle. 'I will also require you to sign a statement that the contracts were sold to me without any guarantee of delivery, and that you told me, at the time of sale, that you were unable to fulfil them.'

'That is a confession.'

'It is an insurance policy. If you run, I will have it. If you lie about me afterwards, I will have it. If you do neither, it will stay in a box in my house for the rest of your life, and you will be free to grow old and be a fool in peace.' She took the tablet Cinnamus held out. 'Read it. Take it to your own advocate if you want. I will be at the inn by the theatre until the ninth hour.'

Felix read it there on the step, with the gulls screaming over the harbour and the stevedores chanting on the quay, and his hands shook.

'Why?' he said. 'You could have waited three weeks and taken everything.'

'Because in three weeks there will be a shortage in this city,' said Antonia, 'and if I wait three weeks I will make a great deal of money out of hungry people and I will be the woman who did it, and this city never forgets a woman who did it. If I buy now, I can deliver, and I can deliver at a price I choose, and I will be the woman who had grain when there was none. That is not virtue. It is arithmetic on a longer term.'

Felix looked at her.

'You are a strange woman,' he said.

'I am a woman who has been poor,' said Antonia. 'There is nothing strange about it. Poor people think about food all the time. It is one of the few advantages.'

Two days after Ostia she went to the office of the prefect of the corn supply, which was in a building behind the Porticus Minucia, and she went at the fourth hour in the morning and she waited in a queue for two hours with forty other people.

The queue was made up of bakers and millers and the agents of shipping families and one man who had come about a boat, and it moved at the speed of a queue of people who are all waiting for a clerk, which is to say that it did not move.

Antonia had brought a stool, a slave with a fan, a tablet, and a book of contracts. She sat in the middle of the queue and read the contracts and marked them with a stylus, and she did it in full view of everyone, and by the end of the second hour three of the men in the queue had asked her what she was reading.

'The grain contracts for September,' she said. 'I am buying them.'

'From whom?'

'From whoever is frightened,' said Antonia, 'which will be eleven men by the end of August, and two of them are in this queue.'

The clerk who took her tablet was a thin, harassed man of forty with ink on his sleeve, and he looked at the contracts and then at her.

'These are contracts for delivery,' he said. 'You are not registered as a corn merchant.'

'I am registered as a creditor of four corn merchants,' said Antonia. 'The contracts are in their names and they have assigned the delivery to me, which is lawful, and I have the assignments here and they are witnessed. I am not asking you for a licence. I am telling you, in a room with a queue in it, that when the shortage comes there will be a woman on the Esquiline who has the paper for four hundred thousand modii, and I would like the prefect's office to have heard of it before it happens and not afterwards.'

The clerk put the tablet down.

'Why?' he said.

'Because in three weeks the price will rise and men will say that someone is hoarding, and the someone will be the woman with the foreign name,' said Antonia. 'I would like there to be a note in a file in this office, in your hand, dated today, that says she came in before it happened and told the office what she was doing. You will not remember me in a year. The file will.'

The clerk looked at her for a long moment. Then he wrote a note.

On the way out she passed a woman of about fifty in a good cloak who was sitting on the bench by the door with a basket on her lap, and the woman said, 'Is it true that you are buying grain?'

'It is true that I have bought paper for grain,' said Antonia. 'It is not the same thing, and in three weeks it will be the only thing about me that anybody remembers correctly.'

'My husband has a mill on the Tiber,' said the woman, 'and he has been buying in a panic since June, and he has borrowed money from his brother to do it, and his brother is a fool and has told the whole street.'

'Then tell your husband to stop buying,' said Antonia, 'and to sell a third of what he has in the last week of August, and to hold the rest.'

The woman stared at her.

'Why would you tell me that?'

'Because a mill on the Tiber will be useful to me in October,' said Antonia, 'and because I have just spent two hours in a queue and I have discovered that I cannot bear a room full of frightened men and no women in it.'

The favour from Vibia Lucana she called in three days later, over dinner in Vibia's house on the Caelian, which was small and full of very good Greek things and had a garden the size of a dining couch, in which Vibia grew nothing but roses and had them cut and sent to people she wished to impress or reproach.

They ate simply: bread, cheese, olives, a fish that had been alive that morning, and a wine that Vibia did not announce the vintage of, which was itself a form of boasting.

'You want me to speak to the prefect's wife,' Vibia said, when the plates had gone.

'Yes.'

'About the grain.'

'About the fact that there will be a shortage, and that when it comes there will be men in the city who say it is the fault of the emperor's officials, and that the prefect will need someone to be known to have been sensible in advance.' Antonia broke a piece of bread. 'His wife does not like me. She will not listen to me. She will listen to you, because your mother was her mother's friend, and because you are respectable and I am not, and because you have never in your life asked her for anything.'

'And you want me to spend that,' said Vibia. 'The one thing I have that is not for sale.'

'Yes.'

Vibia poured more wine. She was forty-five and had buried two husbands and had a laugh that could empty a room or fill it, and she had been, for eleven years, the only person of her class in Rome who came to Antonia's house without pretending that she was doing something else.

'There is a thing I have never understood about you,' she said. 'You could have been respectable. Not at twenty, no. But at thirty, with money, married to some equestrian with a decent name and a bad estate — you could have bought your way into a dinner table and been tolerated, and your daughter would have married a senator's son. Instead you built that house and you filled it with women and you made yourself the one thing this city cannot forgive.'

'What is that?'

'A woman who does not need a man's name to be safe,' said Vibia. 'They can forgive a rich freedwoman. They can forgive a courtesan. They cannot forgive a woman who has a house that men come to, with her own name over the gate. It is not the trade that offends them. It is the sign.'

Antonia turned her cup slowly.

'You are asking me why I did not become respectable,' she said. 'The answer is that I tried. For four years, when I first had money, I tried. I wore the right wool and refused the right invitations and went to the temple of Pudicitia with women who spoke to me and looked at my hands to see whether they were a slave's hands.' She held them up. They were not; they were a rich woman's hands, and the marks of the loom had faded thirty years before. 'And in the fourth year a man at a dinner said something to me, and I answered him, and there was a silence, and then a woman said, very kindly, "She is clever, isn't she," and everyone laughed, and I understood that I could spend the rest of my life being laughed at kindly, or I could build a house where I made the jokes.'

'And now?'

'Now I am laughed at unkindly,' said Antonia, 'by men who need me, in a house I own, and I find that I prefer it.'

Vibia laughed — the real laugh, the one she kept for her own table.

'I will speak to her,' she said. 'And you will owe me.'

'I will owe you.'

'And one day I will ask you for something that is not respectable, and you will not refuse me, because you will remember this fish.'

'What could you possibly want that is not respectable?'

Vibia turned her cup. 'I am forty-five,' she said, 'and I have been a widow for six years, and in this city a widow of forty-five is a piece of furniture that is permitted to talk. I would like, before I am too old, to do one thing that is entirely my own and entirely unwise.' She looked up. 'I do not know what it is yet. When I know, I will come to you, and there will be no discussion of virtue or arithmetic, and you will help me, and neither of us will ever mention it again.'

'Agreed,' said Antonia.

The secret she had kept for more than a decade concerned a fire.

In the third year of the emperor's reign there had been a fire in the ninth region, in a block of tenements off the Via Lata, and nineteen people had died, and the block had belonged to the family of Marcus Sestius Verus, who had been aedile that year and responsible, in a general way, for the city's fire precautions, and whose brother had owned the warehouse next to it.

The tenements had burned. The warehouse had not. It had not burned because the watch had been directed to it first — not by Sestius, but by a man who worked for him, a contractor of the vigiles named Gaius Albinus, who had been paid eleven thousand sesterces to be confused about which building was on fire, and who had come to Antonia nine months later, drunk and terrified, because his son had died and he had begun to think about judgement.

She had written down what he said. She had kept the tablet. She had also kept the name of the woman who had sold Sestius the ground the tenements stood on, which he had bought at a tenth of its value eleven days after the fire.

She had never used it, because a thing like that could only be used once, and because the man who used it would have to be willing to be the sort of man who had it. She had told herself, on the nights when she took the tablet out and looked at it, that she was keeping it for a day when she would need it, and she had known, even then, that the day would come,and that when it came she would find that she had been keeping it not for the day but for the pleasure of knowing it was there.

The day came three weeks after the Ides of May, at the eleventh hour of a warm night, when a young man with his father's jaw and none of his father's sense came to the door of the Golden Vine and asked for the singer.

---

'He is in the atrium,' said Meno, from the doorway of the study, in the voice of a man who had been arguing. 'He has two friends with him and a litter in the street. He says he is the son of the praetor.'

'He is the son of the praetor,' said Antonia. 'Show him into the small room. Fetch Myrrhine and do not fetch anyone else. And Meno — the girl is not in the house. Do you understand me? If he asks for her, the girl is not in the house.'

Meno looked at her for a moment. He had been a doorkeeper for thirty years in four houses and he understood her perfectly.

'A girl is never in the house,' he said, and went.

Lucius Sestius Cirrus was twenty-two. He had his father's breadth and his mother's money and the particular confidence of a young man who has never been refused by anyone except his father. He was drunk in the way of a man who has been drinking since the afternoon and has reached the stage of absolute clarity.

He bowed, badly.

'Cassia.'

'Cirrus.'

'I have come about the singer.'

'There are eleven women in this house and several of them sing. You will have to be specific.'

'The little one. The Greek. Lucilla.' He smiled. 'You know the one. My father heard her at your dinner in May. He said she had a voice like a knife in a drawer. He has talked about it for a month, which for my father is a kind of passion.'

'Your father has excellent taste,' said Antonia.

'He has. He also has a wife, and a career, and a reputation for severity that he is very careful of, and so he will not do anything about it.' Cirrus sat down without being asked. 'I have none of those things. So I am here.'

'To do what?'

'To buy her evening.'

'She is not for sale.'

'Everyone is for sale. My father says so. My father says the only question is the price and whether the seller is honest about it.' He leaned forward. 'I will give you twenty thousand sesterces for one night. Not for the house. For me. She can keep nothing; I'll give the money to you, and you can give her whatever you think is proper, and the whole of Rome will say that Antonia Cassia got twenty thousand for one girl's night, and there are men who will come to you next week with better offers, and you will be richer, and she will have a story to dine out on for the rest of her life.'

Antonia sat down opposite him and folded her hands.

'You have thought about this,' she said.

'I have thought about it all week.'

'And what will you do when she says no?'

'She will not say no. She is a slave.'

'She will say no,' said Antonia, 'because I will tell her to, and because I will be in the room, and because — this is the part that you have not thought about, Cirrus — because she is seventeen and you are a man who has never had to ask twice for anything, and the thing you want is not a night with a singer. The thing you want is to be refused and then to win, and I am not going to give you that, because the last man I gave it to is now a magistrate who cannot walk through the Forum without being laughed at behind his back.'

Cirrus's face changed. He was not stupid, and it was the worst thing about him.

'You are threatening me,' he said.

'I am telling you that you are in my house at the eleventh hour, drunk, with two friends in my atrium, asking for a girl who is not here, and that if you go home now, in the morning this will be a thing you did when you were young.' Antonia stood. 'If you do not go home, it will be a thing that happened to you, and you will not enjoy it, and neither will your father, and your father will know that it was your fault, which he will not forgive.'

For a moment she thought he would hit her. His hand moved on the arm of the chair. Then he stood up, and straightened his tunic, and smiled the smile of his father, which was worse than the anger.

'My father has been looking for a reason to close this house for eleven years,' he said. 'He has the ear of the urban prefect and the prefect of the corn supply and half the Senate. He only lacks a complainant.' He went to the door. 'You have made one tonight. Remember that when the seal goes on your door, Cassia. I want you to remember that it was not the great man on the Palatine who did it. It was the boy you would not sell a girl to.'

He went out. The street door closed. In the atrium his friends laughed at something, and then the litter was lifted, and the sound of it went away down the hill.

Myrrhine came in with her arms folded.

'Well,' she said. 'That was foolish.'

'It was correct.'

'It was correct and foolish and they are not exclusive.' Myrrhine sat on the edge of the table, which she was the only person in the world permitted to do. 'He will go to his father. His father will go to the prefect. In September there will be an inquiry into the morals of this house, and you will be required to answer, and you will need an advocate who is not frightened of the Sestii, and there is no such advocate in Rome except the men who are already Sestius's friends.'

Antonia went to the window. The street was empty and the lamps at the corner had been lit by the watch. Somewhere beyond the wall a woman was singing, very badly, the same song Lucilla had sung at the dinner.

'There is one,' she said.

Myrrhine looked at her.

'No,' she said.

'He is the best jurist in the city. He was consul. He has never lost a case he undertook, and he has undertaken cases against men with better names than Sestius. He hates me, and he would rather see me ruined than saved, and he is the only man in Rome who could stand up in the basilica and say that Antonia Cassia is innocent of a charge and be believed, because everyone knows what he thinks of me and everyone would know that he was saying it against his own inclination.'

'No,' said Myrrhine again.

'Not yet,' said Antonia. 'But by the winter, if it comes to it, I will go to him and I will ask him, and he will refuse, and I will make him do it anyway.'

'How?'

Antonia turned from the window.

'Because twenty-four years ago his father's house did something that his mother has never forgiven him for,' she said, 'and I know exactly what it was, and I have never once in twenty-four years used it. That is what I have instead of a name. It is not much. But it is mine.'
