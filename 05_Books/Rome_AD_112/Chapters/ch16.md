# 16 | The Prosecution

The court sat in the Basilica Julia, in the great hall on the south side, and by the second hour of the morning there were three thousand people in it and around it.

The hall was a hundred and one paces long and half as wide, with a floor of marble in a pattern of squares and two rows of pillars down the sides and a roof so high that the noise of the crowd went up into it and came back down as a low roar. The judges sat on a raised platform at the eastern end: twenty-two men, all of consular or praetorian rank, in their broad stripes, and behind them the presiding magistrate's chair and the standards of the court. The advocates had a space in front of the platform with two tables and a rail. The witnesses would stand in the space between the rail and the platform, in full view of everybody, which was the point.

The rest of the hall was the city.

Antonia came in at the second hour through the crowd at the western door, and the sound changed as she came: not a roar, but a kind of hiss and shift, thousands of people turning to look at one woman in a dark blue dress with her hair pinned up and a bundle of tablets under her arm.

'Do not look at them,' said Cinnamus, at her shoulder. 'Do not look at anyone. Look at the platform, walk to the table, stand still, and put the bundle down.'

'I have been in courts before.'

'Not like this.'

He was right. She had been in courts four times in eleven years, in the matter of a debt, and once as a witness in a will case, and each time she had been one of eleven people in a small room with a magistrate's clerk and a bad smell. This was a different animal entirely. This was the city, in a hall, arranged in rows, in which every man who had ever come to her house and paid her money was now sitting and looking at her with an expression that said: *whatever happens to her, it was not me.*

She walked to the table and put the bundle down and stood still.

Tarquitius was already there, with two juniors and a freedman clerk, and he was in the plain white toga of a man who does not need to be noticed, and he did not look at her. On the other side of the rail, at the prosecution's table, Marcus Sestius Verus was arranging his tablets with great care, and beside him were two men: a young orator named Scaevola, who had made his name that year by prosecuting a provincial governor, and a thin, grey, careful man of sixty who was the family's jurist, and who had drafted the charge.

In the front row of the spectators, on the left, there was a woman in a green dress: Vibia Lucana, with her hands folded and her face quite still.

In the second row, behind the prosecution's table, in a plain grey dress, there was a woman of nineteen who had no business being there at all, and who had come anyway, and who was sitting with her hands in her lap and her chin up: Marcellina.

In the gallery above, where the clerks and the copyists and the advocates' slaves were, Tertia Vipsania was sitting with a stylus behind her ear and her box of tablets on her knee.

---

The charge was read at the third hour.

It took a quarter of an hour. It alleged that Antonia Cassia, freedwoman, of the fifth region, had on the nineteenth day of August caused to be administered to Marcus Sestius Cirrus, a Roman citizen of the senatorial order, a noxious substance; that the same Marcus Sestius Cirrus had died thereof in her house on the following morning; that she had, with malice, secreted the woman who had been present at the death, in order to prevent examination; that her house was a place of habitual vice and the resort of men of rank who were there plied with wine and separated from their fortunes; and that by these acts she had offended against the peace of the city and the dignity of the Roman people.

There were eleven clauses, and by the seventh the crowd had begun to mutter, and by the eleventh it had gone quiet.

Then Sestius rose.

He was fifty-eight and he had been a pleader for thirty years, and he had something that Antonia had not expected and that she should have expected: he was good. He did not shout. He began with his son.

He began with a boy of six learning to ride in the fields at his mother's house at Tusculum; with a boy of twelve who had been beaten for lying and had come to his father afterwards to say that the beating had been just; with a boy of seventeen who had gone to the Danube with the army and had written home eleven letters, and he read out two of them, and they were ordinary and young and full of weather and horses and the price of wine, and by the end of them there were men in the second row who were crying, and the whole vast hall had gone still.

*You will hear,* said Sestius, *that my son died of his own foolishness. You will hear that he bought a box in the Forum from a man with a monkey. I do not deny that a box was bought. I say to you: where does a boy of twenty-two buy a box of Spanish fly? In the Forum, from a man with a monkey, in the afternoon, in the sight of the whole city, because for twenty years the whole city has been selling such things, in shops, in taverns, in houses with painted doors and fountains in the courtyard, and we have said nothing, because the men who go into those houses are men of rank, and we have not wished to know.*

*And when the boy is dead, and his father goes to that house at the ninth hour, what does he find? He finds a woman who has had the body laid out for him, with a lamp at the head, and who has already sent the woman who was in the room away in a closed carriage before dawn. He finds a physician who has been in her pay for eleven years. He finds a household of nineteen people, every one of whom is hers, body and soul, who have been rehearsed. And he finds that he is a praetor of Rome, and that he cannot do anything at all, because the law requires him to bring a charge and to prove it, and the woman has been in this city for more than a decade and knows precisely what can be proved.*

*So he brings the charge. And I say to you, judges, that when you have heard it, you will not be asked to decide whether my son was a fool. He was twenty-two years old and he was drunk and he was a fool, and I will not have him defended by lies. He is dead, and he will be dead whatever you decide, and I have buried him in the ground outside the Colline gate in a stone that I paid for, and I do not want revenge.*

*What I want is for this court to say, once, in public, in the sight of the city, that a house in which a Roman citizen can die in the night and have his death arranged by a woman before breakfast is not a lawful business, whatever its accounts may say.*

He sat down.

The hall was silent for the length of about ten heartbeats, and then the noise came up, and the presiding magistrate had to call for order twice.

'He is better than I thought,' said Tarquitius, without turning his head. 'He will not win on the law. He is not trying to win on the law.'

'What is he trying to win on?'

'The gallery,' said Tarquitius. 'Look at the fourth row. There are eleven men there who have been in your house and who are now afraid that they will be named. They will not vote to convict you. But they will vote to close the house, and they will call it a compromise, and they will go home and tell their wives that they have done justice.' He turned a tablet over. 'The woman. Berenice. Where is she?'

'At a farm above Tibur.'

'She will be produced,' said Tarquitius, 'this afternoon. Sestius has had men in the valley for a month and he has not produced her because he wanted to produce her here, in front of the city, after that speech, when the hall is full and everyone has been crying. When she comes in, she will be frightened, and she will contradict herself, and it will be the best thing that happens to us all day, and it will feel like the worst thing in the world.'

---

At the fourth hour the court broke for a quarter of an hour, and Antonia was taken by her own people into the portico at the back of the hall, where the advocates' clients waited, and given water, and told by Cinnamus that she had not eaten since the previous evening and that she would eat now or he would have her carried out.

She ate bread. She could not taste it.

The portico was full of people who wanted to be seen to be near her without being seen to speak to her. Two men of equestrian rank who had been to her house eleven times between them walked past within three feet and looked at the wall. A woman in a great deal of jewellery came up and took her hand and said, 'My dear, you are magnificent, and the whole city is with you,' and was gone before Antonia could reply, and Vibia Lucana, standing at her elbow, said:

'That was Aufidia Severa's cousin. She has never spoken to you in her life and she will not speak to you in a year's time if you are convicted.' She took the bread out of Antonia's hand and ate a piece of it herself. 'How are you?'

'I am watching the judges.'

'They are watching you.'

'The fourth man on the left and the seventh on the right are with me,' said Antonia. 'The fourth because I lent his brother money in the sixth year and never asked for it back. The seventh because he came to my house nine times and he is terrified that I will remember. The presiding magistrate is against me; he is a friend of Sestius's father and he has decided that the case is a scandal that must be closed quickly, whichever way. And the old man at the end of the second row, the one who has not looked at anything but his own hands since the third hour —'

'Yes?'

'He is the one who will decide,' said Antonia. 'He is not afraid of me and he owes me nothing and he has not enjoyed one minute of this morning. He is the only man on that platform who is thinking about the law.'

'And that is good?'

'That is very good,' said Antonia. 'The law is on my side. It is the men who are not.'

Vibia was quiet for a moment.

'Antonia,' she said. 'The thing I told you I would ask for one day. The unwise thing.'

'Not now.'

'No,' said Vibia. 'Not now. But I have decided what it is, and I am telling you so that you have something to think about in there besides the boy.' She folded her arms. 'When it is over — whichever way it goes — I am going to leave Rome. I am going to Ostia, and I am going to buy a house with a courtyard, and I am going to keep the shipping office open, and I am going to be a widow of forty-five who is not in anybody's family and does not go to anybody's dinners. And I want you to come and eat with me once a month, and I want you to bring the women who have nowhere to go, and I want us to be two old women in a courtyard who are not respectable and do not care.' She looked at Antonia. 'That is my unwise thing. I have been thinking about it since August.'

'Why is that unwise?'

'Because in this city a woman alone is a scandal and a woman alone with a house full of women is a brothel,' said Vibia, 'and I have been respectable all my life, and I am going to give it up at forty-five for a courtyard in Ostia and the company of a woman who has been tried for murder.' She smiled. 'Tell me in three days. Go back in there and be dull and truthful and make them ask the same question three times.'

---

The witnesses began at the fifth hour.

The praetor's physician was first. He was a thin, prosperous man named Hermes, with a Greek manner and a bad memory, and he testified that he had been called to the house at the seventh hour of the morning and had found the deceased already cold, that the countenance was congested, the tongue swollen, the pupils wide, and that in his opinion — his considered opinion, formed over thirty years — the deceased had died of a vegetable poison administered in wine.

'Which vegetable?' said Tarquitius.

'I beg your pardon?'

'You have said a vegetable poison. I am asking which vegetable.'

'I am not a cook,' said Hermes. 'There are a great many substances.'

'Name one.'

'Henbane,' said Hermes. 'Hemlock. The juice of the poppy in quantity. There are a great many.'

'Have you ever seen a man die of henbane?'

'I have seen men die of a great many things.'

'Have you ever seen a man die of Spanish fly?'

The physician was silent.

'Take your time,' said Tarquitius. 'You have thirty years.'

'I have not,' said Hermes.

'You have not seen a man die of Spanish fly. Have you read Galen on the subject?'

'I have read Galen.'

'Then you will recall that Galen describes the symptoms of cantharides poisoning as congestion of the face, swelling of the tongue, dilatation of the pupils, and death by convulsion within twelve hours of ingestion, which is precisely the description you have just given this court of a vegetable poison.' Tarquitius did not raise his voice. 'You will also recall that Galen notes that the same symptoms follow a blow to the head, a seizure, and a great many fevers, and that a physician who has not examined the stomach cannot tell one from another. Did you examine the stomach?'

'It was not permitted. The body had been taken by the family.'

'The body had been taken by the family,' said Tarquitius, 'at the request of the praetor, who is the prosecutor, three hours after the death, and before any magistrate had been informed. So we have a physician who did not examine the stomach, giving an opinion of poison, on a body removed by the prosecutor's household, in a case brought by the prosecutor, and I am asked to believe that this is an inquiry into the facts.' He turned to the judges. 'I have no more questions for this witness.'

The second witness was Meno.

He came up the hall in his good tunic with his stick, sixty-eight years old, a Thracian, with a scar through one eyebrow, and he stood in front of twenty-two consulars without any appearance of being impressed by them.

He testified to the hour of Cirrus's arrival, the condition he was in, the two friends, the litter, the four bearers, the fact that one of the friends had been sick in the street. He testified that the young man had been brought into the atrium and had sat down on the floor. He testified that he had been carried to the small room and laid on the couch, that a man had been placed at the door, that the slave of the deceased had chosen to stay the night, and that the door had been open.

'Was the door open all night?' said Scaevola, for the prosecution.

'It was open when I looked at the second hour and it was open when I looked at the fourth.'

'And who was in the room?'

'Prima, of this house, was in the room at the second hour. The woman Berenice came in at some point after that; I did not see her come, because I was at the gate. The physician came at the fifth hour and the master's son was dead at the sixth.'

'Did you see the woman Berenice leave the house?'

'I did not.'

'Did you see a carriage?'

'I saw a carriage at the garden door at the first hour, and I was told it was for the physician's sister, and I did not ask, because I am a doorkeeper and not a magistrate.'

There was laughter in the gallery, and the magistrate called for order, and Scaevola said, 'The doorkeeper of this house is a witty man,' and Meno said, 'I am an old man who has been at a gate for nine years and has learned that the less he asks the longer he lives,' and there was more laughter, and Antonia sat at the table with her hands folded and thought: he is doing it for me, he is making them like him, and he has no idea what he is doing and it is working.

The third witness was Prima.

She came up the hall in a plain dress with her hair pinned and her hands at her sides and she did not look at Antonia at all. She gave her name, her age, her condition — a freedwoman of this house, freed in the eighth year of the reign — and her occupation, which she gave as *of this household*, and when Scaevola pressed her she said, 'I am a woman of this house. You know what that means. I will not use a prettier word for your convenience.'

'Very well. On the night in question, were you in the room with the deceased?'

'I was. From the second hour until the fourth.'

'What did you see?'

'I saw a young man who had drunk too much and taken something he had bought in the Forum, and who was sick twice, and who could not breathe properly at the fourth hour, and who told me to go away and let him sleep.'

'Did he take anything while you were in the room?'

'He took a second dose from a bronze box. I told him not to. He told me to mind my own business and he took it.'

'Did anyone in this house give him anything?'

'No.'

'Did the accused give him anything?'

'No. She was not in the room. She came to the door at the first hour and looked in and went away.'

'And the woman Berenice?'

'She came in at the fourth hour, when I went to get water, and I did not go back, because I was tired, and because he had told me to go away, and because I had been in that room for two hours with a man who was not himself.' Prima's voice did not change. 'I sat on the bench in the passage until the fifth hour. I heard him breathing. And then I heard him stop.'

Scaevola looked at her for a moment.

'You are a freedwoman of the accused,' he said. 'You are paid wages by her. You are, in effect, her creature. Why should this court believe you?'

'You should not,' said Prima. 'You should believe the box, and the physician who says it was Spanish fly, and the shop in the Forum that sold it, and the two friends who will tell you that he bought it that afternoon and showed it to them in the street.' She turned her head and looked at Sestius for the first time. 'What you should not believe is that a boy of twenty-two can die of his own foolishness in a city that has spent twenty years selling him the foolishness and taking his money for it, and that the fault must belong to a woman with a house because it cannot belong to the men who built the market.'

There was a silence.

'You may step down,' said the magistrate.

'I have not finished,' said Prima.

'You have finished,' said Tarquitius, quietly, and she looked at him, and something passed between them, and she stepped down.

---

They produced Berenice at the seventh hour, when the light was going and the lamps had been lit along the platform, and the hall was so full that men were standing on the bases of the pillars.

She came in through the western door between two of the praetor's men, and she was in a plain dress with her hair down and her hands bound in front of her, which was not necessary and which was done to be seen, and she was so frightened that she could not walk without being held.

Antonia stood up.

'Sit down,' said Tarquitius.

'They have bound her—'

'Sit down,' said Tarquitius, 'and look at the judges, and do not look at the girl, and let me do this.'

The presiding magistrate looked at the bound hands and said, 'The witness will be unbound. This is a court of the Roman people and not a market.'

They unbound her. She stood in the space between the rail and the platform with her hands at her sides and her whole body shaking, and Scaevola rose and said, 'Your name?'

'Berenice.'

'Your condition?'

'I am a slave of the household of Antonia Cassia.'

'Were you in the room with Marcus Sestius Cirrus on the night he died?'

'Yes.'

'Did you give him anything to drink?'

'Water.'

'Did you give him anything else?'

'No.'

'Did anyone in that house give him anything?'

'I do not know. I was not there.'

'Where were you?'

'I was in the room over the kitchen until the fourth hour,' said Berenice. 'And then I came down because the girl Chloe told me that he had asked for me.'

'He asked for you?'

'He asked for the singer,' said Berenice, 'and the singer was not there, and I went because I was the one who was there.'

There was a stir in the gallery.

'And what happened when you went in?'

'He was —' Berenice stopped. Her hands were shaking. 'He was like a man in a fever. He had the box in his hand and he took more of it and he tried to — he wanted — and then he could not breathe, and I got water, and I put it on his face, and he was like that for an hour, and then he stopped.' She was crying now, and she said it as though she had said it a hundred times in her head. 'I did not kill him. I did not even — he could not. He was like that and then he stopped and I thought he was asleep.'

'And then?'

'And then it was morning,' said Berenice, 'and the physician came, and the domina told me to go to Tibur in the carriage, and I went.'

'Why did she send you away?'

'Because she said that if the praetor's household found me in the house they would put me in a cell and ask me for three days what I had done to their master's son, and that a slave's truth in a cell is not evidence.' Berenice wiped her face with her wrist. 'She said I had done nothing wrong and that was why I had to go.'

The hall was silent.

Scaevola looked at her for a long moment, and then he said, 'Thank you. I have no further questions.'

And Tarquitius rose.

'Berenice,' he said. 'You have been at a farm above Tibur for eleven weeks. Who found you there?'

'The praetor's men,' said Berenice.

'How many times did they question you?'

'Four times.'

'Where?'

'In the barn,' said Berenice. 'They took me out to the barn and they asked me.'

'Did they touch you?'

She hesitated.

'Did they touch you?'

'One of them put his hand on my throat,' said Berenice, 'and said that if I did not say what they wanted he would have me sold to the mines, and that no one would ask where a slave of Antonia Cassia had gone.'

The hall came apart.

It took the magistrate a quarter of an hour to restore order, and by the time he had done it the light had gone entirely and the lamps were the only light in the hall, and the whole of the city's opinion had turned around like a weathercock, and Sestius was on his feet, and his jurist was pulling at his sleeve, and Scaevola was standing with his hands spread, saying something that nobody could hear.

'That is enough for today,' said the presiding magistrate. 'The court will adjourn until the third day from tomorrow, and the witness Berenice will be lodged in the custody of the court and not of the prosecutor, and any man who speaks to her before the court sits again will answer to me.' He looked at the hall. 'The court will sit again on the day after the games. Until then, the city will keep the peace, and I will hear no more of this case in the streets, and any man who brings it up at dinner will find that I have a long memory and a great deal of leisure.'

---

They walked out into the dark with the crowd pressing and the torches smoking, and Cinnamus had his hand under Antonia's elbow, and Brennus was in front of them with his stick, and behind them the hall was emptying slowly into the Forum.

'We have won the day,' said Tarquitius, at her shoulder. 'We have not won the case. But we have won the day.'

'They hurt her,' said Antonia.

'Yes.'

'They put their hands on her throat in a barn above Tibur, and she is nineteen, and she told them what they wanted because she is a slave and she has been told all her life that a slave's truth is not evidence, and she has been sitting in a farm kitchen for eleven weeks waiting to be found.' Antonia stopped in the middle of the crowd, and people went around her. 'And I sent her there. I put her in a carriage and I sent her to Tibur because I thought it was mercy, and it was mercy for a week, and then it was a trap, and the trap was mine.'

Tarquitius looked at her in the torchlight.

'Yes,' he said. 'It was yours. And if you had kept her in the house, they would have taken her from the house, which is worse, and if you had sent her further they would have found her anyway, because a slave has no papers and no name and no place to hide in Italy.' He took her arm, which he had never done before, and turned her toward the street. 'You did the best thing available to a woman with no power in a city with no law for her, and it failed in one particular, and she is alive, and tomorrow she will be alive, and in three days the court will sit again and I will make Scaevola explain to twenty-two consulars why his witnesses are in the habit of putting their hands on the throats of girls in barns.' He let go of her arm. 'Go home. Sleep. And Antonia — do not go to the girl tonight. She is in the court's custody and if you are seen with her you will have undone the only good thing that happened today.'

She went home.

At the gate, Meno took her cloak and said, 'There is a woman in the garden, domina. She came at the eighth hour with a box and she says she is the daughter of the house of Tarquitius and that you told her she might come in three days and that she has decided not to wait.'

Antonia stood in her own atrium.

'And there is something else,' said Meno. 'The house on the corner of the Vicus Cyprius — the baker's, the one with the bad roof. It is on fire, domina. It started at the ninth hour and the watch are there, and the wind is from the north.'

---

The fire took the baker's shop and the two tenements behind it and the roof of the wineshop on the corner, and it did not take the Golden Vine, because the wind turned at the second hour of the night and because Brennus and Meno and every man in the house had been on the roof for three hours with wet cloths and buckets, beating out the sparks as they came over.

Antonia stood in the street with her household around her.

It was the first time she had ever seen the whole of the house from the outside at night, and it looked smaller than she had imagined, and more beautiful: the lamps in the peristyle, the fig tree over the wall, the dark cypress at the corner of the garden, and over all of it the orange light from the burning block and the sparks going up and across like a swarm of insects.

The whole street was out. The neighbours were out with their own buckets and their own families; the woman who sold chickpeas was sitting on a chest in the middle of the road with her cat in her lap, weeping; the vigiles had formed a chain from the fountain and were passing buckets hand to hand, and one of them was shouting at a man in a senator's tunic to get out of the way, and the man in the senator's tunic was shouting back.

'Three houses,' said Cinnamus, at her elbow. 'Four. It will not cross the road; there is a gap.'

'Are they all out?'

'All out. There is an old man on a mattress in the road and a woman with a baby, and the wineshop keeper has lost everything and is standing in the street with a jug in his hand, which is the only thing he saved.'

Antonia looked at the fire.

'Go and tell the vigiles that there is water in my cistern and that they may have all of it, and that there is wine for the men, and bread, and that they are to come into the courtyard and take what they need and not ask.' She turned. 'And Cinnamus. When it is out, find the wineshop keeper and the baker and the woman with the baby and bring them to me. Not tomorrow. Tonight. They will need a room and a floor and something hot, and there are eleven empty rooms in this house because women have been going home to their mothers and their sisters all month, and it will be a great deal easier for me to be tried for keeping a disorderly house if the house has a baker's family in it.'

Cinnamus wrote it down.

'Domina,' he said, 'there is a woman in the garden.'

'I know.'

'She has been there for two hours. She came out into the street when the fire started and she has been helping with the buckets; she was on the roof with Brennus at the first hour in her good dress.' He paused. 'She is nineteen years old and she has nowhere to go, and her father will be at the court tomorrow and will see her, and the whole city will know by the afternoon that the daughter of Tarquitius Priscus is living in a brothel.'

'Then I will have to be acquitted,' said Antonia, 'so that she is living in a house.'

She went in through the garden door and across the wet courtyard, where the household was coming back in ones and twos with their clothes black with smoke, and through the peristyle, and into the small room off the library where a girl with a box of clothes was sitting on a stool with her hands in her lap.

Marcellina's face and hands were black with soot. She had lost one of her sandals. She stood up when Antonia came in.

'I could not wait three days,' she said.

'I know.'

'I have left a letter for my father on the table in his study, and it says that I have refused the betrothal and that I am not coming back, and that he may keep the dowry because I will not need it.' Her chin was up and her hands were shaking. 'And I have been on your roof with a bucket for two hours, and I have never done anything with my hands in my life, and I found that I was good at it.'

Antonia looked at her for a long moment.

'You will sleep in the room at the top of the west wing,' she said. 'It has no window and a good lock. In the morning you will wash, and I will find you a dress that is not a senator's daughter's dress, and you will come to the court with me and sit in the gallery with the copyists, and you will watch, and you will say nothing, and when it is over you and I are going to have a conversation about what a woman of nineteen with no dowry and a great name can do in this city.' She turned to go. 'And Marcellina. The letter to your father. Did you sign it?'

'Yes.'

'Then he knows,' said Antonia. 'And a man who knows is a man who will act, and by the morning he will have decided what to do about it, and whatever he decides, it will be done in public, because he is that kind of man.' She stopped in the doorway. 'We have three days until the court sits again and the whole of the city is watching. Try to sleep. I will not be able to.'
