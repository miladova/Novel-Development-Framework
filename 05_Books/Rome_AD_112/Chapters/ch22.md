# 22 | The Sealing of the Doors

March came in with wind and the whole city began to move.

It was the month when leases were renewed and ships were prepared and the roads filled with people going back to their estates, and in the Golden Vine it was the month of the inventory. Cinnamus did it room by room, in the Roman way, with a clerk and a tablet, and it took eleven days, and Antonia went with him for the whole of it, because she had never in eleven years walked through her own house and looked at everything in it.

They began in the cellars, which were four rooms under the west wing, and where there was wine enough to fill a cart and a great deal of the kind of thing that accumulates in a cellar over eleven years: broken couches, a crate of lamps with no wicks, a chest of theatrical masks from the dinner in the fourth year, three hundred clay cups, and a box of the bones of a bird that had got in and died and been forgotten.

'Value?' said Antonia.

'Nothing,' said Cinnamus. 'Eleven sesterces for the cups if we sell them as a lot, and the masks are worth four hundred because they are good and a theatre will buy them, and everything else goes to the rubbish tip at the Esquiline gate.'

The dining rooms: seven couches, the citrus-wood table, the silver, the forty lamps of the zodiac room, the wall paintings which were worth nothing because they could not be moved and were going to be covered in the plaster of an aqueduct.

The bedchambers: eleven of them, and each one had been emptied by the woman who had lived in it, and each one was a different shape of emptiness. Eucharis's room still had the mark of a harp case in the dust on the shelf. Chloe's had a nail in the wall with nothing hanging from it.

The small room by the garden: the couch, the basin, the lamp, the mark on the plaster where a young man's head had rested on the nineteenth night of August.

Antonia stood in it for a while.

'Do you want it cleared?' said Cinnamus.

'It is cleared,' said Antonia. 'There is nothing in it.'

The library: eleven presses, four hundred rolls, a great many of which she had never opened. She gave the law books to Tarquitius's secretary, who came with a cart and a list and took them away with the expression of a man being handed a fortune. She gave the poetry and the histories to the household. She kept eleven rolls: Livy, the first decade, which she had read in a linen room in Arretium, and which had a mouse-bitten edge; a Greek poet, and the scrap of wood with the word *eleutheria* on it, which she had kept for twenty-five years and which was, she discovered, still in the box with the blue glass.

The peristyle: the fountain, the Neptune with his hand up against a wave that would never fall, the mosaic that Sabina said had water under it.

The garden: the fig tree, the cypress, the bench, the wall, the corner where the ladder had stood in November.

---

In the second week a man came to the gate at the ninth hour and asked for the house.

He was about forty, in a good tunic, and he had the look of a man who had been drinking since the afternoon in a careful, controlled way and had come to do something he had been meaning to do for a month. Meno, who had been at the gate for nine years and could tell in three words what a man wanted, came and told Antonia.

'He says he has an appointment.'

'He does not have an appointment.'

'He says he was here in the seventh year and that you will remember him.' Meno paused. 'He says he wants to see the room.'

Antonia went to the gate.

The man was standing in the street with his hands behind his back, and he was not one of the men who had come to the house for eleven years; he was one of the ones who had come once, and had never come back, and had thought about it ever since. She remembered him. He had been in the house in the seventh year, in the winter, and he had been unable to do anything at all and had cried, and she had sat with him for an hour and had not charged him for it, and he had sent her a jar of wine at the Saturnalia for four years afterwards and then stopped.

'Domine,' she said.

'You remember me.'

'I remember everyone,' said Antonia. 'It is a professional defect.'

He looked at the street and then at the gate.

'I heard that you are closing it,' he said. 'I heard it in the baths. And I have been thinking about it for a fortnight, and I have come to ask you —' He stopped. 'I have come to ask whether I might see the room. Not for anything. I am not that man any more; I have a wife and a business and two children. I wanted to see the room and stand in it for a minute, and then I will go home.'

Antonia looked at him for a long moment.

'The furniture is sold,' she said. 'The couch has gone. There is nothing in it but a mark on the plaster.'

'That is all right,' he said. 'I would not know what to do with a couch.'

She took him through the house herself. He stopped in the peristyle and looked at the fountain, and in the small room by the garden he stood for a long time with his hands at his sides and did not say anything at all.

Then he said, 'I have thought about that hour for six years. I have thought about it at night, and in the middle of the day, and once in a temple, which was the worst of it. And I have decided — I decided in the street, on the way up the hill — that what happened was not what I thought it was.'

'What did you think it was?'

'I thought I had been humiliated,' he said, 'by a woman I had paid.' He turned around. 'And on the way up the hill I understood that I had been in a room with a stranger for an hour and that she had not laughed at me, and that I had been so frightened of being laughed at that I could not see it, and that I have spent six years being ashamed of the wrong thing.'

Antonia stood in the doorway.

'It is a very small thing,' she said, 'to sit with a man for an hour and not laugh at him.'

'It is,' he said. 'And in six years, nobody else has done it.' He looked around the room one last time. 'I am not going to come back. I am not going to be one of those men who come to the gate when they are fifty. I wanted to see it once more and now I have.' He held out his hand, which nobody did, and she took it. 'Goodbye, domina. I hope you have a good life. I hope you have a very dull one.'

'I am planning on it,' said Antonia.

---

On the eleventh day of the inventory she sent for the painter.

He was sixty-one and lived in a room in the fourth region and had not painted a ceiling in nine years, because his hands shook, and he came up the hill in his working clothes with his box, and he stood in the zodiac room with his cap in his hands and looked up.

'It has held,' he said.

'It has held.'

'The lapis has gone a little green at the edge of the Archer, which I told you at the time; the pigment was not what the dealer said it was.' He turned around slowly. 'I have thought about this ceiling for more than a decade. I have painted four ceilings since and none of them was any good, and I have thought about this one every day, and I have never seen it from the floor with the lamps lit because I was always on my back in the dark.'

'It is going to be plastered over in a month,' said Antonia. 'There is an aqueduct coming through the north slope and the workmen will be in this room in April and the ceiling will come down with the roof.'

The painter looked up at his own work.

'Then I would like to ask you something,' he said, 'and you may say no, and I will not be offended, and it is not about money.'

'Ask.'

'When I painted the Ram, in the fourth month, I put a small thing in the fleece. It is about the size of a fingernail and it is not in the design and nobody has ever noticed it and I have never told anyone. It is my name, in Greek, in three letters, worked into the curl of the fleece, and it cannot be seen from the floor. I did it because I was on my back in the dark for nine months and I wanted to leave a mark and I was afraid to ask.' He turned. 'I am not asking you to save it. You cannot save it; it is going to come down with the roof. I am asking you to go and look at it before it does.'

Antonia looked at him.

'Show me,' she said.

They went up on the scaffolding that the workmen had put up the week before, and she stood on a plank four feet under her own ceiling with her hand against the plaster, and he pointed, and it was there: three small letters in the curl of the Ram's fleece, worked into the pattern so that they became part of it, so that in eleven years not one of the four hundred men who had dined in that room had ever seen it.

'I am sorry I said no,' said Antonia.

'I have been angry about it for eleven years,' said the painter. 'And then in the summer I heard that you were being tried for murder and that the whole city was against you, and I found that I was not angry any more, and I could not work out why.' He put his cap on. 'I think it is because I understood, in the summer, that a woman who says no to a painter is not the same as a woman who has no reason for saying no. You had a reason. It was a bad reason. But it was a reason, and I have been in this city for sixty-one years and I have worked for eleven men who did not have one.'

---

The deeds were signed in the last week of the month, in the temple of Castor, in front of a magistrate's clerk and nine witnesses.

They signed them in one sitting and it took four hours. Eucharis signed her contract and her lease. The two cooks signed their share in the cookshop. The laundry woman signed her passage and a receipt for the sum. Chloe signed for the fuller's business at Ostia and could not stop crying. Prima signed for the house on the Aventine and the two hundred thousand sesterces and did it without reading it, because she had read it eleven times at home and knew it by heart.

Meno signed with a mark, because he could not write, and the clerk made a note of it, and Meno said, 'I have been in four houses in thirty years and this is the first time anyone has ever asked me to sign anything, and I find that I would rather have the cottage than the ceremony.'

Cinnamus signed last, and the deed was for eleven thousand sesterces and a shop in the Subura, and he had negotiated it himself and had driven a hard bargain with Antonia, and had told her, at the end of the negotiation, that she was a very difficult woman and that he would not have worked for anyone else.

Then Antonia signed the sale of the house.

The buyer was the treasury, and the price was the valuation, and the clerk read the schedule aloud in the flat voice of a man reading a schedule, and when it was done Antonia sat with the stylus in her hand for a moment before she signed.

'Domina?' said the clerk.

'One moment,' said Antonia.

She looked at the tablet. It said: *the house, the land, the portico, the fountain, the mosaic, the wall paintings, the cypress, and the fig tree.*

She signed it.

---

The last night in the house was the last night of March, and there were nine people in it.

They had sold the furniture. They had sold the silver, which had gone to a dealer on the Via Sacra for a sum that Cinnamus considered scandalous and that Antonia considered fair, because the man had to sell it on and there was a great deal of it and everybody in Rome knew where it had come from. They had sold the couches and the lamps and the wine and the masks.

What was left in the house was a table, four stools, nine beds, the plates and cups they were using, and a chest in the hall with the household's own things in it.

Vibia Lucana came up in the afternoon with two baskets of food and stayed. Marcellina came back from her grandmother's house, where she had been sitting for a week, and did not go home again. Tertia came at the ninth hour with Sabina and a jar of wine and said, 'I am not staying the night; I have work in the morning and I am not going to be sentimental in a house I have only known for five months.'

The nine were: Antonia, Myrrhine, Marcellina, Prima, Meno, Cinnamus, Vibia, Tertia, and Faustus the new guard, who had been in the household for four months and who stood in the corner with his plate and looked as though he could not believe his luck.

They ate at the long table in the kitchen court, which was the only room with a fire, and they ate the last of the kitchen's food and drank the last of the ordinary wine, and then, because there was nothing else to do, they told stories.

Myrrhine told the story of the eleventh year, when a man had come to the house at the eleventh hour with a goat, and had insisted, at length and with a straight face, that the goat was a gift for the household and that it was worth forty thousand sesterces, and that he would take thirty thousand in cash. Prima told the story of the night the awning came down in the peristyle and eleven senators spent an hour in the rain. Cinnamus told the story of the seventh box, and how he had been wrong about it for three years and a copyist of twenty-five had been right in an afternoon, and he told it against himself, and Tertia put her hand over her face and laughed.

Meno told the story of the geese.

And at the end, when the fire was low and the wine was nearly gone, Vibia said, 'I will go first, because I have a long way home and I am too old to walk up the Caelian at the second hour,' and she stood up and looked around the kitchen court and said, 'I have been coming to this house for a decade and I have never once been bored in it, and there is no other house in Rome of which I can say that.'

Then she said, to Antonia, 'The courtyard at Ostia. Once a month. Bring the women who have nowhere to go.' And she went.

Prima went next. She was going to the house on the Aventine in the morning; she had the key in her sleeve and had had it there for two months.

At the gate she stopped.

'I have been in this house seven years,' she said. 'I came in at twenty-four with the scars on my back and nothing else. I have been paid wages for seven years and I have two hundred thousand sesterces and a house, and when I am old I will tell people that I was in the house of Antonia Cassia and that it was the best house in Rome, and nobody will believe me.' She looked at the courtyard. 'There is a thing I have wanted to say to you since August and I have never said it, because it is not the sort of thing that is said in a house like this. When you sent Berenice away in the carriage, I told you that you had made a mistake, and I was right, and it nearly cost you everything. But I want you to know that I was standing in the passage when you did it, and I thought: that is what a woman ought to be, and there is not one woman in a thousand in this city who would have done it.' She held out her hand. 'That is all. I will see you at Vibia's courtyard, and I will bring a cake, and I will not talk about the Argiletum.'

She went out. Cinnamus went at the first hour, to the room he had taken in the Subura near the shop, and Faustus went with him as far as the corner.

At the second hour Antonia walked through the house with a lamp.

She went through the empty rooms one at a time. There was nothing in any of them. The mosaic in the atrium was grey with dust; someone had swept it and the dust had come back. The zodiac room smelled of cold plaster. In the peristyle the fountain had been turned off for a month and there was a little green in the basin.

She stood in the small room by the garden and looked at the place where the couch had been.

Then she went out and closed the door and put a wax seal on it, and did the same on the door of the library, and the door of the tablinum, and the door of the room at the top of the west wing, and the street door last of all.

She had had a seal made for the purpose, in the fourth month, from a gem-cutter in the Via Sacra, at a cost of four hundred sesterces, which she had entered in the book as a household expense. It was a small intaglio of a vine leaf with the letters A C.

She sealed the street door on the inside, which was absurd, because the door was no longer hers, and she stood in the doorway with the lamp in her hand and the wax cooling.

Behind her, Myrrhine said, 'You know that we have to go out through the garden.'

'I know.'

'And that the garden door does not lock.'

'I know,' said Antonia. 'Bring the chest. The men are coming for the rest in the morning, and there is nothing in it that anybody would steal.'

---

Tertia came up the hill in the morning and found the street door with a wax seal on it and the workmen's cart already at the corner.

She stood in front of it for a while. Then she walked round through the Vicus Patricius to the garden door, which was not sealed, because the garden door did not lock, and she went into the courtyard and stood among the empty rooms with her box of tablets under her arm.

Antonia found her in the peristyle, looking at the fountain.

'I came to say that I am not going to be sentimental about a house,' said Tertia, 'and then I came up the hill at the second hour and I have been standing in this courtyard for a quarter of an hour and I find that I am.' She turned. 'It is a strange thing to be sad about a building. I have no memories of this place. I was here for five months and I copied four hundred and eleven tablets and ate my dinner at that table and argued with the atriensis about a box that did not balance.' She looked up at the ceiling of the peristyle. 'And I find that I am standing here at the fourth hour of the morning with a lump in my throat because a woman I met in October is closing a house I never lived in.'

'You lived in it,' said Antonia. 'For five months, at a table, eating your dinner. That is living in a house.'

'I know.' Tertia wiped her eyes with the back of her hand, briskly, in a way that was entirely her own. 'I am not going to ask you the question I said I would not ask in March. I want you to know that I have remembered it and that I have decided not to ask it and that I am going to be extremely smug about it for the rest of the year.'

'Good,' said Antonia.

Tertia looked around the empty courtyard one last time.

'What happens to it now?' she said. 'The stones. The fountain. The mosaic that Sabina said had water under it.'

'The mosaic is going to the hall of the sandalmakers,' said Antonia. 'The fountain will be broken up; the lead is worth something and the stone goes into the aqueduct. The paintings will be plastered over in April. The fig tree will be cut down because it is in the line of the corridor, and the cypress also. In three years there will be a wall and a channel of water and a great many people walking past it on their way to the baths, and none of them will know that there was ever a house here.'

Tertia was quiet for a moment.

'Then I am glad I copied the books,' she said. 'There are four hundred and eleven tablets in the fourth region with this house in them, in my hand, and in a hundred years somebody will open a box and find out exactly how much a woman paid a cook in the eleventh year, and that will be the only thing left of it.'

'That is a strange thing to want,' said Antonia.

'It is what I have,' said Tertia, and went out through the garden door.

---

The new house was on the Aventine, three rooms and a courtyard and a yard, in a street of tanners and dyers, and it cost eleven thousand sesterces, and Antonia bought it in Myrrhine's name.

She did not tell her until the deed was in her hand.

They came up the hill at the third hour with a lamp and the chest and a basket, and the street was dark and smelled of the tannery vats, and the door of the house was blue, and there was a fig tree in the yard that was not the fig tree and a wall that was not the wall.

Myrrhine stood in the courtyard with the lamp.

'What is this?'

'It is a house,' said Antonia. 'Three rooms, a courtyard, a yard, a well that is shared with the dyer next door and a roof that needs work in the summer.' She took the deed out of her sleeve. 'It is in your name. It has been registered at the temple of Castor this afternoon. The ground rent is eleven sesterces a year and the taxes are in the schedule.'

Myrrhine did not take the deed.

'Why?'

'Because I have been thinking about what to give you for sixteen years,' said Antonia, 'and I have given you a wage and a room and a position and a share of everything I own, and none of it was a thing of your own, and I have known it the whole time.' She held out the deed. 'I have made a great many mistakes in my life and I am going to make a great many more, and one of them was in the fifth year when I decided that what was between us could be put in a box and kept in a cupboard along with everything else. I am not going to do it again. This is a house in your name, and if you want to live in it with me, that is what I want, and if you do not, it is yours anyway.'

Myrrhine looked at the deed for a long moment.

'You are a very difficult woman,' she said.

'I have been told.'

'And you have done this in the worst possible way,' said Myrrhine, 'because you have bought a house and put it in my name and handed me a deed in a courtyard at the third hour of the night, and now I cannot be angry with you, and I have been saving up to be angry with you about something for about a year.' She took the deed. 'Sixteen years. I told you in September that you would have to ask me twice. This is not asking. This is a deed.'

'It is both,' said Antonia.

Myrrhine stood in the courtyard with the lamp in one hand and the deed in the other.

'Then I will say it once,' she said, 'and then I am going to bed, because it is the third hour and I have been moving a household for four months. Yes. There. Now put the chest down and come inside, and in the morning we will find out that the roof leaks and the well is shared with a dyer who gets up at the fourth hour, and we will be two women in a small house on the Aventine with no name over the gate, and I find that I am looking forward to it more than I have looked forward to anything since I was twenty.'

---

They were in the new house for nine days.

Antonia spent them doing the things on the list. She had the mosaic lifted in a house that was no longer hers, which was absurd and which she did anyway, and gave it to the guild of the sandalmakers for their hall, and it was installed in the summer and it is there still. She wrote eleven letters. She went to the temple of Castor twice. She and Myrrhine argued about a bed and bought one, and the roof was examined by a man who said it would last the year and was wrong.

On the ninth day she went to the Palatine in the afternoon and sat with Tarquitia Priscilla for an hour while Marcellina read to her, and the old woman said, at the end of it, 'You have closed the house.'

'On the last day of March.'

'And what are you now?'

'A woman of forty-two with three rooms on the Aventine and a great deal of money and nothing to do,' said Antonia.

'Good,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'That is the most dangerous thing a woman can be in this city, and it has taken you twenty-five years to get there, and I hope you make a great deal of trouble.'

---

On the eleventh day of April, at the second hour of the night, the north slope of the Esquiline caught fire.

It began, they said afterwards, in a bakery on the Vicus Cyprius, in an oven that had been left too hot with a batch of bread in it, and the baker and his family got out, and the fire went up through the roof and into the block behind, and the wind was from the south and there had been no rain for three weeks.

Antonia was woken by Myrrhine at the third hour.

'There is a fire,' said Myrrhine. 'On the Esquiline. It is the north slope; it started at the second hour and it has taken four blocks.'

Antonia was out of bed and into her clothes in the dark.

'Get Faustus. Get the cart if there is one to be had, and if there is not, we walk.' She was at the door. 'Myrrhine. It is the house.'

'I know.'

'It is the house, and the chest went to the new house, and the four things are in the chest, and there is nothing in that house but plaster and a fountain and a mosaic that I gave away last week.' She stopped with her hand on the door. 'And the book.'

Myrrhine looked at her.

'The book is in the tablinum,' said Antonia, 'in the press behind the second door, in four boxes, and it is the whole of eleven years. It has every name in Rome in it. It has the loans and the favours and the men who came and the wives who borrowed and the magistrate's freedman and the fire in the ninth region and eleven things that would end nine men's careers, and it is in a wooden press in a room with a plaster ceiling and the roof of that block is timber.'

'Then let it burn,' said Myrrhine.

Antonia stood in the doorway.

'If it burns,' she said, 'then there is nothing in the world that anybody can hold against me, and there is nothing in the world that I can hold against anybody, and every man in this city who has been afraid of me for eleven years will know by the morning that he does not have to be afraid any more.' She took her cloak. 'And if it does not burn, and somebody takes it out of that press before the roof comes down, then the whole of it is in the hands of whoever finds it, and there are forty men in Rome who would pay a great deal for it and one man who would pay anything.'

'Then we go,' said Myrrhine, 'and we take the cart, and we take Faustus, and we get there before anybody else does.'

'Yes,' said Antonia.

She went out into the street, where the whole of the Aventine was awake and the sky over the Esquiline was the colour of a coal in a brazier, and the smoke was coming across the river on the wind.
