# 5 | The Linen Room

Arretium, twenty-six years earlier.

The villa of the Tarquitii stood on a ridge above the Arno valley, in country that was green in spring and yellow in July and full of the sound of doves. It had been built by a man who made money in the clay beds and married into a family that made money in the Senate, and it had been enlarged four times since, so that it was not one house but a series of houses leaning against one another, with a courtyard in the middle and a tower at the northern end that was used for storing quinces and, in the summer, for sleeping, because it was the only place on the estate where the air moved.

Cassia was fifteen that spring and had been in the household for two years, and she knew the villa the way a sailor knows a ship: not room by room but by the things that needed doing in each of them.

She knew that the old consul's chamber faced north and got no sun after the fourth hour and was therefore cold even in June, and that he hated being cold, and that there were three braziers in it and only one was ever lit. She knew that the steward, who was called Faustus and was a freedman of the family, counted the wine on the calends and the oil on the ides and the linen once a week, and that his count depended on the weather, because he counted what was dry. She knew that the cook's daughter had a cough that would kill her before the winter, and that the cook knew it, and that no one spoke of it. She knew that the second-best dining room had a mosaic of a hunting dog in the doorway that had been damaged in the year of the earthquake, and that if you walked over it in the dark you could feel the dog's ear with your foot, and that everyone in the household walked over the dog's ear in the dark and no one had ever been told why.

And she knew the linen room, which was hers.

It was in the west wing, off the corridor that ran from the bath to the kitchen court, and it had a window of thick green glass and a table, two chests, and a shelf on which the household's linen was laid in the Roman way: sheets, towels, napkins, tablecloths, the good set with the purple stripe for the master's birthday. Her work was to sort, to mend, to count, to see that nothing went out with a stain or a tear, and to see that nothing went out at all that was meant for someone else. It was not heavy work. It was work that could be done with the hands while the mind went elsewhere, and Cassia's mind went elsewhere a great deal, which was the beginning of everything that happened to her.

She had been born in Baiae, in a house behind the fuller's shop, to a woman who had been born in Nicomedia and sold twice before she was twenty. Her mother had died when she was six, of a thing that began in the throat. She had been sold at eleven to a dealer in Puteoli and sold again at thirteen to Faustus, who had come to the market with a list and had bought her because she was small and quick and because the price was good. She had a name, which the dealer had given her, and no other. She had never seen Rome. She had never seen the sea except from a distance, as a brightness beyond the roofs of Puteoli.

Her whole world, at fifteen, was a villa above the Arno, eleven rooms that were hers to keep clean, and forty-one people whose linen she washed.

She was not unhappy. This is a thing that people who have never been property find difficult to understand, and Cassia understood it better than anyone: she was not unhappy because she had no image of a life in which she would be happy, and a person without such an image can be contented in the way that a dog is contented. She had food, and a bed, and work that she did well, and a corner of the linen room where the sun came in the afternoon, and two friends, and a small store of things she had not stolen but had been given: a bronze hairpin, a piece of blue glass, a Greek word she had learned from the tutor's discarded tablets and kept like a coin.

The word was *eleutheria*. She did not know at first what it meant. She had found it on a wax tablet in the rubbish behind the schoolroom, in a line of a poet, and she had copied it out with a stylus on a scrap of wood and kept it in the box with the hairpin, and it was three months before the tutor's slave told her what it meant, and when he told her she had laughed, because it seemed to her a very large word to give to such a small thing as the freedom to go where one liked.

---

The old consul had been dying for a year and a half. He had dropsy, and a temper that had once been famous in the Senate and had shrunk with him into something small and bitter and domestic. He was carried out to the garden in the morning and carried in at the fourth hour and he shouted at everyone, and his wife sat with him for two hours every day and read to him, and there was no affection in it that Cassia could see, only a kind of punctual duty, like the changing of a dressing.

The mistress of the house was Tarquitia Priscilla, who was then fifty-two and who ran the villa and the estates and the family's affairs as though they were a small army in a country that might at any moment be invaded. She was not cruel. That was the thing that people found hardest to explain about her. She did not beat her slaves, or sell them for trifles, or take their children; she paid the physician when they were ill, and she knew all their names, and when the cook's daughter died in November she sat with the cook for a whole night, and the household saw it, and was grateful, and did not understand that they had been shown the exact size of the box in which they lived.

The son of the house had been away for nine years.

He was a legate with the army on the Danube, and his name came into the villa four or five times a year in letters that were read aloud in the atrium, and the household knew the shape of his career as it might have known the weather in a province: he had been quaestor, he had been tribune of the soldiers, he had commanded a cohort, he had been in Pannonia and then in Moesia, he had written a book about the laws of boundaries that his father had had copied and given to three men. There was a bust of him in the tablinum, made when he was twenty-four. He had a narrow, careful face and hair that went back from the temples. Cassia had dusted it a hundred times.

In the spring of the year, the news came that the army had been beaten in Dacia, that a governor had been killed, that a legion's eagle had been taken, and that the whole of the frontier was in an uproar; and three weeks later the news came that the consul's son was coming home.

---

The villa prepared for him for eleven days.

The whole household was turned out: the mosaics polished, the good couches brought from the storeroom and beaten, the wall paintings in the south range touched up by a man from Arretium who was paid a great deal and did a poor job, the wine brought up, the cook given money and a list and two men from the market. Three rooms were made ready, and then a fourth, because the mistress decided that his study must be his own. The linen was changed twice. On the last day Faustus walked the whole villa with a tablet, and Cassia walked behind him with a basket, and whenever he found a thing that was wrong he said a number, and she wrote it down.

At the ninth hour of the day he arrived, the household was drawn up in the courtyard in order of rank, and Cassia was at the end of the line with the laundry women, and she looked at the gate for a long time before anyone came through it.

He came in on foot, which surprised everyone, with two soldiers behind him and a slave with the horses, and he was thinner than the bust, and older, and he stopped in the gateway and looked at the courtyard as though he were reading a list of things that had been there when he left and were still there.

Then he crossed the courtyard to his mother, and knelt, and put his head against her hands, and the whole household watched, and the cook wept, and the mistress stood with her hands on her son's head and her face perfectly still.

Cassia, at the end of the line, thought: he is the most important person I have ever seen.

---

She did not speak to him for a month.

He was busy with his father, and with the estate's accounts, and with letters that went out every day and men who came up the road from Rome and stayed the night. He walked in the fields in the early morning. He sat with the old man in the north chamber for an hour every afternoon and read to him, and the old man shouted at him too, and he did not shout back.

Cassia saw him at a distance, and she learned him as she learned rooms. He rose before the fourth hour. He drank water, not wine, until the sixth. He read in the garden in the afternoon with his feet on a stone. He was courteous to slaves in a way that made them uneasy, because courtesy from a master is a kind of weather that can change. He had a habit of standing still in doorways, as if he had forgotten what he had come for.

The first time they spoke, she was carrying a jug.

It was the fifth hour of a hot afternoon in June, and she was taking water and a clean towel up to the bath in the north range, because one of the boys had been sent and had not come back, and she came around the corner into the corridor that ran past the linen room and found him standing with his back to the wall and his hands shaking.

There was blood on his hands. Not much. It was on the knuckles and the palm and it had dried in the lines of the skin, and he was looking at it as though it belonged to somebody else.

She stopped. A slave who comes upon a master in a condition should go back the way she came. She knew that. She had known it since she was eleven. But the jug was in her hands and the corridor was narrow and she was fifteen years old and he looked, for a moment, exactly like a man in the road who has fallen off a cart.

'Domine,' she said.

He looked up.

'There is water,' she said, and held up the jug, and then, because it seemed to require saying, 'It is clean.'

He looked at the jug. Then he put his hands out, and she poured the water over them, and he rubbed them together, and the water went down onto the mosaic in a thin pink line and into the drain at the side.

'Thank you,' he said.

'Domine.'

'What is your name?'

'Cassia.'

'Cassia.' He said it as though he were putting it somewhere. 'Whose are you?'

'I am in the linen, domine. Under Faustus.'

'Faustus.' He dried his hands on the towel. 'Faustus is a good man. He is a thief, but he is a good man. It is a combination one learns to value in a household.' He handed her back the towel. His hands were steady now. 'My father,' he said. 'He has been bled. The physician bled him this morning and there was a great deal of blood and my father said —' He stopped. 'It does not matter what he said. Thank you, Cassia. You may go.'

She went. At the corner she looked back, and he was still standing there, looking at the drain.

That night she lay in her bed in the women's room and thought about the word *eleutheria*, and about the fact that a man who could have anything he wanted had stood in a corridor with blood on his hands and had said thank you to a girl with a jug.

---

He sent for her four days later, which was how it began, and the sending was so ordinary that no one in the villa thought anything of it.

The schoolroom had a library attached, or the beginning of one: three presses of books, most of them law, some of them history, one of them poetry that had belonged to his grandfather. He had found the room disordered — the mice had got at a roll of Livy, and the slave who was supposed to keep it had been sleeping in the garden — and he wanted it put right, and he had said to Faustus, 'Send me the girl from the linen. She reads.'

Faustus, who did not know that she could read, said so.

'She writes the numbers in my book better than I do,' Faustus said, 'but that is numbers.'

'Numbers are the hard part,' said the consul's son. 'Letters are for children.'

So she was sent, and she spent four afternoons in the library with a feather duster and a pot of paste and a young man who sat at the table with his sleeves rolled up, and who asked her, on the second afternoon, whether she knew what she was dusting.

'Livy,' she said.

'Which Livy?'

She looked at the roll in her hand. 'The one that begins with the founding.'

'And what does the founding say?'

She had never been asked a question like that by anyone. She stood with the roll in her hands and her heart going, and said, 'It says that Romulus killed his brother.'

He laughed. It was a real laugh and it surprised them both, and it changed the shape of the room.

'It says a great deal more than that,' he said, 'and rather less. Sit down. Not on the floor; there is a stool. Read me the first column.'

'I cannot read Greek, domine.'

'It is not Greek. It is Latin and it is not even good Latin; Livy writes like a man who has swallowed a trumpet.' He pushed the roll toward her. 'Read.'

She read badly, and he corrected her, and she read again, and after an hour she was reading better than she had ever read in her life, and her ears were hot, and she had forgotten to be afraid of him.

That was the first afternoon. There were eleven more over the next two months, and at the end of them she could read Latin, and had begun, secretly, on the easier Greek, and had been given a wax tablet of her own and told to keep it, and had written on it, in her best hand, the word *eleutheria*, and had hidden it in her box with the blue glass.

She was in love with him by the fourth afternoon. She was fifteen and he was twenty-nine and he had a broken provincial accent in Greek and a careful way of listening, and he was the first person who had ever asked her what she thought. That was all. It would have been enough to ruin her if he had been a good man; and he was not a good man, and he was not a bad one either, which was worse, because it meant she had to find out for herself, and by the time she found out it was too late.

---

The autumn of that year was wet. The grapes came in early and the wine was thin. The old consul got worse and then, in October, better, and then worse again, and the household lived from week to week by the physician's face.

The son of the house had been due to go back to the frontier in September. He did not go. There were letters about it; there was one letter, in particular, that came in a courier's bag from Rome and that he read in the garden and then folded and put in his sleeve without a word. After that he stopped speaking about the Danube altogether, and when the men who came up from Rome asked him about the war he answered them civilly and briefly and turned the conversation to drainage.

Cassia did not know then what it meant. She learned it later, in Puteoli, from a man who had served in Moesia: that after the disaster the emperor had looked for men to blame, and that the son of a family that had once opposed him was a very good man to blame, and that a legate who was recalled to Rome in the autumn and not sent back in the spring had been, in the language of the court, set aside.

So he stayed. He wrote his book on boundaries. He walked in the fields. He read in the garden with his feet on a stone. And in November, when the nights had begun to be cold and the household had moved into the winter rooms, he came to the linen room at the second hour of the night with a lamp in his hand and stood in the doorway, and Cassia, who had been mending a sheet at the table and had known his step in the corridor from the first sound of it, put down the needle.

'Domine,' she said.

He stood there for a long moment. She could see him deciding. Afterwards, for years, she would think about that moment and about the fact that he had stood in the doorway for long enough to have gone away.

'You should send me out,' he said.

'I know,' she said.

That was all. He put the lamp on the shelf and closed the door, and the door of the linen room had a latch that could not be locked from the inside, and it did not matter, because nobody came.

Later, when it was over and they were lying in the dark with the lamp turned down and the smell of clean linen all around them, he said, into her hair, 'I am sorry.'

'For what?'

He did not answer that. He said, after a while, 'I will look after you.'

She believed him. That was the thing she could never afterwards explain to anyone, not to Myrrhine, not to the physician at Puteoli, not to the priestess of Isis to whom she went once, in her thirties, in a city where no one knew her: she had believed him, and it had not been stupid, because in the world she had been born into a promise of protection from a man like him was the largest thing a girl could hope for, and because he had meant it, at the time, in the way that such men mean things.

---

The winter was the happiest of her life. She knew that afterwards, and it made her ashamed for years, and then one day it stopped making her ashamed and became simply true: the winter of that year, in the villa above the Arno, was the happiest she had been as a girl, and it was built on a lie, and both of those things were the case at once.

They were careful. They were not careful enough; nobody is, in a house that has forty pairs of eyes and a routine. But they were careful in the way that people in a dangerous situation become expert in small things: she learned to know which of the slaves could be trusted and which could be paid and which would tell, and he learned to arrange papers so that a girl could be sent to the library for an hour without anyone finding it strange, and they invented a whole private language of the ordinary, which is the way such things are always done.

She was taught things in that winter that no free woman of her class would have been taught. He read to her: laws, histories, the letters of Cicero, which he loved and which she found, honestly, insufferable. He taught her to argue — properly, in the way of the courts, to state a case and put the objections and answer them, and he was delighted when she found a hole in his reasoning, and he said once, 'If you had been born a man I would have made you a jurist,' and then looked as though he had said something he should not have.

She kept the linen. She was better at the linen than she had ever been, because she was happy, and because she had begun to think of the household as a thing she might one day have a place in.

In February, Faustus stopped her in the corridor.

He had a basket of tablets on his hip and he was going toward the tablinum, and he stood aside for her, and then he did not move, and she stood with her own basket against her stomach and waited.

'You are in the library a great deal,' he said.

'Domine sends for me to mend the books.'

'There are three slaves in this villa who can read, and two of them are men, and one of them is sixty,' said Faustus, 'and he sends for the girl from the linen.'

She said nothing. She had learned, in that winter, that saying nothing was a skill, and that it could be practised like any other.

Faustus looked at her. He was fifty and had been in the family since he was nine, and he had a wife in Arretium and a son in the vigiles in Rome, and his whole life had been built on knowing which of the family's small disorders to see and which to be blind to.

'I have been in this house for forty-one years,' he said. 'I have seen three girls sent to the mill at Puteoli and one sent to the mines for a thing that was not her fault, and I have never once seen the family lift a finger to stop it, and I have never once seen the family blame the man. Do you understand what I am saying to you?'

'Yes.'

'You do not,' said Faustus. 'You think I am telling you to be careful. I am telling you that when this ends, and it will end, it will end in a morning, with a steward's list, and you will be a line on the list, and there will be a cart. I have written such lists. My hand has written them.' He shifted the basket. 'The old man is dying. When he dies the household will be divided among the heirs and the daughter will come from Rome with her husband, and she is not her mother, and she counts everything twice. If she finds a girl in the house who has been in the library, she will not send you to the mill. She will sell you somewhere that a message cannot reach you, and she will do it in a week, and she will tell herself she is protecting her brother.'

Cassia's mouth was dry.

'What should I do?' she said.

Faustus looked at her for a long moment. Something in his face shifted, and for a moment he was not the steward at all but a tired man in a corridor.

'You should ask him for a paper,' he said. 'Not a promise. A paper. Manumission in his own hand with a date, and a sum, and the date before the old man dies, because after the old man dies he will be a man with a sister and a share of a debt and a name to protect, and he will be a different man, and he will not remember that he was ever the man who taught you to read.' He stepped aside. 'That is all. I have never said this to you and I will never say it again.'

He went. And she stood in the corridor for a while, and then she went and mended a sheet, and she did not ask for a paper, because she was fifteen and she was in love and it seemed to her the most insulting thing in the world to ask the man who had taught her to read for a document.

---

The old consul died in the last week of February, in the night, without a word.

The household knew before dawn. There was a particular sound that a villa makes when its master dies: a door opened and not closed, a woman crying in the kitchen court and then being told to be quiet, the steward's voice going from room to room with the same four words. Cassia heard it all from the women's room and lay still, and understood, with a clarity that was almost physical, that the roof over her had changed.

For nine days the villa was in mourning and in confusion. The body was washed and laid out and the masks were brought from the cupboards. The tenants came up from the farms. The neighbours came. The old man's freedmen came, and his old clients, and two men from Rome who had been his colleagues and who stood in the atrium and looked at the masks and said the things that are said.

The daughter of the house arrived on the third day, from Rome, with her husband and four slaves and a great deal of luggage. She was thirty-six, and her name was Tarquitia Aurelia, and she was not beautiful and had never been, and she had made herself formidable instead, which is a longer road and arrives somewhere more permanent.

She walked into the villa and she did what her mother had done for thirty years and what her mother was now too tired to do: she counted.

She counted the silver and found it correct. She counted the wine and found it short, and Faustus explained, and she accepted the explanation and wrote it down. She counted the linen and found it correct, and she looked at Cassia for a moment longer than the counting required, and Cassia kept her eyes down and her hands still and felt the look go over her like a hand.

That evening, at dinner, she heard the mistress say to her daughter, in the flat voice of a woman who has decided to say a thing once: 'The girl in the linen is not to be sold.'

'Why not?'

'Because I say so.'

'Mother, if there is a reason—'

'There is a reason,' said Tarquitia Priscilla, 'and it is mine, and I am not going to give it to you at table. When I am dead you may do as you like.'

And Aurelia said, after a moment, in a voice like her mother's: 'Very well.'

Cassia, standing at the sideboard with the jug, heard all of it. She did not know what it meant. She would spend the next twenty-four years finding out.

---

In the spring, in March, when the almond was out and the doves had begun again, she went three weeks without bleeding, and then four, and then she was sick in the morning behind the kitchen court, and the cook's wife looked at her for a long moment and said nothing, and Cassia went and sat in the linen room with the door open and the sun coming through the green glass and understood, all at once, the exact size of the box in which she lived.

She did not tell him at once. She waited nine days, and in those eleven days she did the arithmetic that she would do, in one form or another, for the rest of her life.

She was a slave. The child was a slave, and belonged to the household, and would be entered in the census as property. She was fifteen; she would be sixteen in June. If she told him, he would do something — she believed that, she had to believe it — and the something would be a kindness, and the kindness would be arranged within the limits of what a man in his position could arrange without damaging himself, and she had already begun to understand, because she was quick and because the winter had made her quicker, that the limits of what such a man could arrange were not where she had once thought they were.

On the eleventh day she told him.

He was in the library. It was the afternoon and the light was behind him and there was a roll of the law of boundaries open on the table, and he listened to her without moving, and then he got up and closed the door and stood with his hand on the table.

'You are certain?'

'Yes.'

He nodded slowly, as though she had given him a figure in an account. Then he said, 'Say nothing to anyone. Not to Faustus. Not to the women. If you are sick, say it is the water. I will think of something.'

And he did think of something, and it took him eight weeks, and he thought of it with great care and in perfect good faith, and it destroyed her.

She did not know that yet. That night she lay in the women's room with her hand flat on her stomach and a happiness in her that she was later ashamed of, and she thought: he will look after me.

And in the corridor outside, the lamp that the steward carried at the second hour of the night went past the door and stopped, and then went on.
