# 1 | The Golden Vine

The house had been built for a man who wanted to be remembered, and it had failed him twice. The first of its owners had died in exile with his name struck from the fasti; the second had lost it at dice to a man who lost it in turn to a creditor who lost it to Antonia Cassia in the fourth year of the emperor's Dacian war. The walls had kept their painted gods through all of it. On the south side of the peristyle, Diana still hunted with her dogs among a forest of faded green, and above the fountain a Neptune who had once been somebody's portrait held up a hand to a wave that would never fall.

Antonia had kept the Neptune. She had painted everything else.

At the tenth hour of a May afternoon the house smelled of hot stone, box hedge, and the resin the gardeners used on the fig trees. Somewhere behind the kitchen wall a boy was turning a spit too fast and being told so. In the corridor that ran from the service court to the new dining room, two of the under-stewards were arguing in whispers about a crate of Falernian that had arrived with three jars short, and they stopped arguing when they heard her sandals on the mosaic.

She had been in the house long enough that its noises sorted themselves for her without effort: the difference between a dropped cup and a thrown one, the particular flat sound of a slave's shoulder hitting a door frame in a narrow passage, the sudden hush that travelled ahead of a guest like a wind crossing barley. She could lie in her bed at the top of the house and know from the footsteps below whether the kitchen was behind, whether a girl had been crying, whether someone had brought a stranger in through the garden gate instead of the street door.

The new dining room was the largest thing she had ever paid for that could not be sold again. It had taken eleven months, two architects, and a quantity of marble that had come up the Tiber on a barge she also owned, and it held seven couches beneath a ceiling painted with the twelve signs of the zodiac in gold leaf and lapis, on which the painter had laboured so long that his hands had begun to shake and he had asked, at the end, whether he might put his own small sign among the stars. Antonia had said no. She regretted it sometimes. She regretted it now, standing in the doorway with her keys at her belt, looking up at the Ram and the Bull and the Twins and thinking that a man who had spent nine months on his back in the dark ought to have been allowed to leave a mark.

'Well?' said Myrrhine. 'Will it do?'

Myrrhine was thirty-six and Greek and wore her hair in the old way, pinned close, and had a face that could be closed like a shutter. She had been in the house since it had been a bad investment with a good courtyard, and she was the only person in Rome who could ask Antonia a question in that tone and expect an answer.

'It will do,' said Antonia.

'It cost enough to do.'

'It cost enough to be talked about. That is not the same thing.' She walked the length of the room, past the seven couches with their bronze feet, and put her hand on the table where the wine would be mixed. The wood was citrus, from Africa, and the grain moved under her palm like water under ice. 'The lamps?'

'Lit at the ninth hour, all forty. Brennus is at the gate. Meno is at the inner door and unhappy about it.'

'Meno is unhappy about everything.'

'Meno is unhappy about standing at an inner door while an old man with a knife could come over the garden wall,' Myrrhine said. 'He says a doorkeeper who cannot see the street is a decoration.'

'Tell him the decoration is paid the same as the doorkeeper.' Antonia turned. 'Who has come?'

'Nobody yet. Naso's litter was seen on the Vicus Patricius. Sestius will be late because he believes it is a kind of rank. The young man Celer has been here an hour, in the garden, drinking water and trying to look as though he does not care about anything.'

'Does he care about anything?'

'He cares very much,' Myrrhine said. 'He has the face of a boy who has been told his whole life that he is promising.'

Antonia laughed, once, and it was a real laugh, which happened perhaps four times a week. She was forty-one years old. She had a broad, still face that took paint well and needed little of it, a nose that had been broken when she was seventeen and set by a woman who set bones for the circus, and eyes that people described, depending on their feelings about her, as intelligent or as hard. She wore that afternoon a dress of pale Egyptian linen with a border of dull gold thread, and on her right hand a ring with a carnelian the size of a thumbnail, and at her belt the iron ring of keys that had once belonged to a woman in Arretium and now opened forty-one doors in Rome.

Eleven of those doors were locked against her own household. That was not because she was cruel. It was because she had been a slave, and had learned that the difference between a locked door and an open one is measured in what a person is willing to lose.

'They will begin arriving in a quarter of an hour,' she said. 'Eucharis?'

'Dressed. Harp tuned. She has a cough she says she does not have.'

'Then she has a cough.' Antonia went to the door and looked back at the room with the seven couches. 'The girl?'

'Lucilla is in the small room off the library. She has been sick twice. She is seventeen and she has been told she is going to meet men who could buy her whole family, which is true, and she has decided that the way to survive it is to be very quiet and very pretty and to agree with everything.'

'That is a reasonable decision.'

'It is a decision that will last about a year,' Myrrhine said, 'and then she will be twenty and she will not be able to find it in herself to agree with anything, and we will have a different problem.'

Antonia stood a moment. In the corridor beyond, someone was carrying a stack of folding stools and had dropped one, and the clatter went down the passage like a shout. She thought of the small room off the library, of the seventeen years in it, of the way a girl's fear smells — like metal, like the inside of a cup left upside down.

'Bring her to me before the second course,' she said. 'Not to sit. To sing. One song, and then she goes back to her room, and no one goes to her room.'

'There will be men who—'

'There are always men who. That is why there are doors.'

---

The first guests came at the tenth hour and a half, and by the eleventh the house had changed its nature entirely, the way a face changes when it begins to perform. Torches were lit along the street front so that the painted portico seemed to breathe. Two slaves with fans stood at the entrance to move the warm air and to give the arriving litters something to look at. The noise of the Esquiline — carters forbidden by law to move by day and doing it anyway, a woman selling roasted chickpeas, the whole grinding commerce of the Subura coming faintly over the roofs — faded behind the closing of the gate.

Gaius Cluvius Naso came first, as bankers do when they wish to be seen arriving first. He was sixty-one, thin as a nail, with the careful hands of a man who had counted a great deal of money and the pale eyes of one who had counted other people's. He had lent Antonia the money to buy the house. He had lent her the money to buy the barge. He had, in the way of such men, become so entangled in her fortune that he could no longer tell the difference between affection and exposure, and he greeted her with the kiss of a kinsman and the grip of a creditor.

'You have gilded the ceiling,' he said, looking up.

'I have.'

'How much?'

'Less than the ceiling in your atrium.'

'I have no ceiling in my atrium,' Naso said. 'I have a roof. Ceilings are for men who intend to be spoken of.'

'Then you have come to the right house.'

He laughed and let her take his arm, which was itself a kind of payment. Behind him came the others in a slow wash: Marcus Sestius Verus, who was praetor this year and never let a room forget it; the rhetorician Hermagoras of Smyrna, who had been hired for the evening at a price that would feed a street for a month; a widow named Vibia Lucana, who was Antonia's friend in the way that two people who know each other's worst transactions can be friends; and half a dozen more whose names mattered less than their money, and whose money mattered less than their willingness to be seen spending it.

Aulus Rubrius Celer came last, from the garden, with his hair still damp and his new senatorial stripe sewn, Antonia suspected, by his mother. He was twenty-six. He had been in the Senate for four months. He had, in that time, made two speeches, both of which Antonia had paid a man to praise in the Forum and both of which had gone badly.

'Domina,' he said, and then flushed at the word, because it was what a slave called his mistress, and because he never knew what to call her.

'Celer.' She took his hand. 'You have been drinking my water for an hour. Have you eaten?'

'I wasn't hungry.'

'You are always hungry. You are twenty-six.' She steered him toward the dining room with a hand at his elbow. 'You will sit next to Vibia Lucana and you will laugh at her jokes and you will not mention the grain commission until the third course, and when you do mention it, you will mention it as though it were my idea and not yours.'

'I don't understand why—'

'Because Sestius will oppose anything that comes from you, and he cannot oppose a thing that comes from me without admitting that I have ideas.' She smiled at him. 'He would rather swallow a wasp.'

---

The dining room, when they were all in it, was the most beautiful room in Rome that nobody respectable would admit to having visited. The seven couches were arranged in the old fashion around three sides of the table, and the table was set with silver that Antonia had bought from the estate of a man proscribed twelve years before, and the light from forty lamps moved across the ceiling so that the zodiac seemed to turn, slowly, as though the room were a small heaven and the guests were its only souls.

There was a whole roasted boar stuffed with figs and small birds. There were oysters from Baiae, which had come up the road that morning in barrels packed with seaweed. There were dormice rolled in honey and poppy seed, which Antonia disliked and served anyway, because men who had read Martial liked to be able to say they had eaten them. There was a sauce of fermented fish that had cost more than the boar and a wine that was older than the youngest woman in the room.

Antonia lay at the head of the left couch, which was the host's place, and watched them eat.

This was the part of her work that no one ever counted. She had a woman who kept the accounts, and a man who kept the cellar, and Myrrhine who kept everything else, but the watching was hers. She watched who drank first and who waited. She watched which man put his hand on a slave's hip without looking, as though touching furniture, and which man's eyes followed a girl around the room and then away, ashamed. She watched Hermagoras, who had made his name by despising wealth, eat three helpings of oysters. She watched Sestius refuse the dormice and then take one when he thought no one was attending, and she thought: there. That is the whole man, in one small hypocrite's mouthful.

'You are quiet,' said Vibia Lucana, from the middle couch. She was forty-five, twice widowed, comfortably rich in her own right, and had the sort of laugh that made men check their purses.

'I am counting.'

'The silver?'

'The evening.' Antonia turned her cup slowly by the stem. 'In four hours, half these men will have told me something they did not intend to tell me. In six, one of them will have offered me something he cannot afford. In the morning I will know which of them is in trouble and which is merely afraid, and I will be able to tell the difference, and that is worth more than the silver.'

'And the women?'

'The women will have told me everything by the second hour,' Vibia said, 'because we are better at this and always have been.'

The first course came and went. Hermagoras, who had been hired to be brilliant, was brilliant about the decline of oratory, a subject on which he was much in demand and about which he had said the same eleven things for thirty years. Sestius, who had been praetor for five months, spoke about the games he would give, at length, in the manner of a man laying the foundation of a monument; and Antonia, who had heard that he had borrowed heavily against his own praetorship and would be ruined by the autumn if the emperor did not see fit to give him a province, said, 'How fortunate that the people love you,' and watched him try to decide whether he had been complimented.

It was during the second course, when the room had loosened, that Hermagoras put down his cup and said, in the tone of a man introducing a small entertainment:

'I am told, Antonia, that you have a doctrine.'

'A doctrine.'

'About the ownership of things.' He smiled around the room, inviting it in. 'They say that when a man of your acquaintance dies, his heirs find that certain objects have been disposed of in ways that the law did not anticipate. They say you hold that a thing belongs to the person who can keep it, rather than to the person whose name is in the will.'

The room went quiet in the particular way a room goes quiet when the quiet is a form of pleasure. Sestius stopped chewing. Naso's hand, on the table, went still.

Antonia ate a piece of fig. She did not hurry.

'That is not a doctrine,' she said. 'That is the law.'

'The law,' said Hermagoras, 'is rather precise about ownership.'

'The law is precise about who wins. It is a set of instructions for settling disputes in favour of the man with the better advocate.' She looked at him with the mild interest she reserved for insects. 'You have spent your life teaching young men to speak well enough to take things from other young men in a court. Would you like to tell me that this is not a doctrine?'

Hermagoras laughed, but his eyes had gone flat. 'You are a philosopher.'

'I am a woman with a house and a great many enemies. The two things make one practical.'

'Practical,' he said. 'Yes. Rome is a practical city.'

'Rome,' said Antonia, 'is a city that has spent four hundred years telling itself that the only true inheritance is blood, and which has been governed, in that time, by men whose grandmothers were shopkeepers. It is not a mystery. It is arithmetic.'

'You are speaking of the Senate.'

'I am speaking of the emperors,' she said, 'and you may make of that what you like.'

There was a silence of a different quality, and Naso, who was a man of business, said comfortably, 'The emperor's grandmother was a shopkeeper?'

'She was a potter's daughter from Spain,' said Antonia. 'She is buried with a very fine inscription that does not mention it.'

'And you think that is a comfort.'

'I think that is a fact,' she said, 'and facts are the only comfort I have ever found reliable.'

---

Between the third and fourth courses she rose and went to the room behind the library, where the girl was sitting on a stool with her hands folded, being quiet and pretty.

Lucilla had been bought in March from a dealer in the Campus Martius for eleven thousand sesterces, which was a great deal for a girl with no training and a voice that had not yet been placed in a body that had finished growing. She had been born in a villa near Narnia, the daughter of a woman who had died of a fever, and had been sold at thirteen by a man who had a legal claim to her that was probably a fiction. Antonia had looked at her for a long time before buying her, because there was a thing you could see in a girl that told you whether she would survive the house or be destroyed by it, and Antonia had seen it in Lucilla, though she could not have explained it, and had not been sure she wanted to see it.

The girl stood up when she came in, and her hands went to her sides and then to her stomach and then, with an effort, to her sides again.

'You are to sing after the fourth course,' Antonia said. 'One song. The one about the swallow. Do you know it?'

'Yes, domina.'

'You will sing it to the room, not to me, and you will not look at any man's face while you sing, and when you have finished you will say nothing at all, not even if they applaud, particularly if they applaud, and you will come back here, and you will stay here until Meno comes for you. In the morning you will be taken to the house of my physician, who will look at your throat and tell you what your voice is.'

The girl's eyes had filled, but she held them level. 'Domina, if one of the men—'

'No one comes to this room.'

'If one of them sends for me—'

'No one sends for you,' Antonia said. 'You are not in the house tonight to be sent for. You are in the house tonight to be heard. In six months, if you are worth it, men will send for you and I will decide, and you will have some small share in that decision, because I am not a monster and because it is better business. But not tonight.' She reached out and pushed a strand of the girl's hair behind her ear, which was an intimacy she rarely permitted herself and which startled them both. 'Tonight you are a voice. Voices are not for sale. Only the evenings are.'

Lucilla's chin trembled, and then did not. She said, 'Yes, domina,' in a different voice from the one she had used before.

Antonia went back to the dining room with something hot and tight behind her breastbone that she recognised, after years of practice, as the last useless remnant of the girl she had been.

---

When the applause had died down and Lucilla had gone back to her room, Hermagoras, who could not bear a silence that he had not made, said, 'You have a painter's eye, Antonia. The ceiling is very fine. Who did it?'

'A man named Febronius,' said Antonia. 'He is sixty. He has a shop in the fourth region and he has not painted a ceiling in nine years, because his hands shake.'

'Then why him?'

'Because eleven years ago he asked me for a small thing in return for a small thing, and I said no, and I have regretted it ever since.' She turned her cup. 'He painted the ceiling on his back in the dark for nine months, and at the end of it he asked whether he might put his own name among the stars — three letters, in a corner, where nobody would ever see them. I told him no. I told him that a ceiling was a thing I had paid for and that a signature on it was a thing he would be giving himself at my expense, which was the kind of remark I used to make when I was thirty and thought that cleverness was the same as being right.'

'And now you have hired him again.'

'And now I have hired him again, and he has his three letters in the fleece of the Ram, and when the room is finished I am going to have a bronze tablet put up in the portico, and it will say who painted the ceiling and who laid the mosaic and who made the lamps, and it will be in the name of the house and not in mine.' She looked at Hermagoras. 'You may make of that what you like. It is not modesty. It is arithmetic. In twenty years there will be men in this room who cannot remember my name and who will read that tablet and know the name of a painter with shaking hands, and that is the only kind of immortality that money can buy, and it is cheaper than a statue.'

'It is a very Roman kind of immortality,' said Naso.

'It is the only kind I have ever wanted,' said Antonia. 'I do not want a statue. A statue is a thing that is put up in a forum and pulled down by the next government. I want a name at the bottom of somebody else's work, in a place where nobody looks, that will still be there in a hundred years when everything above it has come down.'

She said it lightly, and the table laughed, and moved on to the subject of the emperor's building programme, and Antonia sat with her cup and looked at the ceiling and thought about a name in a register that she had written once, at fifteen, on a scrap of wood, and kept in a box with a piece of blue glass.

---

At the fifth hour of the night the party had separated into its elements, as parties do. Hermagoras had retired with two men who wanted to be told that their verses were good. Sestius had gone out to the garden with Vibia Lucana, either to seduce her or to be seduced, and Antonia thought it would do him good to find out which. Celer was asleep, sitting up, with a cup in his hand and the look of a boy doing arithmetic in a dream. And Naso had asked, with a lifted eyebrow, whether there might be somewhere they could speak without being overheard.

They went to the study at the top of the west wing, which was the one room in the house no guest had ever been invited into, and which contained a table, four stools, a strongbox, a lamp, and a wall of pigeonholes full of wax tablets that were not what they looked like.

Naso sat without being asked. Antonia did not.

'The Bithynian,' he said.

'The Bithynian is paid.'

'The Bithynian is the eighth part of paid. You have paid the interest on the interest. I have carried you through the spring on an assurance.'

'The assurance was good.'

'The assurance is still good, or I would not be sitting in your study at the fifth hour of the night.' Naso rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand. 'There is a difficulty, and I would rather you heard it from me than from a man in the street.'

'Say it.'

'Lucius Tarquitius Priscus has been appointed curator of the public works. Not the grain. Not the aqueducts. The works.' He let that sit. 'He will have the oversight of the streets, the water, the building permits, the inspection of private structures that abut public land. He will have the power to require the demolition of anything that encroaches. He will have the power to decide what is an encroachment.'

Antonia stood very still. The lamp guttered and recovered.

'There are four hundred houses in Rome that encroach,' she said. 'Mine is not one of them.'

'Your portico is nine feet into the street on the north side.'

'My portico has a permit.'

'Which will be reviewed,' said Naso, 'by a commission of which he is the head, and which will be composed of men who owe him favours, and which will meet, I am told, in the autumn.' He spread his hands. 'It is a small thing. It is a nothing. But a nothing in the hands of a man who hates you is a lever.'

'He does not hate me,' Antonia said.

Naso looked at her for a moment with an expression that was almost pity, and it was the first time in eleven years of business that she had wanted to hit him.

'He speaks of you in the Senate,' Naso said. 'Not by name. He speaks of the corruption of the city's pleasures, of the houses where men of rank are separated from their money and their judgement, of women who have grown rich on the weaknesses of their betters. His father spoke the same way. It is a family with a long memory and a great deal of land.' He paused. 'Whatever is between the two of you, it is not a small thing, and I would be a fool to pretend that it is, and I have never been that.'

'There is nothing between us.'

'There is nothing between a man who mentions you every year for twenty years and a woman who flinches when his name is spoken in her own house,' said Naso. 'No. Of course. There is nothing at all.'

Antonia walked to the pigeonholes. She put one finger on the edge of a tablet and did not take it down.

'What do you want?' she said.

'I want my two million sesterces on the Kalends of October, or a share of the house, or a name on the deed. I am not a hard man. I am a man with a wife who has begun to ask questions about my patience.'

'You will have your money.'

'From where?'

'From the grain,' she said, 'and from the fact that in six weeks there will be a shortage in the city that no one has noticed yet, and from a woman in Ostia who owes me an introduction I have never spent.'

Naso was quiet. Then he said, 'You will ruin yourself to spite him.'

'I will ruin him to keep myself,' said Antonia. 'It is not the same, though everyone in this city confuses the two.'

He rose, and at the door he turned and said, in a different voice, almost gentle: 'Antonia. When a man like that comes for a woman like you, he does not come for her portico.'

'I know what he comes for.'

'Do you?'

'Good night, Gaius.'

---

The guests left between the sixth and seventh hours. Antonia stood at the gate and received their goodnights and their promises, which were the same thing, and watched the last litter turn into the street with its lamps swinging, and listened to the door being barred behind her. The house exhaled. Somewhere a girl laughed, and someone else told her to be quiet, and a broom began to move across the peristyle, and the whole great machine of the night began the work of putting itself back in order.

She walked the house as she did every night, and the house gave her its accounts.

In the kitchen, the cook's boy had burned his forearm and was being tended by the second cook with a salve and a great deal of scolding. In the corridor by the bath, Prima was sitting on a bench with her sandals in her hand, thirty years old and beautiful in the way a well-made knife is beautiful, and she said, without looking up, 'Sestius put his hand up my skirt in the garden and told me he would have me flogged if I moved.'

'Did you move?'

'I moved,' said Prima.

'Good.' Antonia paused. 'He will not have you flogged. He will not remember it by morning, and if he does, he will remember that he was drunk and you were kind.'

Prima looked up. 'I was not kind.'

'That is between you and the gods.'

In the small room off the library, Lucilla was asleep on the stool with her head against the wall and her mouth open, and Antonia stood in the doorway for a moment, and then took a blanket from the chest and put it over her, and the girl did not wake.

In the passage by the bath she met an old woman carrying a tray of cups, and the old woman stopped and stood aside, and as she did it she put out one hand and steadied herself against the wall.

Antonia knew that gesture. She had made it herself, at fifteen, in a corridor in a villa above the Arno, with a jug in her hands: the small, automatic, self-effacing reach of a slave who has been on her feet for sixteen hours and does not intend to be noticed being tired.

'What is your name?' she said.

'Silvia, domina.'

'How old are you?'

'Sixteen, domina. Perhaps. I was bought at four and I was not told.'

Antonia looked at her: the flat feet, the hands like her own hands had been at that age, the tray held level with the elbow, the eyes down.

'I was in the linen at Arretium when I was your age,' she said. 'Do you know what the linen is?'

'No, domina.'

'It is a room with a table in it and a window of thick green glass, and everything you do in it smells of lye and the sun.' She took a cup off the tray, a good one, rock crystal, and looked at it, and then put it back and put her hand flat on the tray so that the girl had to stop. 'Silvia. Look at me.'

The girl looked up.

'You will be at the door of the dining room at the tenth hour tomorrow,' said Antonia, 'and you will ask to see the atriensis, and you will tell him that I have said you are to be taught to read. He will not believe you. He will come to me and I will confirm it. It will cost me nothing and it will take you two years and it will make you worth four times what you are worth tonight.' She took her hand off the tray. 'And when you are twenty-five and you have a trade, you will remember that a woman took a cup off your tray in a passage and looked at your hands, and you will do it to somebody else, and that will be the whole of the transaction. Do you understand?'

'Yes, domina,' said Silvia, and went on down the passage with the tray, and did not look back, and did not smile, and Antonia stood in the passage for a moment with her hand against the wall.

In the peristyle the fountain had been turned down to a trickle. Neptune stood with his hand up against the dark. Antonia sat on the edge of the basin, which was cool through the linen, and looked at the sky above the roof, which in Rome is never black but a kind of brown glow, the whole city burning its oil, all those millions of people awake or asleep under the same smoke, each of them convinced of the importance of his own affairs.

She thought about the man who had been appointed curator of the public works.

She thought: twenty-four years.

Then Cinnamus came across the garden with a lamp in one hand and a small sealed tablet in the other, and his face was the face he wore when he had decided to be calm and was not.

'Domina. A boy brought this to the gate. He would not wait.'

She took it. The seal was a boar's head, stamped deep into the wax, and beneath it two letters, L. T. P., in the old-fashioned way, as though the man were a consular writing to the Senate and not a senator writing to her.

She broke the seal with her thumbnail. The tablet was thin, the writing small and upright, without ornament.

*L. Tarquitius Priscus to A. Cassia. You will attend my house on the Ides at the ninth hour. Come alone. Bring nothing with you that you cannot afford to lose.*

Antonia sat with it for a long moment. The fountain trickled. Somewhere beyond the wall a cart went by with a wheel that needed grease, and a dog answered it, and then the street was quiet again.

'Cinnamus,' she said.

'Domina.'

'In the morning, find out everything that is known about the household of Lucius Tarquitius Priscus. The slaves, the freedmen, the clients. Who sleeps in the house and who does not. Who is owed money and who is owed favours.' She turned the tablet over in her fingers. 'And find out whether his mother is still alive.'

'His mother, domina?'

'His mother,' said Antonia, 'is the only person in that house who has ever told me the truth.'

She went up to her bed at the seventh hour of the night, and lay in the dark with the tablet on the table beside her, and did not sleep for a long time.
