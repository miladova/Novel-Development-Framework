# 14 | The Secretary

Hilarus found her in six days, which was fast even for him, and he found her by the simplest method available: he asked the woman who had freed her.

It was done in the way that such things are done. A clerk was sent to the house on the Palatine with a letter from the office of the urban prefect, which was true in the sense that the letter was written on paper from that office and signed by a man who worked in it and had been paid; and the letter asked, for the purposes of a matter of inheritance, whether the household could confirm the manumission of a freedwoman named Tertia, formerly of the household at Arretium, freed by the council's leave in the seventh year before the present consulship.

The steward confirmed it. The letter asked, further, for the woman's present address, and the steward, who was seventy-one and careful, replied that the household did not keep the addresses of its freedwomen, which was a lie, and that the woman had been given a sum and a trade and had gone to Rome, which was true.

Hilarus then did the thing that Antonia would not have thought of, which was to go to the council's register.

Every manumission by the council's leave in the city of Rome was entered in a book, and the book had, by law, to record the freedwoman's name, her former master, the sum paid for the leave, and the guarantor. The guarantor in this case was the household of the Tarquitii, and the entry gave an address for the service of any claim: a shop in the Subura, in the fourth region, in the street of the sandalmakers.

The shop belonged to a builder named Gaius Vipsanius Polla, who had a contract with the household at Arretium and had taken the freedwoman on as his bookkeeper seven years before, and who was, Hilarus reported, a decent man with a bad temper and a great deal of work.

'Her name is Tertia Vipsania,' said Hilarus. 'She took his name when she was freed, which freedwomen do. She is twenty-five. She is not married. She lives in two rooms above a bakery on the Vicus Sabuci with another woman, who is a midwife. She keeps the books for Polla's building business and for two shops in the same street, and she has a reputation for being quick and hard and for finding money that other people have lost.' He put the tablet down. 'She has never been in any trouble. She has never been in any court. She has no children and no husband and no debts, and she has saved, in seven years, something over eleven thousand sesterces, which is a great deal for a woman who was freed with nothing.'

Antonia sat with her hands in her lap.

'What does she look like?' she said.

'I have not seen her, domina. I have seen the shop and the bakery and the woman she lives with. I do not go and look at people; that is not my trade.' Hilarus paused. 'I can tell you that she is short. The woman at the bakery says she is short and that she walks fast and that she counts under her breath when she is thinking, which the bakery woman finds funny and I do not, because I have known two men who counted under their breath and both of them were accountants.'

Antonia stood up.

'I want to hire her,' she said.

'That will be difficult.'

'Why?'

'Because she is not a fool,' said Hilarus. 'Because she has a place and a reputation and eleven thousand sesterces, and because a woman like that does not leave a builder who has been fair to her to go and work in a house on the Esquiline three weeks before that house is tried for murder. She will have heard of you. Everyone in the fourth region has heard of you. She will think that you want her for something, and she will be right.'

'Then I will not hire her,' said Antonia. 'My atriensis will hire her. A trial needs a scribe and a copyist; there are eleven boxes of accounts and four hundred tablets and a book of contracts, and the advocate will want them all copied and arranged by the first of November. Cinnamus will go to the builder and offer to buy her time for a month at double her wage, and the builder will agree because he is a man with a bad temper and a great deal of work and no money to refuse.'

Hilarus looked at her.

'And then?'

'And then she will come to this house,' said Antonia, 'and sit in the tablinum, and count under her breath, and I will not be able to say a word to her, because if I say a word to her before I know what I am going to do with the rest of my life, I will ruin her.'

---

Tertia Vipsania came on the eighteenth day of October, at the second hour, with a box of tablets under her arm and a stylus behind her ear.

Antonia watched her come up the hill from the window of the upper room. She had set herself at the window deliberately, because the alternative was to be in the tablinum when the girl came in, and she did not trust her own face.

She was short. She walked fast. She had dark hair pinned up in a way that was not fashionable and was entirely practical, and she wore a plain grey dress and good sandals, and she stopped at the gate and spoke to Meno for a moment, and Meno laughed — Meno, who laughed perhaps twice a year — and then she came in.

Antonia stood at the window with her hand flat against the shutter and watched her daughter cross the atrium.

She had prepared herself. She had prepared herself for twenty-five years, in a way; she had imagined the meeting in a hundred forms, and in every one of them she had been a woman who knew what to say. In none of them had she been a woman of forty-one standing at a shutter on the upper floor, unable to breathe, watching a stranger with a box of tablets being shown into a room by a doorkeeper.

She was not beautiful. That was the first thing. She had a broad, pleasant, entirely ordinary face with a strong jaw and her grandmother's nose and a small mole by the corner of her mouth, and she had a way of looking at a room as she came into it, once, quickly, from side to side, taking the exits, which Antonia had seen in exactly one other person in her life and which was her own.

She was in the tablinum for four hours. At the third hour Antonia went down and stood in the passage outside the door and listened to her voice, which was low and quick and had a Campanian vowel in it, and to Cinnamus's voice, and to the sound of a stylus on wax.

At the fourth hour she went in.

'Domina,' said Cinnamus, and stood. 'This is the copyist, Tertia Vipsania.'

Tertia stood up. She did not look frightened and she did not look impressed. She looked at Antonia the way a person looks at a door they are deciding whether to open.

'Domina,' she said.

'You have been four hours,' said Antonia.

'Nine boxes,' said Tertia. 'Four of them are in the same hand and the other five are in three different hands, and the fourth box has been wet at some point and the tablets have warped, and two of the entries in the seventh box do not balance, and I have written down eleven questions.'

'Ask them.'

'Not in front of the atriensis,' said Tertia. 'He has told me twice that the seventh box does not balance and he is right, but he is wrong about why, and I would rather tell you why when he is not in the room, because he will be embarrassed.'

Cinnamus made a sound that was almost a laugh and gathered his tablets.

'I will be in the kitchen,' he said, 'being embarrassed.'

When the door closed, Antonia sat down.

'Ask them,' she said.

The questions were good. They were better than good; they were the questions of a woman who had spent seven years finding money that other people had lost. She asked why the house had paid a wine merchant twice in the same month, and whether the second payment had been a loan, and if so where the note was. She asked why there were eleven entries for one woman's wages in a year with twelve months in it, and whether the woman had been ill or had been away. She asked about a loan to a name that appeared three times in five years, always in the spring, always the same sum, and never repaid.

'That is a loan to a man who is not going to repay it,' she said, 'and you know he is not going to repay it, and it is in the book as a loan, which means the book is honest but the man is not. Who is he?'

'He is a magistrate's freedman,' said Antonia, 'and the money is not a loan, it is a payment, and it is in the book as a loan because if it were in the book as a payment it would be evidence of something that would put me in a cell.'

Tertia looked at her for a moment.

'Then you should not have written it down at all,' she said.

'Then I would not have known what I had paid.'

'You would have known,' said Tertia. 'You would have remembered. You are the kind of woman who remembers.' She made a small mark on her tablet. 'It is not my business. I will copy it as it stands and I will put a note in the margin that the entry is unexplained, and when the advocate asks me, I will say that I copied it faithfully and that I do not know what it is, and I will be telling the truth.'

Antonia looked at her.

'You are very careful,' she said.

'I am a freedwoman with eleven thousand sesterces and no family,' said Tertia, 'and I have been in this city for seven years, and in seven years I have learned that the only thing that protects a woman like me is being exactly what she says she is.' She put her stylus down. 'May I ask you something, domina? Not about the book.'

'Ask.'

'Why did your atriensis offer my builder double my wage for a month's work?'

'Because a trial needs a copyist.'

'There are forty copyists in this city who would do it for half the money and would not need to be bought out of a contract,' said Tertia. 'I asked him and he said the work was confidential. I have done confidential work before and I have never been bought out of a contract for it; I have been made to swear an oath and I have been paid at the end.' She looked at Antonia steadily. 'So I am asking you, domina, because I would rather ask now than find out in three weeks. Why me?'

The room was quiet. In the atrium a slave went past carrying a basket, and the light from the door moved across the floor.

'Because you were recommended,' said Antonia.

'By whom?'

'By a man who knows the fourth region and who was told that the best bookkeeper in the Subura was a freedwoman called Tertia,' said Antonia, 'and who did not tell me the rest of it, and I did not ask.'

Tertia's face did not change.

'That is an answer,' she said, 'and it is not the whole answer, and I am going to take the work anyway, because the money is good and because I have never in my life been paid double for anything.' She picked up her stylus. 'But I will tell you what I tell everyone I work for. I will keep your books honestly and I will not steal, and I will not lie for you, and if I am asked a question in a court I will answer it, and if you want a woman who will do otherwise you should hire a man.'

'That is what I want,' said Antonia.

'Good,' said Tertia, and went back to the ninth box.

---

She came every day for eleven days.

She came at the second hour and left at the ninth, and she sat in the tablinum with the shutters open and copied four hundred tablets and made a fair copy of the book of contracts in a hand that was small and upright and beautiful, and she ate with the household at the sixth hour because it was easier, and she talked to Eucharis about the price of wool and to Meno about the races and to Cinnamus about the seventh box, and she called Antonia domina.

Antonia watched her.

She watched her eat — she ate quickly, and she left the best part of anything on the plate, which is a habit that people who have been hungry in childhood never lose. She watched her count under her breath. She watched her sit, and the way she sat, with her feet tucked under her and her shoulders a little forward, as though she were protecting her chest. She watched her hands, which were small and square and had a callus on the second finger of the right hand from a stylus, and which were, unmistakably, the hands of the woman watching her.

On the fourth day she heard her say to Eucharis, in the corridor: 'I was raised by a woman called Sabbia, and she was not my mother, and she never pretended to be, and she used to say that a lie told kindly is still a lie and a truth told kindly is worth two.'

Antonia was in the passage at the time. She went into the small room off the library and stood there for a while with her hand over her mouth until she could come out again.

On the fifth day, Prima asked her about it.

They were in the garden at the sixth hour, and Prima was eating bread and olives and watching the tablinum door, and she said, without preamble, 'Who is she?'

'The copyist.'

'The copyist is a freedwoman from the fourth region who has been in this house nine days and has eaten at our table and has made Meno laugh twice, and she has your hands and your way of standing in a doorway, and she counts under her breath the way you do when you are adding a column, and I have watched you watch her for a week and you have the face of a woman looking at something she has lost.' Prima spat an olive stone into her hand. 'So. Who is she?'

Antonia looked at the tablinum door.

'She is my daughter,' she said.

Prima stopped chewing.

'Your daughter.'

'Yes.'

'You have a daughter.'

'I have a daughter,' said Antonia, 'who was born in Puteoli twenty-five years ago and taken from me the same day and sold into the household of the Tarquitii, and I was told she was dead, and I found out in the summer that she was not, and I have known for certain for four days, and she does not know, and she is copying four hundred and eleven tablets for a trial in which I am accused of murder.' She took an olive. 'You asked. That is the answer.'

Prima was silent for a long moment.

'Does she know she is a freedwoman of that household?'

'She knows that. She has the name and the register entry. She does not know the rest of it, and I do not know what she has been told, and I have not asked her, because every question I ask her is a question I have no right to ask until I have decided what I am going to do.'

'Then decide,' said Prima.

'It is not that simple.'

'It is exactly that simple,' said Prima, 'and you are making it complicated because you are afraid, and you have been afraid since August, and in August your fear made you send Berenice away in a closed carriage and hide her, and now it is making you sit in a room for a week watching your daughter copy tablets.' She wiped her hands on her skirt. 'You told me in September to buy a house with a door and a lock and a deed in my own name. I have been looking at houses. There is one on the Aventine, two rooms and a yard, and it costs eleven thousand sesterces, and I am going to buy it, and I am going to do it because you told me to. So I am telling you: that girl is not a house. You cannot buy her, and you cannot put a deed on her, and you cannot keep her safe by watching her from a doorway.'

'I know that.'

'Then do something,' said Prima, and got up, and went in to help with the bread.

---

On the seventh day, Myrrhine said, 'You have not told her.'

'No.'

'You are not going to tell her before the trial.'

'No.'

'And after?'

'After, if I am acquitted, I will tell her in a room with the door shut, and she will have a choice: she may have a mother, or she may have a client, or she may have nothing, and I will give her the money to be nothing if that is what she wants.' Antonia looked at the tablets. 'And if I am convicted, I will not tell her at all. I will go to Massilia and she will never know, and she will be a woman of twenty-five with eleven thousand sesterces and a good name in the fourth region, and she will not have to carry it.'

'That is cowardice,' said Myrrhine.

'Yes.'

'And you are going to do it anyway.'

'Yes.'

Myrrhine stood in the doorway with her arms folded.

'Then I will tell you what I have been doing for eleven days,' she said, 'while you have been watching her hands. I have been watching her too. She has a mark on the back of her left shoulder, and I have seen it, because she washes her hands at the basin in the corridor and her dress slips, and it is a small crescent, the size of a fingernail, and it is a little darker than the rest of her.' She came into the room. 'So there it is. She is your daughter. There is no doubt in the world now, and you have been sitting in this house for a week with her in the next room, and you have not said a word to her except about the seventh box.'

Antonia sat down slowly.

'You are certain?'

'I am certain,' said Myrrhine. 'And Antonia — I am going to say this once and then I am going to keep the accounts. That girl is not a tragedy. Look at her. She is twenty-five and she has a trade and a reputation and a friend who is a midwife and eleven thousand sesterces in a bank, and she did all of it herself, in a city where a freedwoman with no family is a piece of furniture. She is not waiting to be found. She is not a girl in a tower. She is a woman who has been managing without you for twenty-five years, and if you go into that room and tell her that you are her mother, the first thing she will feel is not joy. It will be that her whole life has been a thing that happened to her without her consent.'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'Do you?'

'I have been that woman,' said Antonia. 'I was told my daughter was dead, and it was a lie, and I have spent the whole of this summer being angry about a thing that was done to me without my consent, and now I am about to do the same thing to her.' She looked up. 'That is why I have not said a word. Not cowardice. I am trying to find a way to tell her that does not take anything from her, and I have not found it, and I have a fortnight.'

---

On the ninth day she went into the tablinum at the eighth hour of the evening, when the household was at dinner and the room was empty except for one lamp and one woman copying.

Tertia looked up.

'Domina. The light is bad; I am doing the summary now and I will finish the copying in the morning.'

'Do not stop,' said Antonia, and sat down on the bench by the wall.

Tertia went back to work. The stylus moved. After a while she said, without looking up, 'You have not asked me anything about myself in nine days.'

'Should I have?'

'Everyone asks. The women here have asked me four times where I was born, and the atriensis asked me on the first day whether I had a family, and Meno asked me on the third day whether I had ever been to Thrace, which I have not, and which he asked because he is Thracian and because he has decided that I am a person worth asking.' The stylus moved. 'You have asked me nothing. You have asked me about the seventh box and the wine merchant and the loan in the spring, and you have not asked me a single question about myself, and I have noticed, and I have been trying to decide what it means.'

'What have you decided?'

Tertia put the stylus down.

'I have decided that you already know,' she said.

The lamp guttered. In the dining room someone laughed, and a chair scraped.

'What do I know?' said Antonia.

'That I was bought,' said Tertia. 'That is what people know about me; it is on the register and it is in the household book at Arretium and it is in the mouth of every woman who has ever been unkind to me. I was bought at four days old for two hundred and forty sesterces and entered as the third purchase of the year, and I was given the name Tertia because I was a number in a book, and I was raised by a woman called Sabbia who was kind to me and who died when I was nineteen, and I was freed at nineteen by the council's leave on the instruction of the old mistress, with a sum of four thousand sesterces and a trade.' She said it flatly, the way one recites a thing one has said many times. 'I was told that my mother was a slave of the household who died, and I was told it once, by the steward, when I was fourteen and asked, and he told me not to ask again, and I did not ask again, because I was fourteen and I was property and I had learned what questions cost.'

Antonia sat very still.

'And now?' she said.

'Now I am twenty-five and free and I have eleven thousand sesterces,' said Tertia, 'and I have thought about it for seven years, and I have come to this: whoever she was, she did not sell me. Sabbia told me once, when she was dying and had had a little wine, that I was taken out of a house in Campania in a cloth, and that the cloth had a cross-stitched hem, and that the woman who gave me to her cried.' She picked up her stylus again. 'That is all I have. A cloth with a hem, and a woman who cried, and a name that is a number. It is not much to build a mother out of, and I have stopped trying, because a woman of twenty-five who spends her life imagining her mother is a woman who will be unhappy for the rest of it.' She looked up. 'And you have the face of a woman who knows something. So I will ask you once, domina, and then I will not ask again. Do you know who my mother was?'

Antonia sat in the lamplight with the piece of linen in her sleeve, folded small, with the cross-stitched hem and the brown stain.

'Yes,' she said.

Tertia's hand was quite steady on the stylus.

'Who?'

The door opened. Cinnamus put his head in and said, 'Domina, there is a man from the prefect's office at the gate and he has a summons for the morning, and he says it is to be served tonight because the court sits at the second hour and not the third.'

Antonia stood up.

'Tell him I will come to the gate,' she said. 'Tertia. Not tonight. After the court, on the day it is over, in a room with the door shut, I will tell you everything I know, and I will not ask you for anything in return, and whatever you decide to do with it, I will do as you ask.' She went to the door and stopped. 'One thing only, and then I will not say another word. The woman who cried: she did not sell you. She was sixteen years old and she was told that you were dead, and she has believed it for twenty-five years.'

Tertia sat with the stylus in her hand.

'Sixteen,' she said.

'Sixteen,' said Antonia, and went out to the gate.

---

On the tenth night, Tarquitius came to the house to prepare the case.

He came at the tenth hour, through the garden door, with a rolled copy of the charge and two tablets of notes, and he sat in the study for three hours with Antonia and Cinnamus and went through the whole of it: the box, the physician's report, the dead woman Doris, the witnesses, the order in which they would be called, the four questions he would ask the praetor's physician, and the one question he would not ask, because a question you cannot control the answer to is a gift to the other side.

At the end of the third hour Cinnamus took the tablets away to be copied fair, and Tarquitius stood in the passage with his cloak over his arm, and Tertia came out of the tablinum with the fair copy in her hands and went past them both with her head down and her stylus behind her ear.

Tarquitius watched her go. Then he said, quietly, 'Who is that?'

'My copyist,' said Antonia.

'She has your hands,' he said. 'She has your hands and your way of walking, and she put her head down when she passed me in a way that a copyist does not do; a copyist looks at a senator, because a senator is a man who might pay her.' He turned. 'Antonia. Is that her?'

'Yes.'

He stood very still in the passage with his cloak over his arm.

'She does not know,' said Antonia. 'Not yet. I have told her that I know who her mother was and that I will tell her when the case is over, and she has agreed to wait, and until then she is a copyist in this house and nothing else, and you will not look at her again, and you will not speak to her, and if you come to this house tomorrow night you will come after the ninth hour when she has gone home.'

He looked at the closed door of the tablinum.

'I have a daughter,' he said, 'who is nineteen and has been in my house all her life and who is going to be married in the winter to a fool, and I have a daughter who has been copying tablets in this house for ten days and who has a stylus behind her ear.' He put his hand over his eyes, briefly, and took it away. 'What is her name?'

'Tertia.'

'Tertia,' he said. 'That is a slave's name.'

'It is the name she was given in your household book,' said Antonia, 'because she was the third child bought that year. She has another name now; she took the name of the builder who employed her, which is what freedwomen do. I am not going to tell you which one.'

He nodded slowly.

'In the morning,' he said, 'I am going to stand up in that basilica and I am going to defend you, and there will be a great many men in that court who will be watching me, and some of them will be able to see what I am feeling if I am not careful. So I am going to be very careful, and I am going to win, and then I am going to go home and I am going to sit in my study and I am going to think about the fact that a woman I have despised for twenty-five years has been raising my daughter's wages in a house on the Esquiline.' He went to the garden door. 'Do not tell her anything about me. Not yet. Not until I have spoken to my mother.'

---

On the eleventh day, the day before the trial, Tertia came to her in the study and said, 'The book is finished. Four hundred and eleven tablets copied and arranged by date, and the contract book, and a summary of the seventh box with the eleven questions and my answers where I have them.' She put a bundle on the table. 'I will be here at the second hour tomorrow and I will bring the summary in the fair copy, and I will stand in the court if the advocate wants me to, and I will say what I have done and nothing more.'

'Thank you,' said Antonia.

Tertia did not go.

'Domina,' she said, 'there is a thing I have been going to say for eleven days.'

'Say it.'

'I know who you are,' said Tertia. 'Not who you are to me; I know who you are in this city, everyone does. I mean that I know what this house is and what it has been, and I have been in it for twelve days and I have watched it, and I want to tell you a thing that nobody in this city will tell you, because they are all either afraid of you or obliged to you.' She stood with her box under her arm. 'You are not going to be convicted. Not because you are innocent; I have read your books and you are innocent of nothing in particular. You are not going to be convicted because the men who will judge you have all, at some point, needed something from a woman they did not respect, and they know it, and when they look at you they will see the woman they needed and did not respect, and they will not be able to bear it. That is not justice. It is the closest thing to justice that this city has, and it will be enough.'

Antonia looked at her for a long moment.

'You are a remarkable woman,' she said.

'No,' said Tertia. 'I am a woman who has had to be right about people, because being wrong was expensive.' She went to the door. 'Tomorrow, domina. The second hour.'

She went out. Antonia sat at the table with the four hundred and eleven tablets in their bundles and the light going down, and did not move for a long time.

Then she opened the drawer and took out the piece of linen with the cross-stitched hem and the brown stain, and she folded it small and put it in the sleeve of the dress she would wear in the morning, and went up to bed.
