# 11 | Two Kinds of Silence

The room was above a bookseller's in the Argiletum, and it smelled of glue and dust and the particular sourness of papyrus that has been stored in a damp place.

There was a table, two chairs, a lamp, and a window that looked out on the roof of the shop next door. On the table was a jug of water and a cup, and nothing else, and Antonia noticed that there was only one cup, and understood from it that the person who had arranged the room had been told to arrange it for one person receiving another, and had not been told what the other was.

She was early. She was always early. She sat with her back to the wall and her hands in her lap and waited, and outside in the Argiletum the shops were shutting: the booksellers, the cobblers, the men who sold second-hand furniture, the women who sold flowers and would sell anything else if you asked them in the right way.

He came at the second hour of the night, alone, with a hood over his head like a man going to a brothel, and she had the satisfaction of seeing that he was embarrassed.

'You came,' he said.

'You asked.'

'I did not think you would.'

'Then you do not know me as well as you think you do,' said Antonia, 'which would be a relief to us both.'

He sat down. He looked at the jug and the single cup, and then he got up and went to the door and opened it and said something to someone on the stairs, and came back, and sat down again.

'There is a boy on the landing who will whistle if anyone comes up,' he said. 'The bookseller has been paid. The bookseller thinks I am a senator who is buying a library from a widow, and he thinks you are the widow, and he will not be surprised by anything, because booksellers never are.'

'You have done this before.'

'I have done a great many things before,' said Tarquitius. 'I am fifty-three.'

---

Before that, there had been eleven days.

The house was closed, which meant that the gate was barred at the ninth hour and that Meno sat in the doorway with his stick across his knees, and that no one came in or out except by the garden door, and that the women did not go to the market or the baths or the temple. It meant that the street outside, which had always been a street, became a thing that was watched: the neighbours on the corner, the woman who sold chickpeas, two men who stood at the fountain at the fourth hour and again at the seventh and did not buy anything.

On the second day the first of the women left.

Her name was Chloe, she was twenty-eight, she had been in the house four years, and she came to Antonia in the study with her hands folded and said that her sister lived at Ostia and that she would like to go and stay with her until the winter. Antonia gave her the money that was owed to her and a month over and a letter to a shipping agent at Ostia, and Chloe kissed her hands, and cried, and was in a cart on the Ostian road by the fourth hour.

'She will not come back,' said Myrrhine.

'No.'

'And two more will go by the end of the week, and one of them will go to a man who has been asking her for a year, and she will tell herself that she is doing it for the house.'

'Yes,' said Antonia. 'And I will give her a dowry, and she will send me a note at the Saturnalia, and in three years she will have two children and a husband who is not unkind, and she will tell them that she once worked in a house on the Esquiline and they will not believe her.' She looked at the accounts. 'Open the door for them, Myrrhine. All of them. Anyone who wants to go. Tonight, before they have to ask.'

'And if all of them go?'

'Then I will be a woman of forty-one with an empty house and a trial in November,' said Antonia, 'and I will still have the price of a good advocate.'

---

Cinnamus came on the third day with the books and the news that the praetor's household had been to the tabularium.

'They have asked for the record of the sale of the woman Berenice, and for the record of the purchase of the girl Lucilla in March, and for the record of the manumission of the woman Eucharis, which is nine days old and which they found, and which has made them very interested indeed.' He put the tablet down. 'They have also asked for a copy of the will of Gaius Cassius Verus, which is your father-in-law's, and which is a matter of public record and which contains nothing that could help them, and which they have asked for because a man who is building a case asks for everything and sees what the clerks bring him.'

'What do they want with the manumission?'

'I do not know, domina. I have thought about it for three days.' Cinnamus sat down without being asked, which he did when he was worried. 'The charge will be that the house is a place of vice. If the women are slaves, the vice is yours, because you own them and you profit. If the women are free and paid, then the vice is theirs, and you are a woman who keeps a house where free women choose to work, which is not a crime in law, and which they know is not a crime in law.' He rubbed his jaw. 'So they will use the manumission the other way. They will say that you freed her eleven days ago because you knew a charge was coming, and that the freeing of a woman who was in the house on the night the boy died is evidence that you are hiding something. It does not matter what the something is. In a court, a woman who does an unusual thing at an unusual time has done it for a reason, and the reason is always the one the prosecutor supplies.'

'Then we will supply a reason first,' said Antonia. 'We will produce the contract she signed and the sum she paid and the date on the tablet, and we will produce three other women who have bought their freedom in the last four years, and we will produce the book, Cinnamus. The whole book. Every woman in this house for eleven years, and what she was paid, and what she paid, and where she went. All of it, in front of the court.'

Cinnamus was silent.

'Domina,' he said, 'the book has things in it that you will not want said aloud in a basilica. There are men's names in that book. There is the man who came in the ninth year and paid for the girl Myrtale to be sent away and the money came through a third party and the third party was a senator's freedman. There is the loan to the wife of a magistrate. There is —'

'There is everything,' said Antonia. 'And if I produce the book, half the Senate will be in that basilica by the second day, not to hear the case but to find out what is in it, and every one of them will be a man who has something to lose, and they will not be able to stop the reading and they will not be able to leave.' She put her hands flat on the table. 'A woman with a book like that does not need an advocate. She needs a court that is afraid of her. And the only way to be feared is to show, once, in public, that you are willing to burn the whole of it.'

'And if they convict you anyway?'

'Then I will be convicted in a city where half the men on the bench know that their own names are in my book,' said Antonia, 'and I will be sent into exile, and I will go, and I will write letters. But they will not convict me. They will acquit me and they will hate me for having made them need to.'

Cinnamus looked at her for a long moment.

'You have changed this summer,' he said.

'Have I?'

'When I came to this house eleven years ago you were a woman who won by knowing things. Now you are a woman who wins by being willing to lose.' He picked up the tablet. 'I do not know whether that is better. It is certainly more frightening.'

---

Lucilla came on the fifth day.

She had been in the small room off the library since the morning of the death, and she had not eaten properly, and she came in with her hands twisting in her skirt and said, 'Domina, I want to go.'

Antonia put down her stylus.

'Where?'

'To my aunt. In Narnia. She is a free woman, she married a smith, she has a house with a yard. I have not seen her since I was thirteen and she will not know me, but she is the only family I have, and I have written to her —' she faltered '— I have written to her twice, and Cinnamus sent the letters, and there is no answer, and I would like to go anyway.'

'You think it is your fault.'

'It is my fault,' said Lucilla. 'He came to the house because of me. He came in May because of me, and he came in August because of me, and a man is dead and the house is closed and Chloe has gone and Berenice has been sent away and there are men in the street watching the door, and all of it is because a man heard me sing.'

Antonia stood up and came around the table.

'Lucilla,' she said. 'Look at me. In May, in this room, I told you that you were not in the house to be sent for, and that you were a voice, and that voices were not for sale. Do you remember?'

'Yes.'

'I told you that because it was true, and because I have been in this business for more than a decade and I know what a girl is worth and what she is not, and I have never once been wrong about it.' She put her hand under the girl's chin and lifted it, which she had done once before, in May. 'What happened in this house happened because a man of twenty-two who had never been refused anything in his life was refused, and because his father had spent twenty years telling him that the world was arranged for his convenience. It did not happen because you sang. It happened because he listened, and because what he heard when he listened was not a voice but a price. That is not your doing. That is a thing that was done to you, twice, and if you go to Narnia and spend the rest of your life thinking that you killed a man by singing, then he will have done it to you a third time, and I will not have it.'

Lucilla was crying, and she was angry, and both at once.

'I am not a —' she said, and stopped.

'You are not a what?'

'I am not one of the women,' said Lucilla. 'I am not Eucharis. I cannot do what she does. I cannot sit at a table and have a man put his hand on my knee and smile. I have tried, in my room, to practise the smile, and I cannot do it, and I am seventeen and I have a voice and no other thing in the world, and if you close this house in January I will have a voice and a room above a laundry.' She wiped her face with the back of her hand. 'So I want to go to Narnia, and I want to go before I am old enough to be told that I am being ungrateful, and I want you to give me a letter to my aunt saying that I am honest, because she will not believe me otherwise, and I want —' She stopped. 'I want to be somebody's niece.'

Antonia stood in front of her for a long moment.

'Go and pack,' she said. 'You will go on the twenty-sixth with Cinnamus, and you will have the letter, and you will have a purse with enough in it to be respectable and not enough to be robbed, and you will not sing in the inns on the road, and if your aunt will not have you, you will go to the magistrate of the town and show him the letter and the purse, and he will find you a place, because a letter from me is worth something even in Narnia.' She went back to the table and sat down. 'And Lucilla. When you are twenty-five, and you have a house with a yard, and a husband who is a smith and does not know what a basilica is, you will remember this summer. Not the boy. The summer. And you will decide what to tell your daughter, and whatever you decide will be right, and I will not be there to see it.'

Lucilla went out with her hand over her mouth, and Myrrhine, who had been standing in the doorway for the last part of it, came in and said, 'You are sending her away without a fee.'

'She is not a fee. She is a girl with a voice and no mother.' Antonia picked up her stylus. 'And if I kept her, she would be worth thirty thousand by the winter, and in four years she would be a woman who had learned the smile, and I would have made her into something that could be sold, and I have done that to enough people.'

---

He began with the case, because a lawyer begins with the case.

'The tablets came from Naso on the ninth day of August and they are in the prefect's strongbox and they will be produced in October. I have read them. There are three receipts with Sestius's brother's name on them and a fourth that is in a different hand but has his seal.' He turned the cup around. 'The prosecution will succeed. Atilius will be exiled and his property will be assessed at four times the amount extorted and the province will be told that Rome has noticed. That is what will happen, and it will happen because you told a banker to open a box.'

'And?'

'And in the same week that I stand up in the Senate and open that case, a charge will be laid against you. It has already been drafted. Sestius has three men working on it and one of them is a jurist of the second rank who owes me money, and it will allege that Marcus Sestius Cirrus died in your house of poison administered by your household, that the woman who was with him has been secreted away by you to avoid examination, and that your house is a place of habitual vice in which men of rank are drugged and robbed. It will ask for the closure of the house, the sale of the women, and your exile from Italy.'

Antonia did not move.

'On what evidence?' she said.

'On the evidence of a physician who will swear that the symptoms of Spanish fly and the symptoms of a vegetable poison are indistinguishable to a man who wishes them to be. On the evidence of the box, which will be produced by Sestius as a thing found in your house, and which is in his possession and not yours. On the evidence of the slave Doris, who will say that she was sold out of your household for knowing too much. And on the evidence of a girl who will be found at Tibur, because Sestius's men will find her, because a physician's sister at Tibur is not a secret; she is a woman with a house and a neighbour and a cook.'

'Then I will produce her myself,' said Antonia. 'I will produce her in the court and she will tell them what she told me, and they will see a girl of nineteen who has been in the house a year and who did not run away, and they can make of it what they like.'

'You will produce her and she will be cross-examined for two hours about what she did in that room and what she did not do, and by the end of it, whatever the verdict, she will be a woman that no house will take and no man will marry, and she will know that it happened because she was in the wrong room on the wrong night.' He looked up. 'You should have kept her in the house. That is what a lawyer would have done.'

'Yes,' said Antonia. 'A lawyer would.'

There was a small silence.

'I am not reproaching you,' said Tarquitius. 'I am telling you what will happen.'

'I know what will happen. I have known since the sixth hour of that morning.' She leaned back. 'You have not brought me here at the second hour of the night to tell me what will happen. You have brought me here to tell me what you will do about it, and you are going to do it as though it were a favour, and I am going to have to be grateful, and we are both going to pretend that this is the first time.'

He looked at her for a long moment.

'I will appear for you,' he said.

'You will not.'

'I will appear for you,' said Tarquitius, 'and it will be the most damaging thing I have ever done to myself, and I will do it, and you will not be grateful, and I will not want you to be. There is no other advocate in Rome who can take that case and win it, and you know it, and I know it, and if I do not do it you will be convicted in November and your house will be sold in December and the women will be sold in January, and in February I will be a man who could have prevented it and did not, and I have been that man before.' He stopped. 'I have been that man before.'

The lamp guttered. In the street below someone was arguing about a price.

'On what terms?' said Antonia.

'Two.'

'Say them.'

'The first is that you close the house.' He held up his hand. 'Not now. In January, when it is over. You will keep the building, you will keep your fortune, you will keep every one of the women as a freedwoman with a contract or a marriage portion or a trade, and you will close the doors, and you will become a woman of business and no more, and in five years no one will remember that there was ever a fountain in the peristyle.'

'And the second?'

'The second is that you tell me what happened in my father's house,' said Tarquitius.

Antonia sat very still.

'You know what happened in your father's house,' she said.

'I know what I did. I was there for that.' His voice was flat and did not change. 'I know what I was told afterwards, and I have spent twenty-five years deciding whether to believe it, and I have not decided, and I am fifty-three and I have a daughter who has begun to ask questions and a mother who has stopped answering them, and I would like to be told by the only other person who was there.' He looked at her. 'That is my price. The house, and the truth. You will not like either of them, and I am not asking because I think you owe me. I am asking because the alternative is to spend the rest of my life in a room with a cupboard in it.'

Antonia looked at the table.

'There are two kinds of silence,' she said.

'What?'

'In a house. There are two kinds of silence, and I have lived in both of them, and I can tell them apart at fifty paces.' She turned the cup around, once. 'The first is the silence of a thing that everyone knows and nobody says. It is thick. It has a shape. You can walk around it. It makes people careful in doorways. That was the silence in your father's house after the funeral, and it is the silence in that house now, and it is the silence of your mother at dinner when your daughter asks what happened.'

'And the second?'

'The second is the silence of a thing that only one person knows,' said Antonia. 'It is thin. It has no shape. It is the silence of a woman who is in a room with eleven people who believe something about her that is not true, and who has decided that the truth would cost more than the lie. There is no walking around it. You carry it. It is not heavy; that is the strange thing. It is light and it never goes away.' She looked up. 'I have lived in the second kind for twenty-five years. You have lived in the first. You do not know what I know, and I do not know what you were told, and if we begin to tell each other now, at this table, in this room, we will get about four sentences in before one of us says something that cannot be taken back, and then we will spend the rest of our lives being right.'

'Then say the four sentences.'

'No.'

'Why not?'

'Because you have not asked me as a man,' said Antonia. 'You have asked me as a jurist. You have offered me a defence and a closing of the doors and a future in which no one remembers the fountain, and in exchange you want the truth, and the truth is not a thing that is bought with a favour. If I tell you, I will have sold it, and I have sold a great many things in my life, and I have never yet sold that.'

Tarquitius sat back.

'You think I am buying it,' he said.

'I think you are a man who has bought everything he has ever had, including his reputation, which he bought with thirty years of being correct, and I think you do not know how to ask for a thing without offering something in return, and I think that when you were twenty-nine you did the same thing to a girl in a linen room and you called it looking after her.' She stood up. 'I will take the first term. I will close the house in January, and I will do it whether you win the case or lose it, because you are right that it is finished, and because I have been running it for eleven years and I am tired, and because I have found something this summer that is worth more to me than the fountain. I will not take the second. Not tonight. Not like this.'

He rose too.

'Then tell me one thing,' he said, 'and I will not ask for more.'

'Ask.'

'Did you take money from my father's house?'

Antonia's hands were cold. She had not expected that; she had expected the child.

'What?'

'When you left,' said Tarquitius. 'There was a sum missing from the household accounts that year. Not a great deal; four thousand sesterces, perhaps, in the account of the steward, in the month you were sent to Campania. My sister found it when she went through the books after my father died and she wrote to me in Rome about it, and I have had that letter in my strongbox for twenty-five years, and I have never once been able to decide what it means.' His face was quite still. 'Did you take it?'

The room was very quiet. Below, in the street, the flower sellers had gone home and the last of the shutters had been closed.

'No,' said Antonia.

'Then who did?'

'I do not know.' She heard her own voice as though it were somebody else's. 'I have never seen four thousand sesterces in one place in my life until I was twenty-six years old. When I left that house I had the clothes I was standing in and a lock of hair and a clay figure, and I was put into a cart at the fourth hour of the morning by a steward who would not look at me, and I was given a piece of bread by a kitchen woman who wept, and I have never in twenty-five years been able to account for the fact that I was sold at Puteoli for two thousand four hundred when I was worth four.' She stopped. 'Four thousand missing. Two thousand four hundred paid. Somebody in that house made a profit on me, and it was not me, and I have had twenty-five years to think about who it was and I have a name, and I am not going to say it in this room, tonight, to you.'

Tarquitius was looking at her with an expression she had never seen on his face before.

'What name?' he said.

'Not tonight.'

'Antonia—'

'Not tonight,' she said, 'and not from me, and not in a room above a bookseller's with a boy on the landing. You have a mother who is seventy-eight and who has been sitting in that house for twenty-five years with the first kind of silence, and if you want to know what happened in your father's house you should go home and ask her, and she will tell you, and then you will know why I will not.' She went to the door. 'Do not follow me down the stairs. The bookseller thinks I am a widow and I would like him to go on thinking it.'

She was on the landing, with the boy standing against the wall, when he said, from the doorway: 'I was told the child died.'

Antonia stopped.

The boy on the landing looked at her, and then at the floor, and then at his own hands, and he was fifteen and he had been paid to whistle and he had heard something he was not supposed to hear and he was trying not to breathe too loudly.

'Who told you?' said Antonia.

The silence went on too long.

'My sister wrote to me,' said Tarquitius. 'In September. She wrote that you had been delivered of a dead child at Puteoli and that you had gone, and that the household had done what was necessary, and that I was not to write to you or to speak of it again, and that our father had died in the spring and that the family could not survive another scandal in the same year.' His voice was very quiet. 'I believed her.'

'You believed her,' said Antonia.

'I was thirty years old and I was in Rome and my father was dead and my sister was the only person in the world who was managing that estate, and I was a man who had been correct all his life, and she wrote me a letter that was correct.' He came out onto the landing. 'I have believed it for twenty-five years.'

Antonia stood with her hand on the rail.

'She was alive,' she said. 'She cried. I heard her cry, and I was told that it was the door, and for twenty-five years I have been told that it was the door, and this summer I found out that it was not.'

Tarquitius did not move.

'What?' he said.

'I have found her,' said Antonia. 'Not her. A paper. A dealer's book. She was sold, at four days old, in your name, to a woman who paid two hundred and forty sesterces, and she was entered in the household lists at Arretium as a foundling, and she has been in your family's house ever since. She may be there still. She may be in this city. I do not know where she is, and I have a man looking, and I am telling you this tonight because you have just told me that you did not know, and because if you go home and ask your mother, she will begin to cry, and she has not cried in forty years, and you should be prepared.'

Tarquitius put his hand out and found the door frame.

'In my house,' he said.

'In your house.'

'For twenty-five years.'

'I do not know that. I know she was sold into it. I know she was entered in the lists. I do not know what became of her after that, and I am not going to invent it for you.' Antonia went down two steps and then stopped and looked up at him. 'You asked me for the truth and this is what it costs. Now you have it, and it is not four sentences and it is not a bargain and it will not close a cupboard; it will open one. And there is one thing I want from you in return, and it is not for me.'

'What?'

'When you find her,' said Antonia, 'if she is alive and if she is in that house, you will not tell her who I am. Not without asking me. You will not go to her with your father's name and your name and your family's guilt and put it on her in an afternoon. She has had twenty-five years of being a number in a list. If she is to be told, she will be told by a woman, in a room, with the door shut, and it will be me.'

He looked down at her from the top of the stairs, and there was something in his face that she had wanted to see for twenty-five years, and now that she saw it she found that it gave her no pleasure at all.

'I will not tell her,' he said.

'Swear it.'

'I swear it,' said Tarquitius, 'by the gods of my house and my father's name.' He was silent a moment. 'Antonia. If she is in that house —'

'Go home,' said Antonia, 'and ask your mother.'

---

She walked home. She would not take a litter; she had come in a litter and she sent it away and walked up the hill in the dark with Brennus ten paces behind her, and the streets were quiet and the lamps were low and the whole city smelled of the end of August.

At the top of the Vicus Cyprius she stopped.

'Brennus.'

'Domina.'

'You heard all of it.'

'I heard nothing, domina.'

'You heard all of it,' said Antonia, 'and you will hear nothing, and one day, if you are ever asked, you will say that you were walking ten paces behind me and that the street was noisy.' She looked down the hill at the lights. 'Brennus, do you have children?'

'Two, domina. In Lugdunum. I was sold away from them when the eldest was six.'

'I am sorry.'

'It was a long time ago,' said Brennus, 'and I have been free for nine years, and I have written twice, and there is no answer, and I have decided that no answer is an answer.' He shifted his stick from one hand to the other. 'If you will permit me, domina. The man on the stairs tonight — he is not a bad man. I have known bad men. I was sold by a bad man and I have worked for two, and I know the smell. He is a weak man who has been told he is strong, and that is worse in a magistrate and better in a father.'

'Is it?'

'Yes, domina,' said Brennus. 'A bad man would have gone home and burned the letter. He went home to ask his mother.'

---

At the house, Myrrhine was waiting up with a lamp and a face like a wall.

'Well?'

Antonia sat down on the bench in the peristyle and put her hands over her face, which she had not done in eleven years, and which she did not do for long.

'He will appear for me,' she said. 'In the winter. He will take the case.'

Myrrhine sat down beside her.

'And what did he want?'

'The house closed, and the truth, and he offered me a bargain and I refused it, and then he gave me the truth for nothing and I gave him mine, and now we are two people who know what the other one knows and neither of us can undo it.' She took her hands down. 'Myrrhine. He did not know. He was told the child died. His sister wrote to him in September and told him the child died and that I had gone, and he believed it, and he has believed it for twenty-five years.'

Myrrhine was silent for a long moment.

'And what did you believe?' she said.

'That he had ordered me sold.'

'And now?'

'Now I believe that his sister ordered me sold, and that she wrote him a letter, and that the letter is in a strongbox in a house on the Palatine, and that I have spent half my life hating a man for a thing his sister did.' Antonia looked at the fountain. 'And I find that I do not feel better. I feel as though somebody has moved the furniture in a dark room and I have been walking into it for twenty-five years and now I know where everything is and I still cannot see.'

Myrrhine took her hand.

'Then we will light a lamp,' she said.

'It is not that simple.'

'Nothing is that simple,' said Myrrhine, 'and I have been in this house for twenty-two years and I have never once seen you ask for help, and you have just done it, and I am going to take it as a good sign and not as an omen.' She stood. 'Come to bed. Tomorrow you have to write to Tibur and find out whether the girl is still there, and then you have to decide whether to produce her or hide her, and then you have to be seen in the Forum looking as though nothing has happened, and you cannot do any of it on two hours' sleep.'

Antonia stood up.

'Myrrhine.'

'Mm.'

'On the twenty-ninth I am going to Puteoli.'

'I know.'

'I want you to come with me.'

Myrrhine stopped with the lamp in her hand. Then she said, 'All right,' and went up the stairs, and did not look back, and Antonia followed her, and the house settled behind them, and outside in the street a dog barked twice and was quiet.
