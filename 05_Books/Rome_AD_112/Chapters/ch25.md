# 25 | The Name

On the third day of May, Tertia Vipsania went to the temple of Castor to have her name changed, and she took Antonia with her.

They went in the morning, on foot, up the Vicus Tuscus and into the Forum, and they went the long way round because Tertia said that she was not going to arrive at the most important morning of her life by the shortest route.

The temple of Castor was where the registers were kept, in a room behind the cella with a long counter and four clerks and a great many boxes, and the whole of it smelled of dust and ink and the beeswax they used on the tablets. There were eleven people in the queue. The queue was made up of freedmen and freedwomen and one man with a goat, and everybody in it was patient, because a queue at a registry office is the most patient thing in the world.

'What do I say?' said Tertia.

'You say: "I am a freedwoman of the household of the Tarquitii, freed by the council's leave in the seventh year before the present consulship, and I ask to be entered under the name Metrodora, which was my mother's name."'

'And then?'

'And then he will ask you why, and you will say that it is your name and that you are a free woman and that you may be called what you like. And then he will look at you as though you have asked him to give you a horse, and then he will do it, because it is the law and because the fee is eleven sesterces.'

The clerk was a thin man of fifty with ink on his fingers. He listened to Tertia, and he looked at the old register, and he looked at her, and he said, 'Metrodora is a Greek name.'

'It was my mother's name.'

'It is not a Roman name.'

'I am aware of that,' said Tertia.

'It will be entered as a Greek freedwoman's name and the tax will be the same and the entries in the margin will say —'

'The entries in the margin may say whatever the law requires them to say,' said Tertia, 'and my name will be Metrodora.'

The clerk looked at her over his tablets. Then he looked at Antonia, who was standing behind her with her hands folded, and something in his face changed, because he was a clerk in the temple of Castor and he had been in that room for twenty years and he knew exactly who she was.

He wrote it in.

It took a quarter of an hour. He made the entry in the register in his careful hand: *Metrodora, freedwoman, formerly of the household of the Tarquitii at Arretium, freed by the council's leave in the seventh year before the consulship of the emperor Caesar Nerva Traianus Augustus, entered this day at her own request under the name Metrodora, born the twenty-ninth day of August in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Corbulo, of a slave mother of the household of the Tarquitii, father unknown.*

'Father unknown,' said the clerk.

'Father unknown,' said Metrodora.

He sanded it and closed the tablet and took the fee and gave her the receipt, and said, in the voice of a man who has done his duty and would like to be done with it, 'Next.'

They came out of the temple into the Forum, and Tertia — Metrodora — stood on the steps with the receipt in her hand.

'I have a birthday,' she said. 'I have a birthday and a name and a mother, and my mother's name is written in the register of the Roman people in the temple of Castor, and in a hundred years anybody who wants to will be able to find it.' She was crying, and she was not doing anything about it. 'It cost nine sesterces.'

'Everything costs eleven sesterces in that room,' said Antonia. 'It is the only honest institution in the city.'

'Do not make a joke. I am going to cry in the Forum and I would like you to stand here and let me.'

Antonia stood there and let her.

When it was over, Metrodora wiped her face with the back of her hand and said, 'I am not going to be Metrodora Cassia. I want you to know that. I am not taking your name.'

'I did not ask you to.'

'I know. That is why I am telling you. I have thought about it for four months and I have decided that I am not going to be the daughter of Antonia Cassia in any document anywhere, because in ten years every man in this city will say that I got my school because of you, and it will not be true, and I will not be able to prove it.' She put the receipt in her sleeve. 'I am going to be Metrodora, and I am going to keep the builder's books and teach three girls their letters in the back room, and in five years I am going to have a school with my own name over the door, and it will be a school for slave girls and freed girls and the daughters of shopkeepers, and we will teach them to read and to add and to write a contract, and every one of them will be able to look at a piece of paper and know what she is signing.' She looked at Antonia. 'That is what I am going to do with my life. I am telling you before I have done any of it, which is not like me, and I am telling you because I want one person in the world to know that I decided it and that I decided it on a step in the Forum with a receipt in my hand.'

Antonia looked at her.

'You will need four hundred thousand sesterces,' she said, 'for a lease and tables and stools and styluses and the first three years of a teacher's wage. You will need an advocate to draw the deed of the school and an introduction to the guild of the sandalmakers for the lease, and you will need a woman who has kept accounts for eleven years to tell you that four hundred thousand is what it will cost and not one sesterce less.'

Metrodora was silent.

'I am not offering you money,' said Antonia.

'I know.'

'I am telling you what it costs, which is what I would tell any woman who came to my study with a plan.' She turned and looked across the Forum. 'And if you ever want it — the four hundred thousand, at no interest, with a deed in your own name and mine nowhere on it, and a clause that says I have no claim on you or on the school and that you may tell me to take it back at any time — then you will come to the house on the Aventine and ask, and I will give it to you, and I will not mention it again and I will not come to see the school unless you ask me.' She started down the steps. 'That is all. That is the whole of it. Now come and eat something; it is the third hour and you have not eaten since yesterday.'

The empress sent for her in June.

The message came through a freedman of the palace in the morning, and it said that the Augusta wished to speak with Antonia Cassia on a matter concerning the maintenance of girls, and that a litter would be sent at the fourth hour, and that the matter was not confidential but that the Augusta would prefer it if the visit were not discussed in the city until it had been decided.

Antonia went in her dark blue dress, with the carnelian ring, and was taken through the palace by a corridor that was not the public one, and into a room with a garden on the Palatine that she had never seen and would never see again.

Pompeia Plotina was perhaps fifty. She was not beautiful and had never been, and she had a face that was entirely without malice, which was the most unnerving thing about her; a woman in Rome who has no malice is either a fool or something the city has no word for.

'Domina,' she said. 'Sit down. There is wine and there is water and I am told that you do not drink in the morning, which is sensible and which I have never managed.'

Antonia sat.

'You have been in the city this year,' said Plotina, 'in a manner which has been discussed in this house a great deal. I do not intend to discuss it. I intend to ask you to do some work.'

'What work?'

'The emperor has decided that the state should maintain children,' said the empress. 'Not all children; there is no money for all children, and the Senate would not permit it. There will be a fund, endowed from the treasury and from the emperor's own purse, and it will pay a monthly sum to the guardians of boys and girls in the towns of Italy — four hundred towns, to begin with — and the sum will be small and the point is not charity. The point is that a child who is maintained by the state is a child who does not have to be sold, and a town that has four hundred of those children is a town that will give the emperor a great deal of money in taxes for forty years.'

'It is a very good scheme,' said Antonia.

'It is a very good scheme and it will fail,' said Plotina, 'for a reason that no one in this palace will say out loud, which is that the men who administer it will steal it. Not the great men; the clerks. A fund of that size passing through four hundred towns and eleven thousand hands will lose a third of itself in the first year to men who tell themselves that they are taking a fee for their trouble, and the emperor will be told in the fifth year that the scheme does not work and that the provinces are ungrateful, and it will be quietly ended.' She put her cup down. 'I have read your file. I have read the Acta of the trial and the report of the prefect's office on the fire, and I have had four conversations with people who know you, one of whom is my own physician, who says that you kept a fire from crossing a street by telling four hundred frightened people where to stand.'

'I was not the only one at that fire,' said Antonia.

'No,' said Plotina. 'But you were the one who did it, and I have been in this palace for fourteen years and I know the difference between a person who is in a crowd at a disaster and a person who makes the crowd into a chain.' She leaned forward. 'I am not offering you a title. There is no title for this; there is no office in the state that a woman may hold, and I am not going to invent one, because the Senate would spend a year on it and the emperor has a war to finish. I am offering you a private commission, paid from my own purse, to travel to eleven towns in Italy in the next two years and look at the accounts of the fund and report to me in writing, and to train the clerks, and to tell me which of the men I have appointed are stealing, with evidence, so that I may have them removed without a scandal.'

Antonia sat with her hands in her lap.

'Why me?' she said. 'There are a hundred equestrians in this city who can read an account and who would be honoured by the commission.'

'Because they would be honoured by it,' said Plotina, 'and you would not.' She turned her cup. 'I have been the wife of an emperor for fourteen years, domina, and I have learned that a person who is honoured by a commission is a person who can be managed by the threat of losing it. You have lost a house and a business and a reputation and a trial this year, and you are still here, and there is nothing I can take from you that you have not already given away, and that means that I can trust you, which is a thing I cannot say about a single man in this palace.'

'That is the most frightening compliment I have ever been paid,' said Antonia.

'Yes,' said Plotina. 'Will you do it?'

Antonia looked out at the garden.

'On four conditions,' she said. 'The first is that I am paid a wage and not a favour, and that the wage is entered in a book. The second is that I choose my own clerk, and that he is a freedman of my household and not a man of yours. The third is that I report to you in writing and that my reports are kept, and that if I am removed the reports are not destroyed. The fourth —'

'Yes?'

'The fourth is that when I find a man who is stealing from a fund for orphan children,' said Antonia, 'I will not be required to be discreet about it. I will give you the evidence and a month to act, and if nothing is done in a month I will take it to the urban prefect myself, and I will not ask your permission, and I will not be sorry.'

Plotina looked at her for a long moment.

'Agreed,' she said. 'All four. And domina — the fourth condition is why I sent for you, and if you had not asked for it I would have found a way to require it.' She stood up. 'There is one thing more, and it is not a condition. The emperor is going to the east in the autumn. He will not be back in this city for two years, perhaps three, and while he is away the men in this palace will be extremely brave. I would like to know that there is one woman in Rome who is reading the accounts.'

---

The first of the seventh-day dinners was on the seventh of May, and it was a disaster, and it was the best evening Antonia had spent in years.

The house on the Aventine had three rooms and a courtyard, and the courtyard held a table for six if nobody moved their elbows. Myrrhine had made the food herself because the household no longer had a cook, and it was a fish and a great deal of bread and a dish of lentils that she had made too quickly and that was, she said, a disgrace.

Tarquitius arrived at the ninth hour with four jars of wine and a face like a man going to a tribunal.

He stood in the doorway of the courtyard with the jars and said, 'Domina.'

'Do not call me domina in this house,' said Antonia. 'It is a courtyard with a fig tree in it and there are five people at that table and one of them is a freedwoman who will not be polite to you.'

'I am aware,' said Tarquitius, and came in.

Myrrhine was not polite to him. She was not rude either, which was worse; she was brisk, and she gave him a stool that wobbled, and when he complimented the lentils she said, 'They are not good, they are too quick, and you are a senator and you know it,' and he said, 'I am a senator and I have eaten in eleven provinces and they are too quick and they are not bad,' and Myrrhine looked at him for a moment and then said, 'There is salt on the shelf behind you,' and that was the end of the war.

Vibia Lucana came, and Marcellina, and Metrodora came late, at the second hour, when they had already begun, because she had been working.

She came into the courtyard in her working dress with a stylus behind her ear and stopped when she saw who was at the table.

Tarquitius put down his cup and stood up.

They looked at each other across the courtyard. Nobody said anything. Myrrhine, who could have said something, did not; she got up and went to the shelf and made a business of the salt.

'I am Metrodora,' said Metrodora.

'I know,' said Tarquitius. 'I am —'

'I know who you are.' She came into the courtyard and sat down in the empty place, which was next to him, because there was nowhere else, and put her box on the ground, and said, 'There is no wine in my cup.'

Tarquitius poured it.

He poured it very carefully, and his hand was not steady, and he did not say anything else at all for a quarter of an hour, and Metrodora ate her fish and answered Vibia's questions about the price of papyrus, and then, at the end of the fish, she turned to him and said, 'You moved an aqueduct.'

'I did.'

'Why?'

'Because it could be moved,' said Tarquitius, 'and because I am the curator of the public works and I have the discretion, and because the treasury did not care and the guild of the sandalmakers has a stronger claim than you did.'

'That is not an answer,' said Metrodora.

'No,' said Tarquitius. 'It is not. The answer is that I had spent the whole of the year being told, in every possible way, that a man in my position could not do anything at all, and I discovered in February that I could move an aqueduct eleven feet, and that it was the only thing I had done in twenty years that was worth doing, and that it was too late, and that I did it anyway.' He looked at his cup. 'The list of things that are too late is very long, and I have been adding to it all year, and I find that I am going to spend the rest of my life doing small useless things in the hope that one of them will not be useless.'

Metrodora ate a piece of bread.

'That is an answer,' she said.

Marcellina went to Lugdunum in June.

She went with her father, who had hired a carriage and a physician and a woman to look after the children on the road, and she went with a box of her own clothes and a purse of her own money and a letter from Antonia to a shipping agent at Massilia, and she was away for eleven weeks.

She came back in September with two children: a girl of fifteen called Verana and a boy of twelve called Brennus, who had been called something else and who had asked, on the second day of the journey, whether he might be called Brennus, because that was the name of the man who had been killed in his father's place.

Antonia met them at the gate of the house on the Aventine, and the boy stood in front of her with his cap in his hands and said, 'Domina, I am to thank you for the four sesterces.'

'What eleven sesterces?'

'The fifteen sesterces in the fold of the shroud,' said the boy. 'The man from the prefect's office told us. He said that a boy in a kitchen in Rome had put eleven sesterces in with my father, and that it was all the money he had, and that he had been saving it, and that he had put it in the shroud and would not take it out.' He held out his hand, and there were nine sesterces in it, and they were the same eleven sesterces. 'I have carried them from Lugdunum. I would like to give them back to him, and I am told that he is in a house in the fourth region now, and that he is a watchman.'

Antonia looked at the coins in his hand.

'His name is Gaius,' she said. 'He is at the workshop of Gaius Vipsanius Polla in the sandalmakers' street, and he is a watchman, and he is sixty-four, and he will cry when he sees you, and then he will pretend that he has something in his eye.' She put her hand on the boy's shoulder. 'You may keep the four sesterces. He would want you to keep them. But you will go and see him tomorrow, and you will tell him that you came all the way from Lugdunum, and you will let him give you dinner, and he will give you the worst dinner you have ever eaten and you will eat all of it.'

In August, the stone was put up over Brennus, outside the Colline gate, and Antonia went to see it with Sestius.

He had paid for it and he had arranged it and he had written the inscription himself, and he had not told her what it said, and they stood in the ground outside the gate in the heat with the cypresses and the small square plots and the sound of the road beyond the wall.

It was a good stone: a plain stele of Luna marble, three feet high, with a moulding at the top and the letters cut deep and filled with red.

It said:

*TO THE MEMORY OF BRENNUS, A SEQUANI, FREEDMAN*
*WHO WAS NINE YEARS IN THE HOUSE OF ANTONIA CASSIA*
*AND WAS KILLED IN HER COURTYARD DEFENDING IT*
*AGED FORTY-FOUR YEARS*
*HIS SON BRENNUS AND HIS DAUGHTER VERANA*
*WHO WERE FOUND, HAVE SET THIS UP*
*AND HIS FRIEND MENO, WHO KEPT THE GATE WITH HIM,*
*HAS PAID FOR THE LETTERS.*

Antonia read it twice.

'He will not be able to read it,' she said.

'No,' said Sestius. 'But he will come and stand in front of it, and somebody will read it to him, and in a hundred years there will be a stone in this ground with a Sequani's name on it and two children's names under it, and that is more than most people in this city get.' He looked at the stone. 'My daughter wrote the fourth line. She would not let me have it my way; I had written "his master's house" and she said that he was a freedman and that it was not his master's house, it was his house, and that the stone would say so.'

'She is right,' said Antonia.

'She is always right,' said Sestius. 'It is the most irritating thing about her.' He put his hands behind his back. 'I am selling the house on the Esquiline. Not because I am ruined; I am ruined, but that is not why. I am selling it because my daughter has said that she will not live in it, and because I have discovered that I do not want to live in a house that my wife will not leave and my son will not come back to.' He looked at the stone. 'I am going to Tusculum. There is a small property there, eleven acres and a bad house, and I am going to grow olives badly and be extremely dull, and once a month I am going to come to Rome and have dinner with my daughter, and once a year I am going to come to this stone and stand in front of it.' He turned. 'Domina. I am not going to ask you to forgive me and I am not going to say that I have learned anything. I am going to say one thing, and then I will go and be dull.

'Say it.'

'When I brought that charge,' said Sestius, 'I believed every word of it. I want you to know that. I was not a liar and I was not a man manufacturing evidence; I was a father who had lost his son and who had been told a story by a physician and a steward who both had reasons of their own. And I have thought about that for a year, and what I have concluded is this: it is possible to be entirely sincere and entirely wrong, and to do a great deal of harm, and to be believed by the whole city while you are doing it. I did not know that at fifty-eight. I know it now. And I find that it is the only useful thing I have ever learned.'

He went away through the cypresses toward the road, limping slightly, in a plain toga, and one of the workmen touched his cap to him as he passed, and Antonia stood by the stone for a while after he had gone.

---

In October, the emperor offered Tarquitius the proconsulship of Asia.

It came through the consul, in the ordinary way, in a letter of four lines, and it said that the emperor had reconsidered the matter of the previous year and that the province would be available in the spring and that he would be glad to know whether the curator of the public works could be spared.

He came to the house on the Aventine with the letter in his sleeve and sat in the courtyard and did not say anything for a while.

'You are going to take it,' said Antonia.

'I do not know.'

'You are going to take it,' she said again, 'and I am going to tell you why, and then we will not discuss it again. You are fifty-four years old and you have one province left in you and you have spent the whole of this year being told that you are finished. If you do not go, you will be a man in a garden in Rome with a great deal of time and no work, and you will become the sort of man who reads the Acta in the morning and complains about the aqueducts, and in five years you will be unbearable and I will not be able to have you at my table.' She poured the wine. 'Go to Asia. Govern it well; you are very good at the law and you have a horror of being thought corrupt, which is better than virtue. Come back in two years. Write to me once a month, and do not write about your feelings, because you write about your feelings like a man drafting a will. Write about the province and the price of grain and the men who are stealing.'

'And Metrodora?' said Tarquitius.

'Metrodora will be here,' said Antonia, 'and she will have her school, and she will write to you if she chooses, and you will not ask her to.' She looked at him. 'You have two daughters, Lucius. One of them is nineteen and has just come back from Gaul with two children who are not hers, and she has discovered that she is good at something, and it is not keeping accounts. The other is twenty-five and has a name in a register and a plan and four hundred thousand sesterces that she has not yet asked me for. Neither of them needs you to stay in Rome. Both of them will be here in two years. Go and be a proconsul and come back with a reputation and a great deal of money and something to say at dinner.'

Tarquitius looked at the letter.

'Two years,' he said.

'Two years,' said Antonia, 'and a letter once a month, and the seventh day of the month when you are in Rome, and no promises. We are neither of us in the business of promises; we are in the business of showing up.' She held out her cup. 'Now drink your wine and tell me about the price of lead, because I have been asked to look at the accounts of a fund in eleven towns and I have discovered that I do not know the first thing about lead.'

---

That night she went up onto the roof.

The house on the Aventine had a flat roof over the back room with a low parapet, and it was not the roof of the Golden Vine and it would never be, and from it you could see the river and the roof of the temple of Portunus and, on a clear night, the top of the Palatine.

Myrrhine came up with a lamp and a cup and sat down beside her.

'Well,' she said.

'Well.'

'He is going to Asia.'

'In the spring.'

'And you are going to be a woman of forty-three in a courtyard with a fig tree, reading the accounts of orphan funds in eleven towns, with a daughter who calls you nothing at all and a girl of nineteen who has adopted two children and a dinner on the seventh day of every month.'

'Yes,' said Antonia.

Myrrhine was quiet for a while.

'When I was nineteen,' she said, 'in the house at Puteoli, I used to lie on the roof at night and look at the sky and think: there is a woman somewhere in the world who has a house and a courtyard and a fig tree and nobody's name over the gate, and if I could be her for one day I would die happy.'

Antonia turned her head.

'And now?'

'Now I am her,' said Myrrhine, 'and I have been her for twenty-two years, and it turns out that being her is mostly reading the accounts and arguing with a dyer about a well and telling a woman of forty-three that she cannot have four jars of wine in the house because there is nowhere to put them.' She lay back on the tiles. 'I would not change a day of it. I want that in the record. Whatever happens, whatever the city says, whatever is written on the plaque in the public garden where the house used to be — I would not change a day of it.'

Antonia lay back beside her and looked at the sky.

It was the fourth day of October and the night was clear and cold, and above the river and the temple and the top of the Palatine the whole of the sky was full of stars, and the Ram was somewhere up there and the Bull and the Twins, and there was nobody on the roof to point them out.

'Myrrhine,' she said.

'Mm.'

'There is going to be a plaque.'

'Of course there is going to be a plaque. The city puts a plaque on everything. It will say that the site was cleared after a fire in the consulship of two men nobody remembers, and in a hundred years there will be a garden with a wall round it and children will play in it and nobody will know.'

'And do you think,' said Antonia, 'that in a hundred years, someone will stand in that garden and say that there was a woman here, and that she was a slave, and that she built a house, and that she was tried for murder in the Basilica Julia in November and was acquitted, and that when the house burned she stood in the street and put four hundred people into a chain?'

Myrrhine thought about it.

'No,' she said. 'But you will know. I will know. Metrodora will know, and she has it in a register in the temple of Castor in her own hand, and Marcellina will tell the two children, and the boy will tell his children that his father was killed in a courtyard and that a woman put eleven sesterces in his shroud.' She reached over and took Antonia's hand. 'That is the whole of it. That is the only immortality there is, and it is not very much, and it is ours.'

They lay on the roof until the second hour of the night, and then they went down into the house, and Antonia put out the lamp.
