# 23 | Fire

They came up the hill through the whole of the city's noise.

It took an hour and a half. The streets were full of people going the other way — families with bundles, a man carrying a door, a woman with a goat, two litters jammed against each other at the corner of the Vicus Cyprius with their bearers shouting — and twice they had to stop and go round, and once Faustus had to take the cart into a side street because a wall had come down across the road.

The sky over the north slope was orange and the smoke was going up in a column that leaned to the north-east with the wind, and it was the colour of the inside of a furnace at the bottom and grey at the top, and it had begun to blot out the stars.

They came up the Vicus Patricius at the fourth hour.

The street was a river of people and water and broken tile. The vigiles had formed two chains from the fountain of the Vicus Cyprius and one from the cistern at the corner, and they were passing buckets hand to hand and throwing the water at the fronts of buildings that were going to burn anyway, and there were men on the roofs of two houses with wet cloths beating out sparks, and there were four men with a rope pulling down the wall of a bakery that had already gone.

The bakery was gone. The whole of the corner where it had stood was a shell of black wall with the roof fallen in, and the fire was in the block behind it, and the block behind that, and the wineshop on the corner was on fire from the ground floor to the roof.

And the Golden Vine was still standing.

Antonia stood in the street and looked at it: the painted portico, the wall, the cypress, the roof, and above the roof the fig tree, and the whole of the front of the house lit orange and untouched.

'It is not burning,' she said.

'It is going to,' said a vigile, going past with a bucket. 'The block behind it has gone and the roof of your portico is timber and the wind is from the south. We are wetting the fronts of the houses on this side of the street and we have got about an hour, and then we are going to pull the porch down and let it take what it takes.'

Antonia looked at her house.

'Myrrhine,' she said. 'The chain. Take Faustus and go to the fountain and put yourself in the chain and do not go into that house.'

'Where are you going?'

'Into that house.'

'Antonia—'

'The book is in the tablinum,' said Antonia, 'and there are four boxes of it, and if the roof comes down on it, then it burns, and I will be a woman of forty-two with three rooms on the Aventine and no enemies. And if the roof comes down on it while it is out here in the street, then whoever picks it up has the whole of it, and there are forty men in Rome who would pay for it, and one of them is a man I put in front of a magistrate in November.'

Myrrhine looked at her for a moment.

'The house is not yours,' she said. 'The sale was completed at the temple of Castor in March and the money is in Naso's bank and the treasury has owned this roof for eleven days. If it burns, it burns the emperor's roof and you will not lose a sesterce.'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'Then why are we going in?'

'Because the book is mine,' said Antonia, 'and because the whole of it is in a wooden press in a plaster room and I am not going to leave the private business of four hundred people to the wind.'

'Then I am coming with you,' said Myrrhine.

---

The house was dark and full of the sound of the fire next door, which was not a roar but a low, enormous, irregular breathing, with cracks in it.

They went in through the garden door, which did not lock, and across the courtyard, and the peristyle was lit from above by the glow through the open roof and the shadows of the columns went across the floor like the bars of a cage. The fountain had been dry for a month. Somebody had left a bucket on the rim.

The tablinum was locked. Antonia broke the seal with her thumbnail and put the key in and the door opened, and the room smelled of dust and old wax and the press was where it had always been, behind the second door, with four boxes in it.

Faustus came in behind them with a lamp.

'Domina, the roof of the portico is alight.'

'Take that box,' said Antonia. 'Myrrhine, that one. I will take the other two. Do not run and do not go out through the peristyle; go through the passage by the bath and out through the kitchen court, and put them in the cart and put the cart in the middle of the street and stand beside it and do not let anyone near it.'

They carried them out.

The portico was burning when they came into the street: the timber of the roof had caught and the fire was running along it in a line, and the painted plaster of the front was blistering and coming away in sheets, and there was a crowd standing in the street watching it, and one of them was the woman who sold chickpeas.

Antonia put her two boxes on the cart and stood there with her hands black and her sleeves smoking.

'Is that everything?' said Myrrhine.

'That is the whole of it,' said Antonia. 'Eleven years. Four boxes.'

The roof of the portico came down at the fifth hour with a crash that shook the street, and the fire went into the front rooms, and by the sixth hour the whole front of the house was burning, and by the seventh the roof of the atrium had fallen in and the fire was in the peristyle and the fountain was giving off steam.

It was at the sixth hour, when the front of the house had gone and the fire was in the peristyle, that Antonia stopped watching her own property and began to work.

It happened without a decision. A woman came past with a child and said that there were eleven people in the insula behind the sandalmakers' street who could not get out because the passage had fallen in, and Antonia turned to Faustus and said, 'The cart. Take the cart to the fountain and fill every vessel in it and drive it up the alley; there is a way in through the yard at the back and the gate is iron and it will open with a bar.' And then she went to the vigile in charge of the chain and said, 'How many men have you got?'

'Fourteen.'

'And how many are on the roof of the bath house?'

'Six.'

'Then you have eight on the ground and you are using them to throw water at a wall that has already gone.' She pointed. 'Put four of them on that corner with hooks and pull the awning of the portico down before it falls on the chain, and put the other four on the cistern and keep the water moving, and I will find you thirty people from this street who will carry buckets if somebody tells them where to stand.'

The vigile looked at her.

'Who are you?'

'Nobody,' said Antonia. 'But I have kept a house with nineteen people in it for eleven years and I know how to make a frightened crowd do one thing at a time.'

She did it for three hours.

She stood at the corner of the Vicus Cyprius with her sleeves rolled and her hair coming down and told people where to stand: the baker's two sons, the woman who sold chickpeas, a group of eleven men from the cattle market who had come up to see the fire and stayed to work, four of Naso's clerks who arrived at the seventh hour with a cart and a chain of their own, and the copyist from the Argiletum, who turned out to have a good head for water and who spent the whole morning doing exactly what he was told.

At the seventh hour they got the nine people out of the insula behind the sandalmakers' street, through the yard at the back, in twos and threes, and one of them was a woman of about eighty who had refused to leave her room until Antonia came in through the smoke and sat down on the bed beside her and said, 'Domina, I am a woman of forty-two and I have lost my house tonight and I am still going to walk out of this door, and I am not going to do it without you.'

The old woman came.

At the eighth hour the fire crossed the sandalmakers' street at the northern end and the vigiles pulled down the portico of the house opposite, and the men on the ropes were at it for a quarter of an hour, and Antonia stood in the street and shouted at them to stand back from the wall, and they stood back, and the wall came down into the street exactly where they had been standing and nobody was hurt.

At the ninth hour a man from the prefect's office found her and asked who was in charge of the civil arrangements on the north side of the Vicus Cyprius, and she said, 'Nobody; there is a vigile at the corner and a man from the corn office at the baths with a list of the homeless, and if you want to be useful you will take that list and find four hundred beds in the baths of Titus before nightfall,' and he wrote it down and went away and did it.

---

They stood in the street and watched it burn.

Somebody brought a stool. Somebody else brought wine and Antonia took it and did not drink it. The baker's wife came and stood beside her with a child on her hip and said, 'We are all out. We are all out, domina; the boy went back for the cat and came out through the window and he is all right and the cat is not, and I have been standing here for an hour telling people that.'

'Which house?' said Antonia.

'The one at the corner of the sandalmakers' street. It has gone.' The woman's face was black with smoke and she was crying without noticing it. 'Nine families. The wineshop keeper's mother is dead; she was ninety and she would not come out. And there is a man who has been going into the buildings all night, a man in a good tunic with his face black — he has been in three times and he has brought out eleven people and he is in the fourth one now and they are saying it is going to come down.'

Antonia turned.

'What man?'

'I do not know his name. He has a southern accent and a bad leg and he has been giving orders to the vigiles as though he were paying them.'

---

Marcus Sestius Verus came out of the fourth building at the seventh hour with a child under one arm and a woman over his shoulder, and he was three steps clear when the floor of the passage gave way behind him and the whole front of the insula came down into the street.

The crowd got him. Two vigiles got him. They laid him on the pavement and somebody put water on his face and he sat up and coughed for a long time and then said, 'There is a man in the back room on the ground floor. He is dead. I could not get to him.'

Antonia came through the crowd and stood over him.

He looked up at her. His face was black and his eyebrows were gone and his tunic was burned across the shoulder, and he was fifty-eight years old and he looked like a man who had been in a war.

'Domina,' he said.

'You have been in four buildings.'

'Five,' said Sestius. 'The first one was the bakery and there was nobody in it.' He coughed. 'I came up at the third hour. My agent has a house on this hill and I was at his table when the fire started, and we came out, and everybody was running that way and I found that I was running this way.' He looked at the burning street. 'I have spent the whole of my life in rooms with men who were afraid of me. I have never once in fifty-eight years done anything that was any use at all. And tonight I put my shoulder against a door and it opened and there were four people behind it, and I find that I do not know what to do with the feeling.'

Antonia looked at him for a long moment.

'Your face needs a physician,' she said.

'My face can wait,' said Sestius. 'There is a man in the back room of that building who is dead and there is a woman in the street who does not know that it is her husband, and I am going to go and tell her, and then I am going to sit down somewhere and not move for a week.' He got up, with difficulty, holding a vigile's arm. 'Domina. The children. In Lugdunum.'

Antonia's hands went cold.

'My agent found them,' said Sestius. 'It took him four months. The girl is fifteen and she is in service in the household of a wine merchant and the boy is twelve and he is with the same household in the stables. They are alive, and they are not free, and the girl has a mark on her arm from a burn that she says she got at seven and that her mother did not, and my agent says the household is a decent one and that the girl can read.' He wiped his mouth. 'I have the papers. The man who owns them has agreed to sell, and the price is eleven thousand sesterces for the two, and I have paid it, and they will be freed by the council's leave in the autumn and my agent will bring them to Rome in the spring, and they are not to be told that I paid for it, because I do not intend to be thanked by a girl of fifteen for a thing I did after her father was killed in my name.'

Antonia stood in the street with the house burning behind her.

'Why?' she said.

'Because I have nothing else to do,' said Sestius, 'and because I have been thinking since November about a man called Brennus who died in a courtyard with a knife in him because my steward was in a rage, and because I have discovered at fifty-eight that the only thing I have ever been any good at is going into buildings.' He looked at the fire. 'Do not thank me. Do not say anything at all. I am going to go and tell a woman that her husband is dead, and then I am going to go home and sleep for two days, and then I am going to go and see my daughter, who has not spoken to me since August, and I am going to ask her whether she would like to come to Lugdunum with me in the spring to fetch two children who are not ours.'

He went off through the crowd, limping, with his burned shoulder, and the crowd made way for him.

---

The house burned until the ninth hour of the morning.

By then the fire had crossed the street in two places and had been stopped at the third, and the vigiles had pulled down the portico of the house opposite and part of the wineshop's wall, and the whole of the north slope between the Vicus Cyprius and the sandalmakers' street was a field of black walls and standing chimneys and smoke.

Antonia's house was a shell.

The front was gone entirely. The atrium had no roof and the walls of it were standing to the height of a man and no higher. The peristyle had kept two of its columns and the mosaic was black and cracked and there was a foot of water in the fountain with something in it that had been the bucket.

The zodiac room had no ceiling.

She went in at the ninth hour and stood in the middle of it with the ash coming down around her like grey snow, and looked up, and there was nothing above her but the sky.

She found the place where the Ram had been. The plaster was there — the ceiling had come down in three big pieces and one of them had landed across the room and was lying face up like a fallen table, and the painting on it was blistered and black at the edges but the middle of it had survived, and the Ram was in the middle, and the fleece was burnt away, and the three letters that had been worked into the curl of it were gone.

Antonia stood in the ash and looked at it for a while.

'Well,' she said.

Then she went round the room, and through the arch into the peristyle, and picked up three things out of the ash.

The first was the bronze boar's head from the garden door, which had come off in the heat and was lying in the courtyard, and which was blackened and had one ear melted, and which had been on that door since before she bought the house.

The second was a piece of the mosaic from the atrium, about the size of her palm, with four white tesserae and one black one in it, which had come up whole when the floor cracked and which she found by stepping on it.

The third was a lump of plaster the size of a fist, with a curl of gold leaf on it and the corner of a fleece, and the fleece was burnt away and the gold had bubbled and gone black at one end, and under the black there was a place where the plaster was clean and pale and there was nothing written on it at all.

She put the three things in her sleeve.

Faustus came across the courtyard with a shovel.

'Domina, the men from the treasury are here. They say it is their building and they want to know what you are doing in it.'

Antonia turned around.

'Tell them I am taking three things out of a house I lived in for more than a decade,' she said, 'and that they may have everything else, and that if they want the boar's head they will have to argue with me in the street.'

---

She was on the corner at the eleventh hour when a woman she did not know came up to her and said, 'You are Antonia Cassia.'

'I am.'

The woman looked at her: the black face, the burnt sleeves, the hair down, the hands.

'I have said a great many things about you in the last year,' the woman said. 'In the market. To my sister. In front of my children.' She had a basket on her arm with a loaf in it, and she was perhaps thirty-five, and she was not apologising; she was reporting. 'I said that you were a wicked woman and that the city would be better without you and that a woman with a house like that ought to be driven out. I said it in August. My husband told me to be quiet and I did not.'

Antonia looked at her.

'And now?' she said.

'And now I have been standing at the corner for two hours and I have watched you put four hundred people in a chain and get an old woman out of a burning insula,' said the woman, 'and I do not know what to do with it, and I am not going to say that I was wrong, because I do not know yet whether I was wrong.' She held out the loaf. 'But you have not eaten since last night and I have a loaf and two children at home and I am not a fool, and I would like you to take it.'

Antonia took the loaf.

'Thank you,' she said.

'It is not a forgiveness,' said the woman.

'No,' said Antonia. 'It is bread. They are different things and I have learned this year that people confuse them constantly.'

---

They put the four boxes on the ground in the street at the ninth hour, and Antonia stood over them, and Myrrhine stood beside her, and Faustus stood beside the cart, and Meno, who had come up at the sixth hour and had not said a word since, stood at the corner with his stick as though there were still a gate to keep.

A man came up to her out of the crowd: a little man, quick, with soot on his face and a bag over his shoulder, and he said, 'Domina, I am a copyist. I have a stall in the Argiletum. If those boxes are wet I can dry them and copy them; I do it for the courts.'

'They are not wet,' said Antonia.

'They are four boxes,' said the man. 'That is a lot of paper to carry about a burning street. I will give you a room to put them in and a lock and I will not ask what is in them, and I will charge you eleven sesterces a day, which is fair.'

Antonia looked at him.

'What is your name?'

'Publius Varius Felix.'

'Publius Varius Felix,' said Antonia, 'you are the first man in this city to offer me a fair price for anything in nine months without knowing who I am.' She looked down at the four boxes. 'There is no need. I am going to burn them.'

Myrrhine said, 'Antonia.'

'I have been thinking about it since the fourth hour,' said Antonia, 'and I have not been thinking about it well, because I have been carrying boxes through a fire, and a person who is carrying boxes through a fire does not think. But I have been thinking about it since the second hour of the night, when I stood in a doorway on the Aventine and decided to come and get them.' She knelt down and put her hand on the top box. 'This is eleven years of the private business of about four hundred people. It is the reason I have never been robbed and the reason no man in this city has been able to touch me since the fourth year. It is also the reason that a praetor's steward knew he could send four men over my wall and that no magistrate would believe a woman with a book like this. It is a knife in a drawer, and I have had it in the drawer for eleven years, and in that time I have taken it out twice, and one of those times a man was taken away in the Forum in front of three thousand people.'

She opened the top box.

The tablets were dry and in order, in Cinnamus's hand and Tertia's fair copy, four hundred and eleven of them, wrapped in cloth.

'It is not power,' said Antonia. 'That is what I have learned this year. A thing that can only be used once is not power; it is a debt. I have been paying the interest on it for a decade and in November it was called in.' She took the first bundle out. 'And there is another thing, and it is the true reason, and I am going to say it out loud in the street so that I cannot pretend afterwards that I did not think it. Half the men in that book are men who came to my house and were lonely in a way they had never been able to say out loud. I have their secrets because I was kind to them at the second hour of the night. And a woman who keeps a book like that is a woman who has decided, once and for all, that being kind is a transaction.'

Myrrhine looked at her.

'And if you burn it,' she said, 'and one of them comes for you in a year, or in ten years, with a charge and a witness, and you have nothing at all?'

'Then I will be a woman with three rooms on the Aventine and a great deal of money,' said Antonia, 'and I will defend myself with the truth, which is what I did in November, and which worked, and which cost me less than this book ever did.'

She carried the four boxes into the shell of her own house, into the peristyle, where the floor of the dining room had fallen into the cellar and the fire was still burning in the ruins underneath, and she opened them one at a time and put the bundles into the fire.

It took a quarter of an hour.

The wax melted first and ran, and then the wood of the tablets caught, and then the papyrus, which burned very fast and very brightly and made a great deal of smoke, and the cloth wrappings smouldered and had to be pushed in with a stick.

The whole household stood in the peristyle and watched, and nobody said anything, and Faustus came with a stick and helped.

When the last bundle was in, Antonia stood over the fire with her hands black and her face black and her eyes streaming from the smoke.

'That is the end of the house,' she said.

---

Meno went and stood at the gate.

There was no gate. There was a gap in the wall where the gate had been, and the doorkeeper's bench was ash, and the street outside was full of rubble and water and people going past with their bundles, and he stood in the gap with his stick across his knees the way he had stood there for nine years.

Antonia came and stood beside him.

'There is nothing to keep,' she said.

'I know,' said Meno.

'You are going to Tibur on the Kalends of May. There is a cottage with a garden gate and a wall and a fig tree and a woman next door who has already written to me twice about the roof.'

'I know,' said Meno. 'I have the letter. I cannot read it; the atriensis read it to me. She says the roof is sound and the well is good and that there is a widow at the end of the lane who keeps bees.' He looked at the street. 'Thirty years, domina. Four houses. This is the first one that burned.'

'It is a bad way to end,' said Antonia.

'It is a bad way to end and a good way to end,' said Meno, 'and I have been standing here for a quarter of an hour working out which it is.' He shifted the stick. 'A gate is a thing that keeps people out and lets people in. I have kept out eleven thousand people in nine years and I have let in perhaps four thousand, and of those four thousand, nine hundred were men who came to the house at night and eleven were men who came to the house at night and were kind to me.' He turned. 'When it burned, I found that I was not thinking about the house. I was thinking about the bench, and about the boy from the kitchen who used to sit on the step at the fourth hour with his bread, and about Brennus, who used to stand where I am standing now and say that a doorkeeper who cannot see the street is a decoration.' He put the stick down. 'So it is a good way to end. It ends with the gate open, and nobody coming over the wall, and everybody out.'

---

They went down the hill at the eleventh hour through streets full of wet ash and people standing in doorways.

At the corner of the Vicus Cyprius a woman came out of a doorway and said, 'Domina. There is a room at the back of our shop, it is small but it is dry, and my husband says you may have it for as long as you need, and there is no rent.'

'Thank you,' said Antonia. 'We have somewhere to go.'

She walked down the hill with Myrrhine beside her and Meno behind her with his stick, and Faustus with the cart, and at the bottom of the hill she stopped at the corner where the Vicus Patricius meets the Subura and turned around and looked back.

The smoke was still going up, but it had thinned, and the sun was on the roofs of the city beyond it, the whole enormous slope of Rome, temples and insulae and the tops of the aqueducts and the great shining roof of a temple on the Palatine, and the city went on, in the way it always does, indifferent and alive and extremely beautiful.

'Forty-one years,' said Antonia.

'What?'

'I was sold at Puteoli when I was sixteen,' said Antonia, 'and I came to this city at twenty-two with a hundred and ten thousand sesterces and a lock, and I have been in it for twenty years, and I have built a house and lost it twice — once in March, when I gave it away, and once last night, when it burned. And in twenty years nobody in this city has ever offered me a room with no rent until this morning.'

'That woman did not know you,' said Myrrhine.

'No,' said Antonia. 'That is the point.'
