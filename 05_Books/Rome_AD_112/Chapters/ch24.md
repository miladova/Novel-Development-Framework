# 24 | The Reckoning

The three days between the fire and the reckoning were the strangest of her life, and she spent them being useful.

There was a great deal to do. The city had four hundred and eleven families without a roof on the north slope, and the corn office had a list and the baths of Titus had beds and the vigiles had a captain who had been up for two nights and who, on the second morning, sat down on a step in the Forum and could not get up again. Antonia went to the baths at the fourth hour with Myrrhine and Vibia Lucana, who had come over from the Caelian at dawn with a cart of blankets and had taken charge of the linen, and she stayed until the ninth hour, and what she did was not noble; it was clerical.

She sat at a table by the door with a tablet and made a list of who had come in and what they had lost: name, occupation, household, whether they had a trade, whether they had relatives in the city, whether they had papers. It was the same work she had done for eleven years, and she did it for two days, and by the second evening there were three hundred names on the list and eleven of them were people she knew.

On the second day a man came in who had been the doorkeeper of the house in the Argiletum in the year Prima was sold out of it, and he was sixty-four and had lost everything and had a cough, and he stood in front of the table and said, 'Domina, I know what you are and I do not hold with it, and I have nowhere to go.'

Antonia wrote his name down.

'There is a room in the fourth region over a bakery,' she said. 'It is small and it is dry and the woman who owns it is a midwife called Sabina and she will not be polite to you. You will go there tonight and you will tell her that I sent you and that you are to be given the room for a year, and in the morning you will go to the workshop of a builder named Gaius Vipsanius Polla in the sandalmakers' street and ask for work as a watchman, and you will tell him that Antonia Cassia sent you, and he will not like it and he will give you the work.'

The old man looked at her.

'Why?' he said.

'Because you were at a gate for thirty years and you have a cough and you are sixty-four,' said Antonia, 'and because I have discovered this year that a person who has been at a gate for thirty years is worth more than the whole of the Senate and I did not know it until November.' She dipped her stylus. 'Next.'

---

On the third day the treasury's clerk came to the house on the Aventine with the papers about the burned site, and Antonia signed them in the courtyard, and the clerk said, 'Domina, there is a difficulty. The site is now a dangerous structure and the treasury does not want it, and the corridor for the aqueduct is going to be re-surveyed, and the curator has asked me to say that the survey will take place in the autumn and that nothing will be built on the north slope before the spring.'

'Why is that a difficulty?'

'It is not a difficulty for you,' said the clerk. 'It is a difficulty for me. I have to explain it in a schedule.'

Vibia Lucana, who was sitting in the courtyard with a cup of wine and her shoes off, waited until he had gone and then said, 'He has stopped the aqueduct.'

'He has delayed the survey.'

'He has stopped the aqueduct, and the whole slope is going to sit empty for a year, and in a year nobody will remember that it was going to be an aqueduct at all, and it will become a public garden with a plaque on it about the fire, and the plaque will not mention you, and I will come and look at it every year and be extremely pleased with myself.' She put her cup down. 'And you will go up the hill on the third day and stand in the ash with him and say whatever it is you have been not saying for twenty-five years, and I will be here when you come back, and I will not ask you a single question, and then I will ask you eleven.'

'You have been talking to Myrrhine,' said Antonia.

'Myrrhine and I have been talking for four months,' said Vibia. 'We have a great deal in common. We are both women who have spent our lives being extremely competent in the service of somebody else's household, and we have both decided, at forty-five and thirty-seven, that we are going to stop.' She picked up her shoes. 'Go up the hill. Take your time. I will keep the courtyard.'

---

She went back on the third day, in the afternoon, alone.

The street had been cleared. The rubble was in heaps along the walls, and the vigiles had put a rope across the gap where the gate had been, and the treasury had put a notice on a post, and the whole north slope smelled of wet ash and the particular sour smell of a burned house, which is a smell she had known all her life and had never once been able to describe: charcoal and lime and something sweet underneath it.

She went in under the rope.

The house was smaller than it had been. That was the thing nobody told you about a burned building: it did not look destroyed so much as reduced, as though the fire had taken a wall away and the rooms had turned out to be the size of rooms and not the size of the life that had been lived in them.

She walked through the front rooms and the atrium and into the peristyle. Two columns were standing. The fountain's basin was full of black water and something in it, and the fig tree in the garden had burned to a black stump with four branches on it, and beyond the wall the cypress was still standing, burned on one side and green on the other.

He was standing in the middle of the peristyle with his hands behind his back.

She stopped.

'How long have you been here?' she said.

'An hour. Perhaps two.' He was in a plain tunic with no toga and no rings, and his shoes were grey with ash, and he had been standing so still that the ash had begun to settle on his shoulders. 'The treasury's clerk let me in. I told him I was the curator of the public works and that I had come to inspect a dangerous structure, which is true; the whole of this slope is a dangerous structure at the moment and I am going to spend the summer signing orders about it.'

'You did not have to come.'

'No,' said Tarquitius. 'I have been coming every day since the fire. This is the fourth time. The first time I came at the fifth hour of the morning on the day itself and the roof was still falling in, and I stood in the street and watched it, and there were four hundred people in that street and not one of them knew who I was.' He looked at the burned fig tree. 'I have wanted to say something to you for eleven days and I have not known what it was, and I have come up this hill four times in order to say it, and each time I have stood in this courtyard and said nothing at all.'

Antonia came and stood beside him.

'Say it now,' she said. 'There is nobody here and the walls are down and the roof is off, and I have discovered this year that there is no better place to say a thing than a room with no roof.'

---

He was silent for a long time.

'I have made a list,' he said at last. 'In the way that I make lists. I have written it out four times and burned it four times and the fourth one was the shortest, and it had three things on it.' He took a folded paper out of his sleeve. 'I am not going to read it to you. I am going to say the three things and then I am going to give you the paper, and you may keep it or burn it, and I am not going to ask you for anything.'

'Then say them.'

'The first is that I lied to myself for twenty-five years,' said Tarquitius, 'and I did it deliberately, and it was not a mistake and it was not a weakness and it was not the fault of my sister's letter. I was told that you and the child had died, and I believed it because believing it was convenient, and I did not ask a single question. I did not write to the household at Arretium. I did not go to Puteoli, which is four days' journey, and I was a man of thirty with a private income and no duties, and I could have gone in September and I did not go for twenty-five years.' He looked at the fountain. 'And then, when I was forty and you opened a house in Rome and every man in the city began to talk about the woman Cassia, I knew. I knew in the first month. There cannot have been a man in Rome who did not tell me the story of the woman from Puteoli who had bought the house on the Esquiline, and I heard it and I said nothing, and I did not go to see you, and I did not write, and I did not send a single message in nineteen years.'

'Why not?' said Antonia.

'Because I was a senator,' said Tarquitius, 'and because the story had me in it, and because a man who goes to that house to ask a question is a man who has answered one. And because — this is the second thing on the list, and it is worse — I was angry with you.' He turned. 'I was angry with you for nineteen years. I had built a version of it in which I had been generous and you had taken what was offered and gone, and in that version you were a woman who had done very well out of a bad business, and every year that your house got richer and your name got larger I was confirmed in it. I mentioned you in the Senate. I did it deliberately. I stood up in that chamber, year after year, and spoke about the corruption of the city's pleasures, and I did it because it was the only way I had of touching you.'

Antonia stood very still.

'That is the truest thing you have ever said to me,' she said.

'It is the truest thing I have ever said to anybody,' said Tarquitius. 'It has taken me fifty-three years and a fire.'

'And the third thing?'

He was quiet for a moment.

'The third thing is that I loved you,' he said. 'I was twenty-nine years old and I was a man who had been sent home from the frontier in disgrace and was living in his father's house with nothing to do and no future, and there was a girl in the linen room who could read and who found a hole in my argument about the law of boundaries and laughed at me, and I have not been laughed at in that way by anybody since.' He folded the paper. 'I have never said that to anyone. I have not said it to my wife, who is dead, or to my mother, or to my daughter. I have said it in a letter every August for twenty-five years and burned it at the ninth hour in the brazier by the wall, which you knew, because your daughter told you, and which I have known that you knew since November, and which is the single most humiliating thing about me.'

---

Antonia looked at the burned stump of the fig tree.

'I am going to say my three things,' she said, 'and then we will be even, and I do not promise that they will be kind.'

'Say them.'

'The first is that I have hated you for twenty-five years, and I have hated you in public and in private and in a way that has been useful to me, because a woman in my position needs somebody to be against, and you were the best available. In the second year after I opened the house I turned down an offer of a partnership and a great deal of money from a man in Ostia because he was a friend of yours, and I told myself that I was being prudent, and I was not being prudent. I was being sixteen years old in a corridor with a jug in my hands.' She looked at him. 'I built a house with a wall around it and I put you outside the wall, and everything I have done since has been easier because you were outside it. That is not a small thing, and it is not a noble thing, and it is mine.'

'I know,' said Tarquitius.

'I know you do not,' said Antonia. 'You have come up this hill four times to say a thing and you have prepared a list, and the list has three items on it, and none of the three is the four thousand sesterces.' She turned. 'You asked me in November whether I took money out of your father's house and I told you no, and you looked at me as though a paper had been turned over. And then you went home and you asked your mother, and your mother told you the truth about the accounts, and she has told me since that she told you, and that you said nothing at all and went out into the garden and stood there for an hour.' She looked at him. 'You have never mentioned it since. Not in November, not at the trial, not in the garden in December when you gave me the letter. Four thousand sesterces. Your sister took it out of the steward's account in the month I was sold, and she used part of it to buy the ground the tenements stood on, and she has lived in a house at Tibur for twenty-five years on the interest, and you have known that since November and you have not said one word to me about it.'

Tarquitius stood with his hand on the basin.

'I have not said one word to her either,' he said. 'I have written to her four times and burned all four letters. She is seventy-nine and she cannot walk and she is in a great deal of pain, and every time I begin a letter I find that I am writing to a woman who is going to die in the spring, and that there is nothing I can say to her that will do anything except make her last months worse.' He looked up. 'That is not a justification. I know exactly what it is. It is the same thing I did when I was thirty and my father died and my sister managed everything and I went back to Rome and was correct. I am a man who does nothing, and I do it with a great deal of deliberation and a great many lists.'

'Yes,' said Antonia. 'You are.'

'And the money,' he said. 'I have it. My mother gave me the whole of it out of her own purse in December, because she had it put by for twenty-five years, and it has been in my strongbox since the ides of December and I have not been able to think of a single thing to do with it. It is four thousand sesterces. It is a sum a man spends on a dinner.' He reached into the sleeve of his tunic and took out a small purse, which he put on the rim of the fountain. 'It is not restitution. There is no restitution. It is a purse on a wall in a burned courtyard, and you may take it and give it to the woman who lost her mother in the wineshop, and it will buy her a bed and a winter, and it will not be anything at all.'

Antonia looked at the purse.

'I will give it to her,' she said. 'And I will tell her where it came from, and she will not understand, and she will say thank you, and that will be the end of it.'

'Yes,' said Tarquitius.

'And you will not send your sister anything,' said Antonia. 'Not a letter and not a word and not a purse. She has been dying for more than a decade and she has had twenty-five years to say one true thing and she has not said it, and you are not going to give her absolution because you are her brother and you are tired. If she wants to say something she knows where the temple of Castor is; she can send a clerk.' She turned away. 'That is not justice either. I am aware of it. But it is what I have.'

---

'The second thing,' she said, 'is that I loved you and I have never once been able to say it in the past tense.' She turned and looked at him properly. 'I was fifteen. I had been a slave since I was eleven and I had never in my life been asked what I thought about anything, and you asked me what I thought about Livy, and you corrected my Latin, and you told me I would have made a jurist, and you said, "I will look after you," and I believed you.' Her voice was steady. 'I have spent twenty-five years being angry with you for what happened afterwards, and this year I have discovered that the thing I cannot forgive is not what you did. It is that I would have gone anywhere with you. If you had come to me in that garden at Puteoli when I was sixteen and said, "Get in the cart," I would have got in the cart. I would have left the child. I would have gone to the Danube with you and lived in a tent and been happy. And there is no version of my life in which I am a woman who would have said no to you, and I have never been able to look at that directly, and I am looking at it now, in a burned courtyard, at the age of forty-two.'

Tarquitius had put his hand out and was holding the edge of the fountain's basin.

'Antonia—'

'The third thing,' she said, 'is that there is nothing to forgive, and I am not going to forgive you, and I want you to understand the difference.' She turned to face him. 'If I forgive you, then we are two people who have had a quarrel and made it up, and we will be friends, and you will come to dinner, and in a year we will be an old scandal that the city has got used to, and there will be a version of it in which it all came right. And it did not come right. There is a girl of twenty-five in the fourth region who was sold for two hundred and forty sesterces and who has spent the whole of her life not knowing her own birthday, and there is a man in the ground outside the Colline gate who was killed in my garden in November, and there is a woman at Tibur who is dying in pain and who will die without ever having said a true thing to anybody. I am not going to forgive you, and I am not going to be your friend, and I am not going to be your old scandal. If we are anything at all, we will be two people who know exactly what happened and who have decided to be in the same room anyway, and that is a harder thing than forgiveness and it lasts longer.'

---

He stood in the ruined peristyle with the paper in his hand.

'There is a thing I have wanted to ask you for a year,' he said, 'and it is small, and it is not on the list, and I do not know why it matters.'

'Ask.'

'The first time we spoke,' said Tarquitius. 'In the corridor by the linen room, in June, in the afternoon. You came round the corner with a jug and I was standing against the wall with blood on my hands.' He looked at his own hands. 'I have thought about that afternoon for twenty-five years, and I have never been able to remember it properly. I remember the blood and the corridor and the jug. I do not remember what I said. I have a memory of saying something about your name, and I do not know whether that is true or whether I have made it up out of wanting to have asked.'

'You asked me my name,' said Antonia. 'You asked whose I was, and I said I was in the linen, under Faustus, and you said that Faustus was a good man and a thief and that it was a combination one learns to value in a household.'

Tarquitius made a sound.

'That is exactly the sort of thing I would have said,' he said. 'Gods. I was a pompous man at thirty and I have got worse.' He rubbed his face. 'What were the blood and the jug?'

'Your father had been bled,' said Antonia. 'The physician had bled him that morning and there was a great deal of blood, and your father had said something to you that you did not repeat, and you stood in that corridor and looked at your hands as though they belonged to somebody else.' She paused. 'You said thank you. That is the thing I remember. I poured water over your hands and you said thank you, and I went away and lay in the women's room that night and thought that a man who could have anything he wanted had said thank you to a girl with a jug.'

Tarquitius was silent for a long moment.

'I do not remember saying thank you,' he said. 'I believe you. But I do not remember it, and that is the whole of it: I was thirty years old and I was kind for eleven seconds in a corridor and I did not even notice, and it was the most important thing that happened to me that year.' He looked at her. 'I have spent the whole of my life being told that I am a man of consequence, and the only thing that ever changed anybody was eleven seconds in a corridor that I do not remember.'

'That is not the only thing,' said Antonia. 'There was also the winter, and the library, and the Greek. Do not make it smaller than it was in order to make it neater.'

'No,' he said. 'All right.'

'And there is a thing I am going to say to you now that I have not said to anyone, and then we will go on to the settlement.' She looked at the burned stump of the fig tree. 'I have not been able to be a wife, and I have not been able to be respectable, and I have not been able to be a mother to a girl of twenty-five who calls me nothing at all. And I have spent this year discovering that the only thing I have ever been good at, in the end, is being a woman who keeps a house. Not the business. The house. The bread at the fourth hour and the man at the gate and the girl on the roof and the accounts.' She looked at him. 'My whole life is going to be three rooms on the Aventine and a dinner on the seventh day of the month. And I find that I am not sorry. And I would like somebody in the world to know that I am not sorry, because everyone in this city will think that I am.'

'Then what are we?' he said.

'We are two people who are going to have dinner,' said Antonia, 'on the seventh day of every month, in a small house on the Aventine that has three rooms and a yard and a roof that will not last the year. Myrrhine will be there and she will not be polite to you. Tertia will be there when she chooses to be, and she will call me nothing at all and she will call you nothing at all, and you will not ask her to call you anything, and if you do I will have you put out of the house. You will bring the wine because you have better wine than I do. We will talk about the law of boundaries and the price of lead and the aqueduct, and we will not talk about the villa at Arretium unless one of us says the word, and if one of us says the word we will talk about it for as long as it takes.' She held out her hand. 'That is the settlement. That is all of it. It is not very much and it is more than most people get, and it is what I have, and you may take it or leave it.'

Tarquitius looked at her hand.

'You are a very hard woman,' he said.

'Yes,' said Antonia. 'I have had to be.'

'I am not complaining,' he said. 'I have spent the whole of my life in rooms where nobody was hard with me, and I have discovered this year that it is a kind of loneliness that I did not have a word for.' He took her hand. 'I will take it. The seventh day of every month. I will bring the wine, and I will not be polite to your atriens—' He stopped. 'To Myrrhine. I will not attempt to be.' He let go of her hand. 'There is one thing more, and it is not on the list, and I have to say it now or I will not say it at all. I am not going to ask you to marry me. I want to be clear that I have considered it and that I am not going to do it, and I am going to tell you why, because you will otherwise think that it is because of my name.'

'Why?'

'Because a marriage would make you respectable,' said Tarquitius, 'and I have spent this year watching what respectability does to women in this city. It would give you a place at the games and a seat in the second row and the right to be spoken to at dinner, and in exchange you would be the wife of Lucius Tarquitius Priscus for the rest of your life, and every man in Rome who came to your house in the last eleven years would say that you had done very well for yourself, and they would be right, and it would be the smallest possible version of everything you have built.' He put the paper into her hand. 'You are the only person I have ever met who is not improved by being attached to me. I am not going to ruin it.'

Antonia stood in the ash with the paper in her hand.

'That is the first thing you have said in twenty-five years that I did not expect,' she said.

'I know,' said Tarquitius. 'I have been practising it for four days. It is the only good sentence I have ever constructed without a pen.'

---

They walked out through the rooms together.

In the front of the house, where the atrium had been, the treasury men had put up a trestle and were making a list of what could be salvaged, and there was nothing on the list but lead and iron. In the street the rope was down and a cart was going past with a load of burnt timber, and the woman who sold chickpeas had set up her brazier at the corner and was doing a good trade with the workmen.

At the gap where the gate had been, Tarquitius stopped and took the folded paper out of her hand and unfolded it and read it.

It said:

*1. That I knew and did not ask.*
*2. That I was angry with her for nineteen years and used her name in the Senate to do it.*
*3. That I loved her, and that I have never said so to anyone, and that it is not an excuse.*

At the bottom there was one line more, in smaller writing.

*4. That everything I have done this year has been too late, and that I did it anyway.*

He folded it and gave it back to her.

'You may burn it,' he said.

'I know,' said Antonia, and put it in her sleeve.

---

She walked down the hill alone, past the baths of Titus and the place where the street narrowed, and at the corner of the Vicus Cyprius she stopped and turned round and looked back at the black slope and the ruined houses and the cypress standing with one side green and one side burned.

Then she took the paper out of her sleeve and read it again, standing in the street in the middle of the afternoon with the carts going past.

She did not burn it. She had burned a great many things in her life, at the ninth hour of the evening, in a brazier, and she had discovered in the last year that a thing burned is not a thing finished; it is a thing that has been fed to the fire and will come back in the smoke.

She folded it and put it in her sleeve, and went down the hill to the three rooms on the Aventine, where Myrrhine was arguing with a man about a bed.
