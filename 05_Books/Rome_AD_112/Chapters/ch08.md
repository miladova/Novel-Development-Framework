# 8 | A Daughter of the House

Marcellina had never in her life been alone in a street.

That was the thing she noticed first, and she noticed it with a kind of astonishment: that a person could walk down the Vicus Patricius at dusk with a hood over her hair and a basket on her arm and no one would look at her twice. She had expected to be stopped. She had prepared four answers, and one of them was a lie so elaborate that she had rehearsed it aloud in her room. Nobody stopped her. A man with a barrow shouted at her to move, and she moved, and he went past without seeing her at all.

She had come out of the house at the eighth hour, through the garden gate, while her father was at a dinner at the house of a man named Sura and her grandmother was asleep and the steward was counting the silver. She had told the girl who attended her that she was unwell and was not to be disturbed. She had taken off her rings and put them in a box and put on the plain grey dress of a woman of the merchant class and a pair of her nurse's sandals, which were a size too large.

The dress was the hardest part. A senator's daughter does not own a plain grey dress. She had bought it, three weeks earlier, from a woman who sold cloth in the market by the Temple of Tellus, and she had paid for it out of the money she had been given for the shrine of the crossroads, and she had hidden it under the floor of the linen press, and she had thought, each time she looked at it, that she was committing a crime.

The Golden Vine was not what she expected either. She had expected lamps and music and women in paint and a doorkeeper who would refuse her. There were lamps, and there was a doorkeeper — a large old Thracian with a scar through one eyebrow, who looked at her for a long moment — and there was a fountain in a peristyle, and a smell of box hedge and hot stone and something cooking that made her suddenly, absurdly hungry.

'You will wait here,' said the doorkeeper.

'Will you tell her—'

'I will tell her a young woman with a basket is at the door and will not give her name,' said the doorkeeper. 'What she does with that is her business.'

She waited in a small room by the entrance with a bench and a worn mosaic and a lamp. There was a smell in that room that she could not place and afterwards identified as beeswax and cheap wine and the dust of a hundred sandals.

Then the doorkeeper came back and took her through.

---

Antonia Cassia was standing in the peristyle with her hands in the folds of her dress, and she was not what Marcellina expected either. She was not painted. She was not young. She was a woman of forty-one in a plain dark dress with her hair pinned up and no jewellery except one ring, and she looked at Marcellina for a long moment, and her face did something that Marcellina did not understand, and would understand much later.

'You had better come into the garden,' said Antonia Cassia. 'There are eleven people in this house who can hear a whisper through a wall, and four of them are honest.'

The garden was small and walled and full of the smell of a fig tree. There was a bench under the wall, and Antonia sat on it and gestured, and Marcellina sat.

'Take off your hood,' said Antonia.

Marcellina took off her hood.

'You are Tarquitia Marcellina,' said Antonia. 'You have your father's mouth and your grandmother's bones, and you have come here in a grey dress you bought in the market and a pair of sandals that do not fit you, and you have left your rings at home, which was sensible, and you have come at dusk, which was also sensible, and you have made four mistakes since you came through the gate, the first of which was giving your name to the doorkeeper in a voice that has been taught to speak to inferiors.'

Marcellina found that her heart was going very fast and that she was not frightened.

'What were the other three?' she said.

'You looked at the mosaic,' said Antonia. 'A woman who has lived in a house with a mosaic all her life does not look at a mosaic. You said "will you tell her" instead of "will you tell your mistress," which means you have been in a house where the doorkeeper is a man with a name. And you are wearing your nurse's sandals, which are a size too large, and a girl who has run away from a house does not borrow her nurse's sandals; she steals her own back from the laundry. You are not running away. You have come out for an evening and you intend to go back.' She folded her hands. 'What do you want?'

'I want to know what my father will not tell me,' said Marcellina.

'About what?'

'About you.'

Antonia was silent for a moment. In the dark of the garden the fig leaves moved, and somewhere beyond the wall a woman laughed and was hushed.

'Why?'

'Because he has thought about you every day of his life and I have watched him do it,' said Marcellina, and heard her own voice shake, and went on. 'When I was nine I asked him who had painted the ceiling in the north room and he told me the name of the painter and the year and the price of the gold, and then he stopped in the middle of a sentence and went out of the room. When I was twelve, a woman at the baths said your name in front of him and he finished his cup and left. When I was fifteen, I found a cupboard in his study that was locked, and I picked the lock with a nail, which took me three days, and there were no papers in it at all. It was empty. He had a cupboard built, and a lock put on it, and there was nothing in it, and I understood that the nothing was the point.' She stopped, and then said, 'Every year, in August, he writes a letter and burns it. He does it in the garden, at the ninth hour, and he does not know that I have seen it. He writes it, and he does not send it, and he burns it in the brazier by the wall, and then he goes in and washes his hands.'

Antonia did not move.

'Every year?' she said.

'Every year since I was old enough to watch,' said Marcellina, 'and my grandmother says he has done it since before I was born.'

The fig leaves moved. Antonia sat with her hands in her lap and looked at the wall for what seemed to Marcellina a very long time.

'And what have you been told?' Antonia said at last.

'That you are a woman of no family who keeps a house where men go, and that my father has had dealings with you, and that I am not to speak of it.' Marcellina lifted her chin. 'And that whatever happened was twenty-five years ago and is not my concern.'

'That is more than most girls are told.'

'It is nothing at all,' said Marcellina, 'and I am nineteen, and in the winter I am to be married to a man who has a great name and no money and who plays at dice with cheats, and I will go into his house and be the daughter of my father, and the wife of my husband, and one day I will be the mother of a boy who will be told that his father's name is the most important thing about him. And I will have a cupboard in my house with nothing in it, and I will sit in front of it, and I will not be able to say a single true thing to anyone I love, because the truth will have been decided for me by people who are dead.' Her voice did not rise. 'I am not here to be scandalised. I have been scandalised for a week and it was boring. I am here because there is a true thing in my father's life, and he will not tell it to me, and he never will, and if I marry in the winter and go to that house I will never have another chance to hear it.'

Antonia looked at her for a long moment.

'You are very like him,' she said. 'You have his manners at the end of a speech.'

'I have been told I have his mouth.'

'You have his manners,' said Antonia. 'The mouth is your grandmother's.' She stood up and walked to the end of the garden and back, and Marcellina watched her, and it was like watching a woman do arithmetic. 'I am going to tell you three things,' she said, 'and then you are going to go home, and you are not going to come back, and if you are asked where you were this evening you will say that you were at the house of your nurse's sister, and you will say it as though you were bored.'

'Three things.'

'The first is true and will do you no good: your father and I have known each other since I was fifteen, and whatever else there is, there is that, and it cannot be undone by either of us. The second is also true and is the only thing in the world that will help you: he is not a cruel man, and that is worse for you than if he were, because a cruel man can be hated and a man who is not cruel can only be disappointed, and you will spend your life being disappointed by him in small ways and he will never understand why. The third—' She stopped. 'The third is that whatever you have heard about me is probably true, and that the reason I am not going to tell you the rest is not that I am protecting him. It is that I have spent twenty-five years not telling it, and I have not yet decided what it is.'

Marcellina sat very still.

'That is not an answer,' she said.

'No,' said Antonia. 'It is a door. You may knock on it again when you are older and I will decide then whether to open it.' She turned. 'Now. Before you go. You will do something for me.'

Marcellina's eyes narrowed. 'What?'

'You said there is a cupboard in his study with nothing in it. Is there anything else in that room with a lock? A box, a chest, a drawer in the table?'

'There is a strongbox in the wall behind the second press. It has an iron door and two locks.' Marcellina paused. 'I have never opened it.'

'Do not open it,' said Antonia. 'But tell me — is it opened often?'

'Every month. He opens it himself, and the steward is not present, and he counts something and locks it again. I have heard him do it. It takes about a quarter of an hour.'

Antonia nodded slowly.

'That is all,' she said. 'You have paid for your evening. Now go home before the ninth hour.'

Marcellina stood. She felt, suddenly, that she had been handled, and she was not sure she minded, and that frightened her more than anything else that had happened.

'Why do you want to know about his strongbox?' she said.

'Because in the winter your father is going to try to ruin me,' said Antonia, 'and I would like to know what he keeps in it before he does.'

'And you think I will tell you.'

'I think you will think about it for a month,' said Antonia, 'and then you will come back, and you will tell yourself that you are coming back to ask a question, and you will bring me something in your basket, and you will not know why you are doing it, and neither, by then, will I.' She walked her to the gate herself. 'Marcellina. One more thing, and then we will not speak of it. There is a man in this city named Marcus Sestius and a boy named Cirrus, and if either of them ever speaks to you at the games, you will be very polite and you will tell your father that same evening, whether you wish to or not.'

'I do not know them.'

'You will,' said Antonia. 'Go home.'

---

Marcellina's betrothed came to dinner two days before.

His name was Servius Aelius Gracilis and he was twenty-seven, and he had the misfortune to be exactly what he appeared to be: a pleasant, weak, well-mannered young man from a family that had been consular in the time of the divine Augustus and had not produced a magistrate of any consequence in four generations. He was not a villain. He was not even a fool, quite. He was a man who had been told all his life that he was the last of something, and who had responded to that information by developing a taste for expensive horses and a talent for losing money in company, so that the losing could always be described as generosity.

He arrived at the eighth hour with two friends and a jar of wine that had cost him more than he had, and he was charming to the grandmother, and respectful to Tarquitius, and he treated Marcellina with a courtesy that was so careful that it was almost a form of distance, as though she were a valuable object he had been permitted to look at.

At dinner he talked about the games. He talked about a charioteer named Scorpus, and the factions, and a race in which a man had been killed at the turn, and he told the story well, and the two friends laughed, and Tarquitius asked a question about the size of the purse, and Marcellina sat with her hands in her lap and watched him.

He would be kind to her, she thought. He would be kind to her the way a man is kind to a thing that has been given to him and that he does not quite know how to hold. And in seven years he would have spent her dowry, and he would be sorry, and he would be sorry in the way of a man who has never had to be anything else.

After dinner, when the men had gone to look at the horses, her grandmother said, 'He has good manners.'

'He has nothing else,' said Marcellina.

'Good manners are not nothing. I have been married to a man with bad manners and a great deal of money and I would not recommend either.' The old woman turned her blind face. 'You are thinking of refusing him.'

'I am thinking that no one has asked me.'

'That is not the same as refusing,' said Tarquitia Priscilla, 'and it is the most dangerous thing a woman can think, because it feels like virtue and it is only delay.' She put out her hand for her cup. 'If you are going to refuse, refuse in the summer, while your father still has something to lose. If you wait until the contracts are signed you will be refusing a man who has already been humiliated and you will find that the world has very little sympathy for a girl who changes her mind after the wine has been bought.'

'I do not know how to refuse,' said Marcellina.

'No,' said her grandmother. 'Nobody teaches us that. It is the great omission in the education of daughters.' She drank. 'You will learn. Every woman learns, or she is broken, and you are not the broken kind. Your father's sister learned it and used it too well, and I learned it and never used it at all, and you will have to find your own way, and I will not be here to see it.'

---

She got home at the ninth hour and went in through the garden gate and up the back stairs, and she was in her room with the grey dress in her hands when the door opened and her grandmother came in, feeling her way along the wall with one hand.

'You have been out,' said Tarquitia Priscilla.

Marcellina stood with the dress against her chest.

'The nurse's sister,' she said. 'She is ill.'

'You have been out,' said her grandmother again, 'and you have come back with the smell of a fig tree on you, and there are no fig trees between this house and the house of your nurse's sister, which is in the fourth region and has a courtyard with a bay tree in it. You have been on the Esquiline.'

Marcellina said nothing.

The blind face came toward her. Her grandmother's hand found the edge of the bed and then the dress, and her fingers went over the cheap wool.

'Grey,' she said. 'You bought a grey dress, and you paid for it out of the shrine money, and you have been to that house.' She sat down on the bed. 'Sit down. Do not stand there breathing like a horse.'

Marcellina sat.

'Did she see you?' said her grandmother.

'Yes.'

'Did she tell you?'

'Nothing,' said Marcellina. 'She told me three things and one of them was that she had not yet decided what the truth is.'

Her grandmother was silent for a long time. In the corridor the watchman went past with his lamp, and the light came under the door and went away.

'She is a better woman than your father,' said Tarquitia Priscilla at last, 'and she is a worse one, and both of those things are true in the same way that a coin has two faces and you cannot buy bread with the edge of it.' Her hand found Marcellina's hand and held it. 'I am going to tell you something, and then we are not going to speak of it again, and you are not going to repeat it to your father, because he would not survive it and I am too old to watch him not survive it.'

'Grandmother—'

'When I was young,' said the old woman, 'I was told that there was a way for a woman in this family to live. You kept the accounts, you kept the peace, you kept your mouth shut, and in exchange the family kept you. It is a bargain, and it is not a bad bargain, and I made it, and I made it for sixty years, and I have been comfortable, and I have been respected, and I have not once been happy, and there is not a day that goes by that I do not think about a girl in a linen room and what I did not do.'

Marcellina's hand was being held very tightly.

'What girl?' she said.

'Go to sleep,' said her grandmother, and stood up, and found the wall with her hand. 'The grey dress goes in the fire tonight. I will hear it, so do not lie to me about it in the morning. And Marcellina—' At the door she stopped. 'When you marry in the winter, and you are in that house, and they tell you that the way to live is to keep the accounts and keep the peace and keep your mouth shut — you will remember that your grandmother did it for sixty years and was never once happy, and you will decide for yourself, and whatever you decide, you will not tell me, because I will be dead.'

---

Antonia did not sleep that night.

She sat in the small room off the library, where a girl with a remarkable voice had once slept on a stool, and she had a lamp and a cup and a tablet, and she did not write on the tablet.

Every August, at the ninth hour, he wrote a letter and burned it.

She had written eleven letters herself, over the years, and had burned all of them, and she had done it in the brazier in the corner of the courtyard at the ninth hour of the evening on the twenty-ninth day of August, and it had never once occurred to her — not once, in twenty-five years — that he might be doing the same thing at the same hour on the other side of the city.

It was not proof of anything. That was what she told herself, and it was true, and it did not help. A man might burn a letter out of hatred as easily as out of grief, and a woman might sit in a small room at midnight and invent a symmetry because she needed one.

But she sat there until the lamp went out, and she thought about a girl of nineteen in a grey dress with her grandmother's bones, who had picked a lock with a nail when she was fifteen and found nothing in the cupboard, and who had come across the city at dusk to ask a woman she had been taught to despise for the truth.

In the morning she sent for Cinnamus.

'The cupboard in the study of the house on the Palatine,' she said. 'It is empty. He had it built and there is nothing in it. And there is a strongbox in the wall behind the second press with two locks, and he opens it once a month, alone, and counts something, and it takes a quarter of an hour.'

Cinnamus wrote it down.

'Domina,' he said, 'may I ask how you know this?'

'You may not,' said Antonia, 'and you will not write that down either.'

---

Three days later, Hilarus came to the shoemaker's shop with the second report.

He had found Phoebus, the dealer on the Aventine, and Phoebus had been cooperative, and the reason Phoebus had been cooperative was that he was sixty-two and frightened of dying and had begun, in the manner of such men, to keep a book of his own in which the prices were written honestly.

The girl child sold out of Puteoli in the September of that year had been sold for two hundred and forty sesterces, which was the price of a healthy infant with no defects, to a household in Rome.

'A household,' said Antonia.

'A household,' said Hilarus, 'and Phoebus remembers it, and he remembers it because it was not the kind of buyer he usually had. It was a woman of about forty, well dressed, with a slave with her, and she paid in silver and she asked for the bill of sale to be made out in the name of a man who was not present, and Phoebus, who has been in the trade for thirty years, asked no questions, because the questions are what cost you your licence.' He turned the tablet. 'The name on the bill of sale was Lucius Tarquitius Priscus.'

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the fuller's vats were being stirred.

'That is not possible,' said Antonia.

'It is written,' said Hilarus. 'It is written in a dealer's book in a hand that is not Phoebus's, and it is twenty-five years old, and it is the only thing in the world I have found that was not meant to be found, and I would not believe it either, except that I have spent fourteen years learning that the thing a careful man arranges is never the thing that happens.' He put the tablet down. 'The child was sold into the household of the Tarquitii. She was sold in her own father's name, by a woman, for two hundred and forty sesterces, and Phoebus does not know where she went after that, because the household did not sell her. Households do not sell a child they have bought; they keep it, and it grows up, and it becomes a maid, or a secretary, or a cook, and it is never entered in any record again unless it is sold or freed or dies.' He looked at her. 'Domina. Are you all right?'

Antonia was looking at the wall.

'It could be a fiction,' she said at last, and her voice was steady, and she was proud of it. 'A dealer with a book. A name written in the margin. He may have written the name because a woman gave it and he wanted a respectable name on the bill; dealers do that. It may mean nothing at all.'

'It may,' said Hilarus.

'Have you been to Arretium?'

'Not yet. I wanted to bring you this first.'

'Go,' said Antonia. 'The household will have a slave list. Every villa with forty slaves has a list, because the steward must account for them, and the list will be in the estate records, and if a foundling was bought in the autumn of that year she will be entered, and she will have been given a name.' She stood up, and found that her hands were shaking, and put them behind her back. 'And Hilarus — the name. If she was bought into a household as an infant with no name, the steward will have given her a number, because when you buy four slaves in a month you enter them as primus, secundus, tertius. And a girl entered as a number is called the number for the rest of her life, and it becomes her name, and it becomes the name she is freed under, and it becomes the name she signs on the certificate. So look for a woman's name in that household that is also a number.'

Hilarus looked at her for a moment with something that was not quite admiration and not quite pity.

'You have done this before,' he said.

'No,' said Antonia. 'I have been on the other end of it. It is how I know what the paper looks like.'

---

She walked home from the shoemaker's, which she never did, and Myrrhine met her at the gate with her face already set.

'You have been to the sixth region,' she said. 'Alone, veiled, at the seventh hour of the evening. If you were seen, half the city will know by the morning that Antonia Cassia goes to that neighbourhood, and by the afternoon they will have invented a lover, and by the winter it will be in a speech in the Senate.'

'Then I will not be seen,' said Antonia, and went past her.

'Antonia.' Myrrhine caught her arm in the corridor, which she had not done in eleven years. 'Antonia. Stop.'

Antonia stopped.

'You have found something,' said Myrrhine. 'Look at me. You have found something, and you are not going to tell me, and you are going to go up to that room with the strongbox and sit in it until the morning with the four things in the box and the one name you will not say, and then you will come down tomorrow and you will be a woman who has decided something, and I will be told the decision and not the reason, and it will be the same as it has been for twenty-five years, and I am tired of it.'

Antonia stood in the corridor with the lamps lit and the smell of the evening's cooking coming up from the court.

'She is in a house in Rome,' she said. 'She has been in a house in Rome for twenty-five years. The paper says she was sold into a great household, and the name on the paper is the name of a man I know, and I do not know whether he bought her or whether he signed the bill and never saw her.' She heard her own voice, flat, careful, and it was the voice of the accounts. 'I do not know if she is alive. I do not know if she is a slave. I do not know whether the household I am thinking of is the household, and if it is, I do not know whether she has been in a room above my head at a dinner table at which I was not present, and I do not know what I will do when I find out, and I have been thinking about it for two hours and I have got exactly as far as I got in the first minute.'

Myrrhine let go of her arm.

'Oh,' she said.

'Yes.'

'Oh, Antonia.'

'Do not,' said Antonia. 'Not tonight. If you are kind to me tonight I will not be able to do what I have to do tomorrow, and what I have to do tomorrow is write letters to four men as though the world were ordinary.'

'What do you have to do tomorrow?'

'Wait,' said Antonia. 'That is the thing nobody tells you about money. Everyone thinks it buys you the power to act. It buys you the power to wait, and the waiting is the part that costs.'

---

That night she did not go up to the room with the strongbox.

She went up to the roof instead, and lay on the tiles in the dark with the whole of Rome making its noise beneath her, all those millions of people awake or asleep under the same smoke, each of them in a room with a door, and behind some of those doors, she thought, there was a girl of twenty-five who had been entered in a list as a number and had been called the number ever since, and who did not know that she had a mother on the Esquiline who had been looking for her for eleven weeks, and who had, at some point in her life, learned to read, or not.

The last of the four things in the strongbox was the clay figure of a woman with her hands over her face, which a slave in Puteoli had given her to keep her from dreaming.

She had never once in twenty-five years slept with it under her pillow. She took it downstairs that night and put it on the table by her bed, and in the morning she put it in her sleeve, where it stayed for the rest of the summer, and where anyone who had known what to look for would have seen a small clay woman with her hands over her face, worn smooth by somebody's thumb.
