# 2 | What the House Requires

Morning in the Golden Vine arrived in five waves, and Antonia met them in order.

The first was the house itself, waking. Water carriers in the service court. The smell of bread and of the previous night's wine being scrubbed off the mosaic with vinegar. Meno unbarring the street door and swearing at the hinge he had sworn at every morning for nine years. She lay in the dark and listened to it the way a farmer listens to rain, and when she came downstairs at the second hour, dressed plainly, with her hair still wet from the basin, the house was already at work.

The second wave was the accounts. Cinnamus waited for her in the tablinum with the tablets stacked in the order he knew she liked: money owed to the house, money owed by the house, and the third stack, which was the one that mattered, and which contained the things that were neither.

Cinnamus was fifty-four and had been her first purchase as a free woman, bought at a price he had never been told, and he had repaid the favour by knowing everything about everybody and never once, in eleven years, letting a rumour leave the house on his own breath. He had a broad Celtiberian face, a limp, and a wife in the Subura whom he visited on the seventh day of each month, and he was the only person in Rome who called Antonia by nothing at all.

'Well?' she said.

'We are solvent,' Cinnamus said. 'We are not comfortable.'

'That is a change from last month.'

'Last month we were comfortable and we were not solvent.' He set a tablet on the table. 'The dinner cost forty-one thousand sesterces. That is the boar, the oysters, the hire of the rhetorician, the wine that the young Celer drank without noticing he was drinking it, and the three jars of Falernian that went out the garden gate with Hermagoras's slaves at the fourth hour of the night.'

'The wine went out with him?'

'Three jars under a cloak. I have it from the doorkeeper, who is sure, and from the boy who carried them, who is terrified.'

Antonia considered. Somewhere in the house a woman was singing, badly, and stopped.

'Send the boy to the country for a month,' she said. 'Not punished. Working. And when Hermagoras next writes to me — he will write, he will ask for the loan of the library for his students — I will tell him that my wine merchant has raised his prices and that I am no longer able to offer the kind of hospitality he has enjoyed. He will understand it as poverty, which will please him, and he will not come again, which will please me.'

'He is a famous man. His dislike has a price.'

'His dislike has a price and his friendship has a cost, and the cost is higher.' She took the next tablet. 'The doctor?'

'Pamphilus came at the fourth hour. The girl Lucilla has a stricture in the throat, which he says is not a disease but a shape, and which she must learn to sing around. He says she has a rare voice and that she will ruin it if she is used too early.'

'How early is too early?'

'He says three years.'

'She has three months.'

Cinnamus did not argue, because arguing with her about a slave's price was a waste of his time and he knew she knew the arithmetic as well as he did. He moved to the third stack.

'The portico.' He put the tablet down. 'I have been to the tabularium. The permit was granted in the consulship of Commodus and Lateranus. It is good for the life of the building. It grants three feet of encroachment on the public street, not nine.'

Antonia's hand stopped on the tablet.

'It grants three feet,' Cinnamus repeated, 'and there is a second page, which is missing. The clerk showed me the binding. It was cut out with a knife, and cut out a long time ago, not last week.'

'Who cut it?'

'The clerk does not know. The clerk is a freedman of the urban prefect's office and has been in that room for thirty years, and he says that in those thirty years the book has been consulted by eleven people, of whom he remembers seven, and of the seven, four are dead.' Cinnamus paused. 'He remembers your father-in-law consulting it in the year you bought the house.'

'Gaius did no such thing.'

'I did not say he did.' Cinnamus looked at her steadily. 'I said he consulted it. There is a difference, and the difference is the only thing standing between us and a demolition order.'

Antonia sat back. Outside the study window the fig tree moved in the morning wind, and a pigeon on the roof made the noise pigeons make, which is the sound of a city pretending to be a farm.

'Then we have a permit for three feet,' she said, 'and we have built nine, and we have a new curator of works who has spent twenty years waiting for an excuse to take this house apart stone by stone. And if the missing page were found, it would show that the six extra feet were granted by a clerk in the year —' She stopped herself. 'What year?'

'The year you bought it.'

'Then it is what I have always known it was.' She stood. 'Bring me the stone.'

'Domina?'

'From the portico. Not one of the good ones. The one at the corner that the carters knock against. Bring it in and put it by the fountain, and leave it there, and if anyone asks why, the house is having repairs.'

'And what will the stone do?'

'Nothing,' said Antonia. 'It will be a stone in a house. But a man who comes to inspect my portico will see that I am having repairs and that I am not afraid, and he will go away and tell his master that I am not afraid, and that will cost him a night's sleep. It is the cheapest harm I can do him.'

---

The third wave of the morning was people, and the people who came to the Golden Vine's door in the morning were not the men who came at night.

There were petitioners. A woman whose husband had been taken for debt, who had come because someone in her insula had told her that the woman in the big house on the north slope could get a man out of the carcer if she chose. A ship's captain with a claim against a merchant who had already been paid twice for the same cargo. A freedman from the Gardens of Maecenas with a scheme for a water clock that would tell the hour in any weather, who wanted four hundred thousand sesterces and who had brought drawings, and whose drawings were, Antonia thought, genuinely beautiful and completely impractical.

She saw them all. That was a rule older than her fortune. She saw them in the small room by the entrance, where the mosaic was worn, sitting on a bench with her hands folded, and she listened to each of them for as long as she judged them to be telling the truth, which was sometimes a quarter of an hour and sometimes ninety heartbeats.

'Why do you see them at all?' Celer had asked her once, in the early days, when he still believed that her time was a thing that could be wasted. 'They have nothing to give you.'

'Everyone has something to give me,' she had said. 'The woman with the husband in the carcer has a story that six insulae believe, and when I get her husband out she will tell it better. The captain has a ship and a grudge and a route to Ostia that passes a warehouse I want to buy. And the man with the water clock has a mind like a jeweller's, and in ten years he will be the most useful engineer in Rome and he will remember that I gave him an hour and the others gave him a door.'

'And in the meantime you have lost an hour.'

'In the meantime I have bought a man's whole future for an hour,' said Antonia, 'which is the best price in this city, and if you cannot see that, you will not last three years in the Senate.'

The woman with the husband in the carcer she got out, at a cost of eleven thousand sesterces and one favour owed to a praetor's clerk who would one day want something she would not want to give. The captain she sent to Naso with a note, because a man who has been cheated twice by the same merchant will be a very good client for a banker who likes to watch a cheat being squeezed. The man with the water clock she gave two hundred sesterces and her Greek engineer's opinion, and told him to come back in the winter, and he wept, which happened about once a month and which she had long ago stopped being moved by and never once stopped noticing.

The fourth wave was the women of the house, and this was the part that no ledger recorded.

There were nineteen of them at that time, of whom eleven were on the roster and eight were not: the two who cooked, the woman who did the linen, the girl who carried water, the woman who tended the sick, and three children born in the house who were not yet anything at all. The eleven had come to it by eleven different roads. Prima had been taken in a raid on a village in Pannonia when she was nine and had been in Rome ever since, and had been bought by Antonia at twenty-four out of a house in the Argiletum where she had been beaten so regularly that the scars on her back had healed into a pattern like a river delta. Eucharis had been sold by her own father, a harp teacher on Delos, for a sum that he had used to buy a boat. Two of the women had been married and widowed. One had been a free woman of good family who had run off with a gladiator and discovered that the gladiator's affection had a term, and who still had the manners of a senator's daughter and used them like a weapon.

Antonia paid them. This was the thing she was known for and it was the thing that respectable men found most offensive, more offensive even than the trade: she paid wages. Not a share of what a guest left, not a gift at the Saturnalia. A sum at the end of the month, written in a book, with a column for the food and the clothes and the doctor and a column for what was their own. Some of them had bought their freedom with it. Three had left the house and married. One had come back.

She held them to the account, too. There was the rule about the guests and the rule about the doors, and the rule that a woman who was hurt by a guest could come to Myrrhine in the night and that the guest would never be admitted again, no matter what he was, no matter what he could do to them; and there was also the rule that a woman who stole from the house was gone before dark, and the rule about drink, and the rule that none of them, ever, was to speak to Antonia about the past unless Antonia spoke first.

That last rule had been broken exactly once, by Prima, in the fifth year, and Antonia had listened to her for an hour and had said thank you, and then had gone upstairs and been sick.

---

The fifth wave of the morning was the city, and the city came in with the news.

It came with Celer, who arrived at the fourth hour with his toga crooked and his hair still wet, and who had clearly been running.

'Have you heard?'

'I have heard a great deal,' said Antonia. 'Sit down. You look like a man who has been chased.'

'It is not a rumour. It is in the Acta. Tarquitius Priscus has the cura operum publicorum, and he has already issued an edict, and the edict says that all encroachments upon public ground in the fourth and fifth regions will be inspected before the Kalends of October, and demolished at the owner's cost.' Celer sat, and then stood up again. 'The fourth region is the Temple of Peace. The fifth is the Esquiline. He has named your hill, domina. He has named the hill and not the street, which is how a man names a thing without naming it.'

'And?'

'And he has been made a candidate for the proconsulship of Asia, and the lot will be drawn in the winter, and everyone says the emperor favours him, and if he is proconsul of Asia he will be away for a year and will come back with an army of clients and a fortune, and then there will be no touching him for a decade.'

'Then he will be away for a year,' said Antonia.

Celer stared at her. He had the beautiful, furious sincerity of the young, and she liked him for it and was sometimes tired of it, the way one is tired of a lamp that burns too brightly in a small room.

'You are not afraid,' he said.

'I am extremely afraid,' she said. 'I am afraid in the way that a man on a ship is afraid of the sea. It does not go away. It becomes part of the work.' She poured him water. 'Sit. Drink. And then tell me what he said in the Senate, in his own words, and not the words the Acta gave him, because the Acta are written by a freedman who loves the sound of his own indirect speech.'

Celer sat. He drank. He said, 'He spoke about the corruption of the city's pleasures. He said that Rome had grown rich and that wealth without ancestry was a fire without a hearth. He said that there were houses in this city where men of the equestrian order were separated from their judgement and their money in the same evening, and that the wives and mothers of Rome had begun to ask why the state permitted such houses to flourish within sight of the temples.' He paused. 'He did not say your name.'

'He never says my name.'

'He said "a certain woman." Twice.'

Antonia looked out of the window. A lizard had come out on the warm stone of the sill and was holding itself perfectly still, as lizards do, as though stillness were a form of armour.

'He has done that for twenty years,' she said. 'When I was thirty he said it in a case about a will. When I was thirty-four he said it in a debate on the corn supply, which had nothing to do with me at all, and three men came to my door that week to see whether I was ruined. He is not a man who forgets. He is a man who keeps accounts, and he has kept an account of me, and he adds to it every year, and one day he will present the bill.'

'Why?' said Celer. 'What did you do to him?'

There was a small silence. In it, Antonia heard the water clock in the corridor, the one that did not work, ticking anyway.

'I made money,' she said. 'In a city where he believes money should belong to men like him. It is the only crime he recognises.'

Celer did not believe her. She could see that he did not believe her, and she could see him decide, out of loyalty and good manners, not to say so, and she was grateful, and she made a note to give him something valuable before the month was out, because a young man who suppresses a question for your sake has earned a payment.

---

Before the afternoon, she went to the baths.

The thermae of Trajan had been open three years and were already the largest thing of their kind in the world: a mountain of brick and marble laid over the slope of the Oppian, with a caldarium that could have held a market and a frigidarium in which the water was so cold that men made offerings to it. Antonia went twice a month, not for the water but for the crowd, because there was no place in Rome where a woman could hear more truth in an hour.

She took with her Myrrhine and two of the women and a slave with the oils, and they went in at the hour when the city's respectable women came, which is to say before the men, and she sat in the tepidarium with her hair pinned up and her feet in the warm water and listened.

Two wives of equestrians were discussing a divorce, in the oblique way that respectable women discuss such things, which is to say that they discussed a house in the country and a daughter's dowry and the difficulty of finding good wool, and in the middle of it one of them said, 'Of course, if he takes her to Baiae in September, everyone will know,' and the other said, 'Everyone has known since March,' and they both looked at Antonia, and looked away.

A merchant from Narbo talked to a merchant from Alexandria about the price of wheat, and the Alexandrian said that the harvest in Egypt had been good and the ships were coming, and the man from Narbo said that the ships were always coming and the price was always rising and there was a something in the city, a nervousness, a rumour that the granaries had been oversold by men who did not have the grain to sell. He said it quietly. He said it in the tone of a man who did not want to be overheard saying that the emperor's annona was being cheated, because that was a thing that could be said by a man with a name and not by a man with a warehouse.

Antonia, with her eyes closed and the warm water moving against her legs, made a small calculation and put it away.

And then a woman came and sat beside her, and said, 'You are Antonia Cassia.'

She was perhaps fifty, dressed in the plain heavy wool of a woman who had never in her life needed to be beautiful, and she had the face of a woman who had spent forty years being obeyed.

'I am.'

'I am Aufidia Severa. My husband is a friend of Lucius Tarquitius.' She arranged her towel. 'I am told you have a house on the Esquiline where men go to do what they cannot do at home.'

'You are well informed.'

'I am told also that you are rich, and that you were not always rich, and that you have a great many friends who would not know you if they met you in the street.' The woman smiled at the water. 'I have wondered, sometimes, what it is like to be in a room full of people who are all, in some way, in your power. It must be very tiring.'

'It is,' said Antonia. 'But it is less tiring than being in a room full of people who are all, in some way, in the power of one man.'

Aufidia Severa turned and looked at her fully for the first time, and there was something in the look that was almost respect, and something that was certainly dislike, and Antonia had been looked at in that way by a hundred women of her class and had long ago stopped needing to know which part was which.

'They say he will be proconsul of Asia,' the woman said.

'They say a great many things in the tepidarium.'

'They say he has asked to see you.'

Antonia did not move. 'Who says that?'

'My husband's secretary's brother is a slave in the house of the Tarquitii,' said Aufidia Severa, and stood, and gathered her towel. 'You should know that in that house, a thing that is said at the second hour is in the city by the sixth. It is not a discreet family. It has never needed to be.' She looked down. 'Whatever he wants from you, he will get it, and then he will punish you for having given it. That is what he does. It is what his father did.'

She went away toward the caldarium. Myrrhine, who had been sitting within earshot with her eyes on the water, said, 'Do you believe her?'

'I believe the first half,' said Antonia. 'The second half is what every woman in Rome believes about every man, and it is usually true, which does not make it information.'

---

In the carriage going back up the hill, Myrrhine said, 'You are going to the Palatine.'

'I am.'

'On the Ides. Alone.'

'Yes.'

Myrrhine looked out at the street, where a man was selling water from a cart and two children were fighting over the dipper. She had been nineteen when Antonia had bought her out of a house in Puteoli, and had been, for two years, the person Antonia loved, and then for twenty-two years the person Antonia trusted, and the two things had been separated so cleanly that neither of them ever spoke of the operation.

'There is a thing I have never asked you,' Myrrhine said.

'Ask it.'

'If he offered you the whole of it, this afternoon — the name, the house on the Palatine, the respectability, the masks in the atrium and the wife's place at the games — would you take it?'

Antonia watched the cart go by.

'No,' she said.

'Because of what he did?'

'Because of what I would have to become to be given it,' said Antonia. 'They would not give me the name. They would give me a place in a house where the name was, and I would spend the rest of my life being grateful for it, and I have been grateful for things before and I did not care for the taste.'

Myrrhine was quiet for a while. Then she said, 'That is not the same as no.'

'No,' said Antonia. 'It is not.'

---

That afternoon, alone, she went up to the room at the top of the west wing that had no window and opened the strongbox.

There were four things in it that were not money. A ring of iron keys, too small for any door in the house. A lock of hair, dark, cut with a knife and tied with a thread that had once been red. A scrap of papyrus on which someone had written a name and a date in a hand that was not hers. And a small clay figure of a woman with her hands over her face, which had been given to her by a slave in Puteoli who had told her that it would keep her from dreaming.

She did not take any of them out. She put her hand flat on the lid of the box and stood there for a while.

Then she went down and told Cinnamus to buy a gift.

'What kind of gift?'

'A book,' she said. 'Something old and dull and expensive. Varro on agriculture. And have it wrapped and sent to the house of Lucius Tarquitius Priscus, for his mother, with my respects, and no letter.'

Cinnamus's stylus stopped.

'To his mother,' he said.

'To his mother.'

'Domina, if I may — a gift to that house will be read as a submission.'

'It will be read as a courtesy,' said Antonia, 'by everyone except the one man who knows it is not. And he will spend the whole of the Ides wondering what it means, and a man who is wondering is a man who is not yet acting. We need six weeks. A dull book buys me six weeks.'

Cinnamus wrote it down. He had a beautiful hand, and he wrote slowly, and he did not look at her, and she was grateful for that too.

---

On the Ides she went to the Palatine at the ninth hour, alone, in a plain litter, with no escort but the two bearers, wearing a dark dress and no jewellery but the carnelian ring. It was the first time she had been on that street in twenty-four years.

The house of the Tarquitii had been in the family for six hundred years, and it showed. Not in splendour: in wear. The stone of the threshold was dished by the feet of thirty generations. The bronze of the door knocker, which was a boar's head, had been polished so often that the bristles had been rubbed nearly smooth. In the atrium, the ancestral masks were arranged in their cupboards, and the wax of the oldest had gone the colour of old honey, and the eyes of them all were holes.

A slave took her name and went away, and came back, and took her into a small room off the peristyle where a woman sat in a chair with a rug over her knees.

Tarquitia Priscilla was seventy-eight. She had been beautiful in a hard, narrow way — the bones were still there, the long jaw and the straight nose of the coins — and she was blind, and her hands were in her lap, and her hands were the only part of her that had not aged, because they had never done anything.

'Cassia,' she said.

'Domina.'

'Come where I can hear you. There is a stool. Do not sit on the couch; the couch is bad for my back and I do not like to hear it being sat on.' The blind eyes moved, not quite toward her. 'You are heavier than you were. Your voice is lower. You have been smoking your throat with that Egyptian stuff, and you have been angry for a long time, and you have not slept properly since the spring.'

Antonia sat on the stool.

'You have not changed,' she said.

'I have changed in every way that cannot be heard.' The old woman's mouth moved, and it was not a smile. 'I hear that you have a house full of beautiful women and that senators go there and forget their own names. I hear that you are richer than my son. I hear that you pay wages, which is said in the city as though you had taught the birds to plough.' She turned her head. 'Was the book your idea?'

'Yes.'

'It was a good book. I have had it read to me twice. Varro is a bore, but he is an honest bore, and there are not many.' A pause. 'He has been in a fury since it came. He believes you are mocking us.'

'I am mocking him.'

'I know. I told him it was mockery and he did not believe me, which is the only advantage of being blind: when I tell a man a thing he does not wish to hear, he can pretend I have not seen it.' Tarquitia Priscilla lifted one hand, and a slave came and put a cup into it, and she drank, and put it back into the slave's hand without looking. 'Why have you come?'

'He summoned me.'

'He summoned you to humiliate you. He will keep you waiting and then he will be cold and then he will offer you something you cannot accept, and when you refuse it he will be entitled to say that you refused him.' The old woman's voice was dry as a husk. 'You should not have come.'

'I had to come.'

'Why?'

Antonia looked at the hands in the old woman's lap. Twenty-four years ago those hands had held hers, once, on a night in November, and had let go.

'Because I want to know what he wants,' she said. 'And because I want to know whether you are still alive, and whether you have anything to say to me.'

The blind face did not move for a long moment. Somewhere beyond the room a young woman's voice was reciting something in Greek, badly, and being corrected, and the correction was patient.

'He does not know what he wants,' Tarquitia Priscilla said at last. 'He has never known. He wanted to be his father, and his father died, and then he wanted to be better than his father, and that is a thing a man can only do in the Senate, where no one can see him. He has spent his life being magnificent in public and unanswerable in private. And he is fifty-three and he has one daughter and no son, and the name will end with him, and that is the only thing he thinks about at night, whatever he tells himself.'

'And the daughter?'

'The daughter is nineteen and she is better than he is and he does not know it.' The old woman's mouth tightened. 'She has been betrothed to a boy with a great name and no money, and she will be married in the winter, and she will have his children, and she will be unhappy in a way that no one is permitted to notice. It is the fate of women in this family. It was my fate. It was my mother's.' Her blind face turned, and it was terrible, because it was not aimed at Antonia and yet struck her. 'You escaped it, Cassia. You escaped it by a door that no decent woman would use, and you are the only one of us who is free, and I have hated you for it for twenty-four years and I am too tired now.'

Antonia's hands were cold. She said, 'I did not escape.'

'No,' said Tarquitia Priscilla. 'I know.'

And then a slave came to the door and said that the master would see her now, and the old woman's face closed like a shutter, and the moment was gone, and Antonia followed the slave down a corridor lined with the masks of dead men into the room where the master of the house was waiting.
