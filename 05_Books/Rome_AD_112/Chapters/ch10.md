# 10 | The Dog Days

By the thirteenth day of August the city had given up.

It gave up in a particular way, which anyone who has lived through a Roman summer knows: not with collapse but with a kind of slow, good-humoured surrender. The rich went to the coast or to the hills, and the ones who stayed sent their wives and children ahead and stayed themselves because of business, and were irritable about it. The poor moved their beds into the street. The markets opened before dawn and shut at the fourth hour. The shops that sold cold drinks did the trade of the year and the shops that sold wool did nothing at all. Dogs lay in the shade of the aqueduct arches and did not move for the flies. The Tiber went down and smelled of mud and old iron, and the sewers smelled of something worse, and the whole city acquired a smell that was, if you had been born in it, simply the smell of August.

The Golden Vine changed too.

In the winter the house was a machine with a roof and a fire and eleven women who were always warm. In the summer it was a house with the shutters closed at the third hour and the floors wet down twice a day and the water jars refilled four times, and the women lay on the roof at night on mattresses dragged up the back stairs, and the whole household became nocturnal, and the work happened between the ninth hour and the second hour of the night, when the air moved.

Antonia slept on the roof with the rest of them. It was the only time of year she did. She would come up at the ninth hour with a pillow and a cup and lie on her back and listen to the women talking in the dark, and they talked in a way they never did in the day: about their mothers, about the towns they came from, about the men who had been kind and the men who had not, about money, about whether it was better to be a free woman in a bad marriage or a slave in a good house. There was no conclusion to any of it. It went on until the second hour and then it stopped, and the whole roof slept, and above it the sky was the colour of a coal that has been left in a brazier overnight.

That was how the house sounded when it was at peace. She was to remember it, afterwards, more clearly than anything else that happened that year: nine women on a roof in the dark, talking about their mothers.

---

Rubrius Celer came on the nineteenth, in the afternoon, when no sane man was out, and he came on foot with his toga over his arm and his face as red as a brick.

He had been at his mother's house at Tusculum for a month, and he had come back to Rome because he had been told, at a dinner, that the curator of the public works had been seen talking to Antonia Cassia in the portico of the basilica; and because he was twenty-six and had no other way of finding out whether it was true.

'It is true,' said Antonia. 'There was a rainstorm and there were four hundred people in the portico and he spoke to me about the grain supply.'

'They are saying—'

'I know what they are saying. I have known since the day it happened, and I have had eleven days of it, and I am tired of it, and you are tired and hot and you have walked up this hill in the sun for nothing.' She poured him water. 'Sit down.'

He sat down. He drank the water. He put the cup down and said, 'I am going to ask you something and I want you to answer it as though I were a man and not a boy.'

'Ask.'

'Do you want me to speak for you in the Senate? About the house. About Sestius.' He looked up. 'I have been a senator for seven months. I have no weight. I know that. But I have a name, and I am not afraid of him, and if I stand up in the autumn and say that the house of Antonia Cassia is a lawful business and that the praetor's son is a liar, it will be said, and it will be said by a Rubrius, and it will be in the Acta, and it will make a difference.'

Antonia looked at him for a long moment.

'It would make a difference,' she said. 'It would also end you. You are twenty-six and you have no money and one speech in the Senate, and if you spend it on me you will be the young man who defended a brothel, and the men who decide who gets the tribunate will read the Acta and they will remember it, and in ten years you will be a man of forty with a name and a grudge and no career. I have seen that man. He is the most bitter thing in Rome.'

'I do not care.'

'You do not care now. You will care at forty.' She sat down opposite him. 'Celer. I am going to tell you a thing that will sound cruel, and I want you to hear it as a kindness. You are the only person in this city who has ever offered me something that cost him something, without being asked and without a price, and I am not going to spend you. Not because I am noble. Because you are worth more to me in ten years as a man of standing who owes me nothing than you are this autumn as a boy who ruined himself for me and blamed me for it.'

He stared at the floor.

'And if you are convicted?' he said.

'Then I will be convicted. I have been poor before and I was not very good at it, and I have got better.' She stood. 'Go back to Tusculum. Do not come to this house again before October, and do not be seen with me. And if you are asked, in the autumn, in the Senate, whether you know me, you will say that you have met me, that I lent you money when your mother's estate was in difficulty, and that you do not know anything about my house because you have never been in it after the ninth hour.' She smiled at him, and it was a real smile. 'That last part is a lie, and you will tell it badly, and everyone will know it is a lie, and they will respect you for it.'

He got up. At the door he said, 'You are always deciding things for people.'

'Yes.'

'Why?'

'Because somebody has to,' said Antonia, 'and because in this city, if you are a woman and you wait to be asked, you will wait until you are dead.'

---

The Nemoralia came on the thirteenth, which was the festival of Diana of the grove, and which was, in the city, the slaves' day.

It had been a slaves' day for as long as anyone could remember, and nobody in authority had ever quite decided why: the goddess of the grove at Nemi had been the goddess of the runaways, and the runaways had become the household slaves, and the household slaves had kept the day, and the city had let them, in the way that the city let a great many things happen that it did not approve of. The custom was to walk out to the grove with torches, and to hang a piece of cloth or a lock of hair on a tree, and to ask for something, and to spend the day in the open air with whoever one liked, and to come back at night.

Antonia gave the household the day. It cost her nothing — the house would have been empty of clients anyway, because the men who came to it were at Baiae — and it bought her a great deal. She had a pig killed and roasted in the courtyard and set out with bread and wine before the household went out at the fourth hour, and she stood at the gate and watched them go: the cooks, the water girl, the boy from the kitchen, the two under-stewards, the laundry women, Prima and Eucharis and Lucilla and four of the others with their hair down and their good clothes on, and Cinnamus with his wife's hand in his, and Meno, who was a Thracian and had been a slave for fifty years and had never once, in all that time, told anyone what he asked for at the grove.

'You are not going?' said Myrrhine.

'I am too old to walk to Nemi in the heat.'

'You are forty-one.'

'And I have been to Nemi,' said Antonia. 'I went when I was twenty-six, and I asked for something, and I got it, and it has been nothing but trouble ever since, and I have decided not to ask for anything else.'

---

Lucilla sang that night on the roof.

She had been in the house three months and had changed in the way that girls of seventeen change when they are fed properly and not frightened: she had put on weight, and her face had settled, and she had stopped apologising for the space she took up. Pamphilus had given her exercises and Eucharis had taught her four songs, and she had a habit, when she thought nobody was listening, of humming under her breath in the corridor.

She sang the song about the swallow, which she had sung at the dinner in May, and she sang it better. The roof was quiet. When she finished, Prima said, from the far end, 'Well. There it is,' which was the highest compliment she had paid anybody in six years.

'She will be worth thirty thousand by the winter,' said Eucharis, later, when Lucilla had gone down to fetch water. 'I have heard women worth thirty thousand. She is better than any of them and she does not know it, which is the thing that makes it work.'

'She will not be sold,' said Antonia.

'She will not be sold,' Eucharis agreed, 'but she will be asked for, and you will have to decide what she is. A singer who is not for sale is a curiosity, and a curiosity can be exhibited for years. A singer who is for sale is a commodity and lasts two seasons. You will have to choose, and whichever you choose, there will be men who hate you for it.'

'You have been thinking about this.'

'I have been free for nine days,' said Eucharis, 'and it has made me impertinent.'

Antonia laughed. Down in the courtyard someone dropped a jug and there was a shout, and the whole roof laughed, and the night went on.

At the second hour, when most of them were asleep, Antonia said, into the dark, 'Myrrhine.'

'Mm.'

'On the twenty-ninth, I want the carriage.'

A pause.

'To Puteoli?'

'To Puteoli. I go every year. You know I go every year.'

'I know you go every year and I know you tell me it is business.' Myrrhine's voice was flat with sleep. 'Do you want company?'

'No.'

'All right.' A longer pause. 'Antonia.'

'Mm.'

'Whatever you find there this year, remember that you have a house full of women who would be on the street in a week if you were not in it, and that they did not choose to be your children, and that they are yours anyway.'

Antonia lay on her back and looked at the sky.

'I know,' she said.

---

The Sestii came on the twenty-first, at the eleventh hour of the night, and they came without warning, which was the point.

Antonia was in the study with Cinnamus, going over the Ostian contracts, when Meno came up the stairs two at a time, which he had not done in nine years, and said, 'Domina. There are three of them at the gate and one of them is the young Sestius and he is drunk and he has brought a litter and he says he will not go away.'

'Is he alone?'

'Two friends and four bearers and a slave. The friends are drunker than he is. One of them has been sick in the street.'

Antonia put down the stylus.

'Where are the women?'

'Prima is in the garden with the physician. Eucharis is in her room. The girl is asleep.'

'Get Brennus and two of the men and put them in the peristyle, not at the gate. Bring the young man into the atrium and nobody else. And Meno — the gate stays open. If he makes trouble, I want the street to see him being put out of it, not the other way round.'

She went down.

Lucius Sestius Cirrus was in worse condition than she had expected. His eyes were loose in his face and his tunic was wet down the front, and he was standing in the middle of her atrium with his hands hanging at his sides, looking at the masks on the wall as though he had never seen a mask before.

'Cirrus.'

He turned. It took him a moment to find her.

'You,' he said.

'I am the only person here who owns the floor you are standing on. Sit down.'

'I am not going to sit down.' He swayed. 'I have come to say something.'

'Say it and then go home.'

'I have come to say that you are a' — he stopped, and breathed, and the breath went on too long — 'that you are a woman who has made a business out of the worst thing in men, and that the whole of this city knows it, and that you have a girl in this house who is worth thirty thousand sesterces and that you are keeping her for a better price, and that everybody knows that too, and that I will not be made a fool of by a freedwoman in a house with a fountain.'

Behind him, in the doorway, Brennus had appeared with two men and was standing very still.

'You have said it,' said Antonia. 'Now go home.'

'I am not finished.'

'You are finished. You have come to my house at the eleventh hour with two drunk men and a slave and you have shouted at me in my own atrium, and if you do it for another minute I will have you carried out into the street and laid on the pavement, and the watch will find you there in the morning and every man in Rome will know by the ninth hour that the son of the praetor was thrown out of a brothel by a woman and two slaves.' She said it quietly. 'You do not want that, Cirrus. Go home.'

He looked at her. And then something happened in his face that she had seen before, in other men, in other rooms, and that she had learned to recognise as the point at which a drunk man stops hearing words.

'I want the girl,' he said.

'No.'

'I will give you forty thousand.'

'No.'

'Sixty.'

'Go home,' said Antonia, 'before you say something that your father cannot undo.'

He came toward her. He was twenty-two and heavy and she was forty-one and not tall, and the distance between them was four feet of mosaic.

'You think you are safe,' he said, 'because you are rich and you have a house and men come to you. You are not safe. You are a freedwoman with a brothel and no name, and my father is a praetor of Rome, and in the winter he will put a seal on your door and take your girls out of this house one at a time and sell them, and you will be in the street with them, and you will come to my house and ask me for work.'

And then his legs went.

It was not a collapse. It was worse than that: he sank, slowly, and sat down on the mosaic in the middle of the atrium with his legs out in front of him, and he began to laugh, and then the laughing stopped and he put his head in his hands and said, 'I am going to be sick.'

Antonia looked down at him for a moment.

'Brennus,' she said. 'Take him into the small room by the garden and put him on the couch and have a basin brought. And find his friends and put them in the garden with a jug of water and tell them that if they make a noise I will have them thrown into the street in their own vomit and I do not care whose fathers they have.'

She went to the door of the small room and stood looking at him as they laid him down. Then she said, to Meno: 'Send for his slave. Not the friends. The slave. Tell him his master is unwell and that he may either take him home in the litter now or stay the night in this house and take him home in the morning when he can walk, and if he stays there will be a man outside the door all night.'

The slave, who was perhaps thirty and looked as though he had done this before, chose the morning.

---

She went up at the first hour of the night and lay on the roof and could not sleep.

At the third hour she went down to check the shutters in the garden, which she did anyway, and stopped in the passage because there was a light in the small room and the door was ajar.

Prima was sitting on the floor beside the couch with her back against the wall.

'What are you doing?' said Antonia.

'Watching him breathe.' Prima did not get up. 'The slave is asleep in the corridor. Meno is at the gate. Brennus has gone to his bed and left the boy from the kitchen, who is fifteen and would run away from a mouse. So I am sitting here.'

'You do not have to.'

'Yes,' said Prima, 'I do. Because if he dies in this house in the night we will need to be able to say exactly who was with him and when, and if he wakes up and puts his hand on a girl in this house I want to be the one he puts it on.' She looked up. 'You should go to bed.'

Antonia sat down on the floor on the other side of the couch.

They sat there in the lamplight with the praetor's son asleep and breathing between them, and the shutters open to the garden, and the smell of the fig tree coming in.

'He will not die,' said Antonia.

'No,' said Prima. 'He will wake up at the fourth hour and be ashamed and go home and be cruel to his slave in the litter. That is what they do.' She was quiet. 'When I was fifteen there was a man in the house in the Argiletum who used to come in the afternoons and sleep in the room at the top of the stairs. He never touched me. He used to give me a copper and ask me to sit on the floor and talk to him about the market. And then one afternoon he did touch me, and it was not the touching, it was that he had been kind first, and I have never been able to explain to anyone in eleven years why that was the worst thing that ever happened to me.'

Antonia looked at the lamp.

'I know,' she said.

'Yes,' said Prima. 'You do.'

---

He was dead at the sixth hour of the morning.

It was Pamphilus who said it, having been sent for at the fifth, and it was Pamphilus who stood in the small room with his bag in his hand and his face grey and said, 'Domina, this is not the wine and it is not the heat. He has taken something. There is a smell to him that I know from the army, and I have seen two men die of it in my life, and the second one was a tribune who took it because he had heard that it made a man a bull, and it made him a bull for one night and then it made him dead.' He wiped his mouth. 'It is Spanish fly. It is bought in the market at the ninth hour by boys who do not know what it does. There will be a box in his clothes.'

There was a box in his clothes. It was a small bronze box with a lotus on the lid, and it was empty, and it was not his, because his friends said afterwards that he had bought it himself in the Forum that afternoon from a man with a monkey.

The praetor's son lay on the couch in the small room in Antonia's house with his mouth open and his face the colour of old wax, and the whole of the house stood in the passage outside the door, and nobody said anything at all.

Antonia looked at him for a long moment.

Then she said, 'Myrrhine. Where is the woman who was with him?'

'There was no woman with him,' said Myrrhine.

'There was a woman with him. He did not come alone into that room and he did not die alone, and the man who was outside the door went to sleep, and I am not going to have it said in this city that a boy died in my house in a locked room and that I cannot produce the person who was in it.' She turned. 'Who was with him?'

The girl's name was Berenice. She was nineteen, she had been in the house a year, and she was in the room over the kitchen with her hands over her ears and had been there since the fourth hour of the night, and when Antonia came in she began to shake so violently that her teeth knocked together.

'Tell me,' said Antonia.

'He woke at the third hour and he was —' The girl could not find the word. 'He was not himself. He was like a man in a fever, and he wanted — and he had the box, and he took more of it, and then he could not breathe properly, and I went and got water and I put it on his face and he did not —' She stopped. 'I did not kill him, domina. I did not even — he did not do anything to me. He could not. He was like that for an hour and then he stopped and I thought he was asleep and I sat on the floor and I fell asleep and when I woke up he was cold.'

Antonia stood in the doorway of the little room over the kitchen, where it was already hot at the sixth hour of the morning, and she made a decision that she would spend the rest of her life paying for.

'Berenice,' she said. 'You are going to go and stay with my physician's sister at Tibur. You will go today, in the carriage with the shutters closed, and you will stay there until I send for you, and you will not speak to anyone about anything.'

'Domina—'

'You did nothing wrong,' said Antonia. 'That is exactly why you cannot stay. If the praetor's household finds you in this house they will have you in a cell by the ninth hour and they will ask you for three days what you did to their master's son, and you will tell them the truth, and it will not matter, because a slave's truth in a cell with a praetor's men is not evidence. You are going to Tibur, and you are going to be alive, and when it is over you will come back, and I will make it up to you.'

She went out. Myrrhine was waiting in the corridor with her arms folded.

'That was a mistake,' said Myrrhine.

'It was mercy.'

'It was both,' said Myrrhine, 'and you have just made the only witness in the house into a woman who has fled in a closed carriage before dawn, and when the praetor's people come here this afternoon — and they will come this afternoon — I will have to stand in the atrium and say that I do not know where she is, and I will be telling the truth, and it will sound exactly like a lie.' She stepped closer. 'You should have kept her. You should have kept her in that room with the door open and the whole household able to see her, and sent for the praetor's steward before you sent for the physician, and had every one of us stand in the passage and say what we saw.'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'Then why?'

Antonia looked at the passage, where the household was standing: the cooks, the water girl, the boy from the kitchen, Brennus, Meno, Eucharis with her hair down, Lucilla with her hands over her mouth. All of them looking at her.

'Because she is nineteen,' said Antonia, 'and because I have been that girl, and because when I was that girl there was nobody in the world who would have put me in a carriage.'

---

Marcus Sestius Verus came at the ninth hour with six men, a steward, and a physician of his own, and he came in the way that a praetor comes: not as a father, but as an officer of the state arriving at a place where the state's business has gone wrong.

Antonia met him in the atrium. She had washed and changed her dress and put on the carnelian ring, and she had had the body laid out in the small room by the garden with the door open and a lamp at the head and two of her own people standing beside it, and she had had the whole household assembled in the peristyle, in order, in clean clothes.

He stopped in front of her and looked at her for a long moment.

'Where is my son?'

'In the room behind you,' said Antonia. 'He has been laid out and nothing has been moved. My physician is here and will show you what he found. There is a box in his clothes that is not mine and that I have not touched, and there is a slave of his own household who was in the corridor outside the door from the third hour of the night, and there is a doorkeeper who will tell you what hour your son came to this house and in what condition.'

Sestius looked past her at the open door.

He was fifty-eight, and he had been handsome, and he had the face of a man who has spent his life being obeyed in a city where obedience is a currency, and he was not prepared for this. That was the thing Antonia saw, and that she was not supposed to see, and that she never forgot: under the praetor's manner there was a man in the middle of a sentence he could not finish.

He went into the room. He was in there for a long time.

When he came out his face had closed like a hand.

'My son,' he said, 'was in good health yesterday. He dined at the house of a friend and he came to this house at the eleventh hour, and by the sixth hour of the morning he was dead, in your house, in a room with your woman in it, and the woman is gone.' His voice was perfectly level. 'The doorkeeper says she left at dawn in a closed carriage.'

'She left at dawn in a closed carriage,' said Antonia.

'Where is she?'

'That is a question I am not going to answer.'

There was a silence in the atrium. The six men behind him did not move, and Brennus, at the door, did not move, and somewhere in the house a fly was hitting itself against a shutter.

'You understand,' said Sestius, 'that I am the praetor of this city.'

'I understand that you are the praetor,' said Antonia. 'And I understand that if I give you the woman, you will have her in a cell in the vigiles' barracks by nightfall, and in three days she will have confessed to whatever your household requires, and then your son will have been murdered by a slut in a brothel instead of having been killed by a box of Spanish fly that he bought himself in the Forum at the ninth hour with money from his own purse, and the second of those things is true and the first is convenient.' She took a step toward him. 'I have nineteen women in this house and a doorkeeper and an atriensis and a physician, and every one of them will stand up in front of a magistrate and say what happened, and my books will be produced, and the box will be produced with the seal of the man who sold it, if I can find him. I do not intend to be tried for your son's death in the court of public opinion in the month of August. If you want to charge me, charge me. The courts open in October.'

Sestius looked at her.

'You have made an enemy of me,' he said. 'Do you understand that? Not a man who dislikes you. Not a man who will speak against you in the Senate. An enemy. In this city an enemy is a man who wakes up in the morning and thinks about you before he thinks about his own affairs, and I will think about you every day until one of us is dead.'

'I have had enemies before,' said Antonia.

'Not like me.'

'No,' she said. 'Not like you. You are the first one who has ever been in my house as a guest, and eaten my food, and put his hand up the skirt of a woman who worked for me, and gone home and told his wife that the wine was poor.' She turned away. 'Take your son home, Sestius. You may take him out of my house and you may have the physician's report and the box, and I will send you the names of the men who came with him, and the name of the shop where he bought it if I can find it. That is all I have. There is nothing else in the world that I can give you, and if you stay in this house one minute longer I will begin to think that you came here for something else.'

He stood very still. Then he said, to his steward, 'Bring my son.'

They carried Cirrus out of the house through the garden gate with a cloth over him, and the whole street came out to watch, and Antonia stood in the peristyle with her household behind her and watched it go.

When it was gone, and the gate was closed, and the sound of the litter had gone down the hill, Myrrhine said, quietly, 'You have just told the praetor of Rome that his son died in a brothel because he was a fool, and you said it in front of six witnesses.'

'Yes.'

'Why?'

Antonia stood in the middle of her peristyle in the heat of an August afternoon.

'Because it is true,' she said, 'and because in this city a true thing said out loud in front of witnesses is the only kind of armour there is, and because if I had been soft with him he would have thought I was afraid, and he would have been right.'

---

The house did not open that night.

It did not open for eleven nights, which was the longest it had been closed in eleven years, and Antonia sat in the study on the first of them with a cup of wine she did not drink and the box of tablets open in front of her, and wrote, in her own hand, on a tablet that she would later burn:

*The girl is safe at Tibur. The body is with his father. The box is with his father. Berenice is safe and will not be found and I have made an enemy who will think about me every day for the rest of his life.*

*And on the twenty-ninth I am going to Puteoli, and I will stand in a garden at the bottom of the Lucrine road, and I will be forty-one years old, and there is a woman somewhere in this city who is twenty-five, and I do not know her name.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then she burned it in the brazier, at the ninth hour of the evening, and went up onto the roof, where eleven women were lying in the dark, and lay down among them, and did not sleep.
