# 17 | The Defence

The games lasted three days, and the whole city went to them, and the case was the thing that everyone discussed in the intervals.

Antonia did not go to the games. She spent the three days in the house with the shutters open and the doors unlocked and the baker's family in the west wing, and she did four things.

The first was to find the man with the monkey.

Hilarus found him in a day and a half: a small, quick, frightened man named Scylax who sold cures and amulets and boxes of things in the Forum from a tray, and who had sold a bronze box with a lotus on the lid to a young man in a good tunic on the afternoon of the nineteenth of August for eleven sesterces and had told him exactly how much to take and had told him, twice, that more than that would kill him.

He had also, being a careful man in a careless trade, kept the nine sesterces separate, because they were the largest single sale he had made that month, and he remembered the young man, and he remembered the two friends, and he remembered that one of them had laughed and said, *he will not need it, he has no trouble in that direction.*

'Will he say that in court?' said Antonia.

'He will say it for four hundred sesterces and a guarantee that he is not prosecuted for selling a noxious substance,' said Hilarus, 'and I have given him both, and he has signed a statement, and he is frightened enough to be honest, which is the best combination available in a witness.'

In the intervals of all this, the city went to the games.

Myrrhine brought back the reports, because Myrrhine went, on the second day, with two of the women and a basket of food, and came home at the ninth hour with her ears ringing and her opinions sharpened.

'They are selling dolls of you,' she said. 'In the portico of Pompey. Little clay figures with a house on the base, and you can take the roof off. Eleven sesterces, and the man has sold four hundred.'

'What do I look like?'

'You look like a woman with a large nose and a key in her hand, and there is a scroll under your arm that says VIRTUS on it, which I think is meant kindly.' Myrrhine sat down and took off her sandals. 'The talk in the crowd is that you poisoned the boy and that you will be acquitted because you have half the Senate in your book, which is not true, and because you are the emperor's mistress, which is not true either, and one man near me said that you had been a gladiator. I did not correct him.'

'Why not?'

'Because he was enjoying himself,' said Myrrhine, 'and because the truth is going to be in the Acta tomorrow and he can read it then, and because I have discovered in the last four days that I do not care what the city thinks of you, and that this is a great relief, and that I should have discovered it years ago.' She rubbed her feet. 'The chariot racing was very good. There was a crash at the second turn and a man was killed and the whole crowd stood up at once. I have never seen anything like it and I do not want to see it again.'

The baker's family stayed in the west wing for four days.

The baker was called Gaius and he was fifty and he had lost his shop and his oven and his stock and he cried on the first night and then stopped and spent the next three days doing things: he mended the hinge on the garden door, which had been broken for nine years and which Meno had sworn was beyond repair, and he built a new frame for the kitchen fire, and he made bread in the house's oven for the whole household every morning at the fourth hour, and by the third day the entire house had rearranged itself around the fact that there was fresh bread at the fourth hour.

On the last morning Antonia found him in the courtyard with a bag of tools.

'I have no money to pay you,' he said. 'I had eleven sesterces and they went in the fire. So I am paying in work, and I will go on paying in work until the shop is rebuilt, and the whole street knows I am in your house, so the whole street knows that you took us in, and that is worth more than money to you at the moment, and I am not a fool and I know it.'

'You are not a fool,' said Antonia.

'I am a baker,' said Gaius, 'and I have been in this street for twenty-two years and I have watched your house for all of it, and I have said things about it in the wineshop that I am not proud of.' He put the bag down. 'And when my shop burned, your doorkeeper was the first man in the street with a bucket, and your women carried water for three hours in their good clothes, and I have been trying to work out ever since what I am going to say to the wineshop keeper, and I have decided that I will say that I was wrong, and that I do not know what goes on in that house, and that they took my family in when nobody else did.'

---

The second thing was that she wrote out, in her own hand, on eleven tablets in a fair copy, the whole account of her life from the day she was sold at Puteoli to the day she opened the house on the Esquiline, including the parts that no one in Rome had ever heard, and gave it to Tarquitius.

He read it in the study on the second night of the games, with the lamp between them and the shutters open and the noise of the city coming in.

At the end he put it down and sat for a while without speaking.

'You are going to put me in the box,' he said.

'I am going to put myself in the box,' said Antonia. 'You are going to ask me questions.'

'They will destroy you.'

'They will try.'

He turned the tablets over.

'You have written here that you were sold for two thousand four hundred sesterces. You have written that you were in the house of Caesonius Verus for six years and that you bought your freedom with a hundred and ten thousand. You have written the name of the man who bought you and the name of the woman who delivered you and the name of the house.' He looked up. 'You have not written the name of the father of your child.'

'No.'

'Why not?'

'Because it is not my secret to give away,' said Antonia, 'and because in three days you are going to stand in that court and be asked whether you have ever been in my house, and you will say no, and it will be true, and it will be the last true thing you say in this case that costs you anything.' She met his eyes. 'If I put your name in that box, the whole of it comes out — the villa at Arretium, your father, your sister, the child, the bill of sale. You will be destroyed and so will your mother and so, most of all, will Tertia, who has not agreed to any of it and who will be sitting in the gallery with a stylus behind her ear while the whole city learns what she is.'

Tarquitius sat back.

'I have thought about that for four months,' he said. 'I have thought about nothing else. Do you know what I have concluded?'

'Tell me.'

'That the truth is not a thing that can be kept in a cupboard,' said Tarquitius, 'and that if I stand up in that court and defend you as a woman of business whose house is a lawful business, and if I am clever and fortunate and we are acquitted, then I will have spent the whole of it — my name, my mother's silence, my daughter's marriage, your twenty-five years — to obtain a verdict that says that Antonia Cassia is a woman of business.' He put his hand flat on the tablets. 'And it will be a lie. Not the verdict. The frame. I will have defended the wrong woman. I will have defended the woman they can tolerate, and the woman they cannot tolerate is the one who is actually standing there, and if I do that, then all of it — the villa, the linen room, the child, all of it — will have been for nothing at all.'

'What are you saying?'

'I am saying that I am going to ask you the question,' said Tarquitius, 'and you are going to answer it, and whatever happens after that, happens.'

---

The court sat again on the fourth day after the games, and the hall was fuller than before, and there were men standing in the porticoes and in the street outside, and the presiding magistrate had the doors shut at the third hour and put two cohorts of the watch at them.

Tarquitius spoke for two hours and eleven minutes.

He began with the box. He produced Scylax, who stood with his tray in his hand and his eyes on the floor and told the court what he had sold and what he had said and what the friends had said, and Scaevola cross-examined him for a quarter of an hour and got nothing but a clearer account, and at the end of it the hall was in no doubt at all that Marcus Sestius Cirrus had bought a box of Spanish fly, in the Forum, in the afternoon, from a man with a monkey, in front of witnesses.

He produced the physician Pamphilus, who had been in the house for eleven years and was sixty-one and had been in the army and had no fear of consulars, and who testified that the symptoms of cantharides poisoning were as he had described them in writing on the morning of the twentieth of August, and that the praetor's physician had given an opinion of vegetable poison without examining the stomach, on a body which had been removed by the prosecutor's own household before any magistrate had been informed.

He produced the books.

Cinnamus brought them in in four boxes and set them on the table, and Tertia's fair copy of the contract book was laid before the court, and Tarquitius read out, one after another, the terms on which eleven women had bought their freedom in eleven years, and the wages paid, and the sums advanced, and the two marriages and the one return, and the manumission of Eucharis eleven days before the charge was laid.

He read out the entry for Doris, sold in June to a baker, and then bought back in July by the household of the praetor, and then he stopped.

'Judges,' he said, 'the woman Doris is not in this court. She is dead. She died on the fourth day of September in the household of the prosecutor, of a fever, according to a physician; and she was buried on the fifth day outside the Colline gate without a stone. I cannot prove that she was killed. I have no body and no witness and no physician who will say otherwise, and I am not going to stand in this court and allege murder without evidence, because I am a jurist and because it would be a lie.' He turned a page. 'But I will say this. She was bought back by the man who is prosecuting this case, and she was in his house for six weeks, and she died there, and no one has explained why a household that had bought a woman for her evidence buried her in an unmarked grave in five days. The court may make of it what it wishes.'

There was a silence in the hall of a different quality from the noise of the first day.

'And the woman Berenice,' said Tarquitius, 'who was produced in this court on the first day with her hands bound, and who told you that she was taken to a barn and questioned four times and put a hand on her throat and told she would be sold to the mines — the woman Berenice is not a witness for the prosecution. She is a witness for the truth, and she was found by the prosecutor's men and frightened by the prosecutor's men, and she told the truth anyway, and I ask the court to notice that.'

He sat down.

The hall was quiet, and the presiding magistrate said, 'The court will hear the accused.'

---

Antonia stood up.

She had thought about what to wear for a long time, which was absurd, and she had decided in the end on the plain dark blue dress she had worn to the dedication, with no jewellery but the carnelian ring, and her hair pinned up in the way she had worn it since she was thirty.

She walked to the space between the rail and the platform, and she stood in front of twenty-two men who had all, at some point in their lives, owned another human being.

Tarquitius began.

'Your name?'

'Antonia Cassia.'

'Your condition?'

'I am a freedwoman. I was freed at Puteoli in the year of the consulship of Domitian and Corbulo, at the age of twenty-two, by the heir of the man who had owned me, and the manumission was registered in the town records and a copy is among the papers before this court.'

'You are the owner of a house in the fifth region, the house in which Marcus Sestius Cirrus died.'

'I am.'

'What is that house?'

Antonia looked at the judges.

'It is a house where men come,' she said. 'They come in the evening. They eat, and they drink, and they listen to music, and some of them go upstairs, and some of them do not. There are nineteen women in the house at present, of whom eleven are what the prosecution calls the business, and eight are cooks and laundresses and a physician and a doorkeeper, and one is a girl of seventeen who sings and is not for sale.' She paused. 'The men pay. The women are paid. They are paid wages, at the end of the month, in coin, and entered in a book, and they may leave when they choose and eleven of them have, and three have come back.'

'And you take a share.'

'I take a share,' said Antonia. 'It is my house. I bought it and I keep it and I pay the taxes on it, and I take a share, and I have made a considerable fortune, and I am not going to pretend in front of this court that I have not.'

'Have you ever sold a woman?'

'Twice. Once in the fourth year, to a man in Ostia at her own request and with a contract, and once in the seventh year, a woman who stole from the house, and I sold her to a baker in the fourth region because the alternative was to have her flogged and put in the street.' She looked at the judges. 'I am not going to tell this court that I am a kindly woman who has never done anything hard. I have done a great many hard things in that house. I have turned men away and I have taken their money and I have kept the accounts honestly and I have never once sold a girl to a house worse than mine, and I have refused that four times, and one of the men I refused is sitting in the second row of this court.'

The hall stirred.

'That is a dangerous thing to say,' said Tarquitius.

'It is a true thing to say,' said Antonia, 'and I have been told by a woman of seventy-eight that I am to be dull and truthful and to say no more than is true, and I am doing my best.'

'Then tell the court how you came to this house.'

And she told them.

She told it in the flat voice she used for the accounts, and she did not hurry, and she did not once look at the gallery. She told them about the villa above the Arno, and the linen room with the green glass window, and the word *eleutheria* that she had copied onto a scrap of wood and kept in a box. She told them about the steward who had told her to ask for a paper, and about not asking. She told them about the house at Puteoli and the woman who had told her the child was dead, and about the six years and the book she had learned to keep, and about the hundred and ten thousand sesterces and the room above a laundry on the Aventine and the first thing she had bought, which was a lock.

She did not tell them about the villa's son.

When she had finished there was a silence in the hall that went on for a long time.

'You were sold,' said Tarquitius. 'At Puteoli. For two thousand four hundred sesterces.'

'I was.'

'How do you know the price?'

'Because a man came to the house with the papers when I was sixteen years old and read them out to me in the courtyard,' said Antonia, 'and I have remembered it for twenty-five years, and when I was thirty I paid a clerk four sesterces to find the record in the town books, and I have the copy.'

'Why?'

'Because I wanted to know what I had cost,' said Antonia, 'and because a woman who knows what she cost can never be told that she was given anything.'

Tarquitius was quiet for a moment. Then he said, 'Judges, I have no further questions. But there is one thing I wish to say to this court, and I will say it as the advocate for the accused and not as a witness, and the court may weigh it accordingly.'

He turned to the platform.

'Everything that has happened in this case has happened because a young man of twenty-two died in a house where he should not have been, having taken a substance he should not have been able to buy. That is a tragedy and it is not a crime, and the man who sold him the substance is in this court and has testified. The prosecution has brought this case not because the accused poisoned a boy, but because the city has decided, over twenty years, that the existence of a house like hers is a scandal, and because a praetor of Rome is a convenient man to be scandalised.'

'And I say to you that this is the oldest mistake in our law. We have a city full of men who buy and sell, and we have built an empire on the buying and selling of human beings, and the law has arranged itself, over four hundred years, so that the buying is honourable and the selling is a trade, and the woman who is bought is a scandal. She sits in this court today because she is the only person in the whole of this affair who was ever, herself, a thing for sale, and because we would rather try her than look at what that means.'

'My client is not innocent of everything. She has taken money from men for things that this court would not permit in its own household, and she has done it lawfully, in a city whose laws permit it, and she has done it better and more honestly than the men who condemn her do their own business. She is not a good woman. She is a woman who has been a slave, and who has made herself free, and who has made herself rich, and who has kept eleven other women out of the street by the only means this city left to her.'

'If you convict her, you will not be convicting a poisoner. You will be saying, in public, in the sight of this city, that a woman who was sold at sixteen and who built a house at thirty and who pays her women wages at forty is a thing that must be put down. And the whole of Rome will hear you say it, and the whole of Rome will agree with you, because the whole of Rome has been saying it for four hundred years in every transaction it has ever made.'

He sat down.

---

Before she stepped down, Scaevola rose.

He was thirty-four and he had a narrow, intelligent, unhappy face, and he had spent the whole day watching the hall turn, and he knew he had lost. What he did in the next quarter of an hour was the last thing available to him, and it was not a lie, and it did more damage to him than to her.

'Domina,' he said, 'I am going to ask you four questions and I would be grateful if you would answer them as briefly as you did the advocate's.'

'Ask.'

'You have told this court that you have never poisoned anyone. Is that correct?'

'It is correct.'

'Have you ever in your life wished a man dead?'

Antonia looked at him.

'Yes,' she said.

'How many?'

'Two. The first was a man in a house at Puteoli who put out a girl's eye with a bronze lamp in my fourth year, and he died of a fever eleven months later, and I did not kill him, and I was glad.' She said it without heat. 'The second is standing at the prosecutors' table and I have wished him dead every day since the fourth of September, when a woman in his household died of a fever in five days.'

There was a noise in the hall.

'That is a very convenient answer,' said Scaevola.

'It is a true one,' said Antonia. 'I have discovered in the last four days that the two are not the same, and that this court is not used to hearing the second one spoken out loud.'

Scaevola's jaw worked.

'You have told the court about a child,' he said. 'You have told the court that you were delivered of a child at Puteoli and told that it died, and that you have spent twenty-five years believing it. Is that true?'

'It is true.'

'Have you ever — since the day you were freed, when you have had money, and house, and influence — have you ever made any attempt to find that child?'

The hall went quiet.

'No,' said Antonia.

'Why not?'

'Because I was told that she was dead and buried in a garden,' said Antonia, 'and because for twenty years I gave money every year to a wall around a piece of turned earth, and because I was a woman of thirty with a strongbox and a lock and no power at all in law, and because a midwife in a small town had told me that it was done and there was no stone and no record and I believed her.' She stopped. 'And because — this is the part that you want, advocate, and I am going to give it to you for nothing — there was a part of me that did not want to look. Because as long as she was dead I could be a woman with a sorrow, and if I looked and found that she was alive somewhere in a house like mine, I would have to be a woman who had not looked for twenty years.'

Scaevola stood very still.

'And now?' he said.

'And now I have found her,' said Antonia.

The hall came apart.

It took the magistrate four minutes, and the guard had to be called, and men in the gallery were standing on the benches, and the presiding magistrate stood up and shouted twice and then, when that did not work, sat down and waited with his hands folded until the noise had exhausted itself, and then said, in a voice of terrible patience:

'This court is not a theatre. If there is one more demonstration I will clear the hall and sit in the empty room with the advocates and the accused, and the city may read the Acta like everybody else. The witness will not answer questions that do not relate to the charge. The court has heard enough.'

He looked at Scaevola.

'Advocate, have you anything further?'

Scaevola looked at the papers on his table for a long moment. Then he said, 'No, your honour.'

'Then we are finished for today,' said the magistrate, 'and tomorrow we vote, and I am going home, and I would like the whole of this city to consider, while it is asleep, the fact that a woman has just told the truth in a court of law and the court had to be cleared because the truth was too interesting.'

---

Scaevola asked her nothing more. He stood for a moment with his tablets in his hand and looked at the platform, and then he said, 'The prosecution has no further witnesses,' and sat down beside a man who would not look at him.

The presiding magistrate looked at the court.

'The court will consider,' he said. 'Judges, we will vote in the morning. The accused will remain in the custody of her own household, on her own recognizance, and I will take a bond of one hundred thousand sesterces, and if she leaves the fifth region before the court sits I will have every one of you' — he looked at the platform — 'explain to the emperor why.'

---

---

That night the house was quiet in a way it had not been all year.

The women had gone up to the roof because the night was warm for November, and the baker's family were asleep in the west wing, and Meno was at the gate, and Cinnamus was in the tablinum with the books, and Myrrhine was in the study with a lamp and a cup of wine she had not touched.

Antonia went up to the room at the top of the west wing, where a girl of nineteen with soot on her hands had slept for three nights, and knocked on the door and went in.

Marcellina was awake, sitting up in bed with her arms around her knees.

'I have been thinking,' she said, 'about something my grandmother said. She said that in this family the men are magnificent in public and unanswerable in private, and the women keep the accounts and the peace and their mouths shut, and that she did it for sixty years and was never once happy.' She looked up. 'I have been in your house for three days. Nobody in this house keeps their mouth shut. The cook shouts at the atriensis. The atriensis shouts at the doorkeeper. The doorkeeper shouts at everybody and then makes the bread at the fourth hour for the baker, which he is not supposed to do, and the baker lets him, and yesterday I heard the physician tell you that you were a fool, and you said, "Yes, but I am a fool who is going to be acquitted," and he said, "Not if you keep eating nothing," and then he gave you a powder and stood over you until you took it.'

'He is sixty-one,' said Antonia. 'He has earned the right.'

'That is what I mean,' said Marcellina. 'I have been in a house for three days where a physician of sixty-one can tell the woman who owns it that she is a fool, in front of a copyist, and nobody is shocked, and the house does not fall down.' She put her chin on her knees. 'My father would not have permitted it. Not because he is cruel. Because in my father's house there is only one person who may be right at a time, and it is him, and everyone knows it, and the whole house is arranged around it, and it is the most tiring thing in the world.'

Antonia sat down on the end of the bed.

'He is not a bad man,' she said.

'I know that.'

'He is a man who was told a lie when he was thirty years old and who built a life around not looking at it,' said Antonia, 'and in the morning he is going to stand up in that court and hear the verdict on a woman he has been told to despise, and in the afternoon the urban prefect is going to lay a document on a fire before the Senate, and by the evening the whole city is going to be talking about something else.' She looked at the girl. 'And then, in a week or a month, you are going to have to go and see him, and it will be the hardest thing you have ever done, and you will do it, and he will say something that hurts you, and you will not shout at him, because he is your father and he is fifty-three and he has lost a daughter and a marriage in the same winter, and you will say what you have to say and leave.'

'And if he will not listen?'

'Then he will not listen,' said Antonia, 'and you will have said it, and in twenty years you will not be a woman with a cupboard in her house.'

Marcellina was quiet for a moment.

'May I ask you something else?'

'Ask.'

'The woman in the gallery. The copyist with the stylus. Tertia.' She looked up. 'She was crying when you said that you had found her. And she went out at the end, before the court was cleared, and she did not come back, and nobody went after her.'

Antonia was silent.

'Is she?' said Marcellina.

'Yes.'

Marcellina nodded slowly.

'I thought so,' she said. 'She has your hands.'

---

They came out into the Forum at the seventh hour of the following morning and the crowd was so thick that it took them a quarter of an hour to get from the doors to the steps.

'What will happen?' said Marcellina.

She was in the grey dress with her hair pinned and she had been in the gallery all day with a stylus, taking notes, because Antonia had told her to take notes.

'Nobody knows,' said Antonia.

'You know,' said Marcellina. 'You have known since the first hour. I watched you. You were counting votes on your fingers under the table when Scaevola was speaking.'

Antonia looked at her.

'Eleven to acquit,' she said. 'Six to convict. Five undecided, of whom three are afraid of the emperor's opinion and two are afraid of their wives' fathers. If the presiding magistrate does not push them, we win by four. If he pushes them, we win by one.' She took her arm. 'It is not the verdict that worries me. It is the morning.'

'Why?'

'Because in the morning, before the court sits, the urban prefect is going to lay a document before the Senate about a fire in the ninth region nine years ago,' said Antonia, 'and the document names the prosecutor.'

Marcellina stopped walking.

'You did that,' she said.

'I did that in September,' said Antonia, 'and I did it because I was a woman with no power in a city with no law for her, and because I had a document in a strongbox and a man who had been cheated for six years by the man who was coming for me.' She started walking again. 'It will not be in this court and it will not affect this verdict. It will be in the Senate in three weeks, and there will be a prosecution, and Sestius will be ruined or he will not, and whichever it is, it will be said in the city forever that Antonia Cassia was acquitted on the day the praetor fell.' She looked at the steps ahead. 'That is not justice either. But it is the closest thing available, and I have been in this city for more than a decade and I have learned that the closest thing available is what a woman gets.'
