# 20 | Blood in the Courtyard

The Ides of November was a cold, bright day with a wind from the north, and the house of the Tarquitii in the morning smelled of woodsmoke and the last of the roses.

Antonia went in through the front gate, in the morning, and gave her name to the doorkeeper, as she had been told to.

The doorkeeper was not the same man. He was young, and he took her name and went away and came back, and then the steward came, and the steward looked at her for a moment with an expression she could not read and said, 'He is in the garden, domina. He has been in the garden since the second hour. He does not want to be in the study.'

He was standing by the wall at the end of the peristyle with his hands behind his back, looking at a fig tree, and he turned when he heard her, and for a moment neither of them said anything at all.

'You came to the front gate,' he said.

'You told me to.'

'I did not think you would.' He gestured at the wall. 'This tree was planted by my great-grandfather. It is dying. It has been dying for eleven years and every year the gardener says it should come down and every year I say it may have one more year.' He turned. 'Where is she?'

'She is not coming.'

He stood quite still.

'She has asked for a month,' said Antonia. 'She has asked to be left alone for a month to decide what she wants, and I have promised her that she will not be written to and that I will not come to her, and I am not going to break that promise for you or for anybody. She will come to dinner at my house on the twenty-fourth of December, and she will bring the woman she lives with, and she is going to be called Metrodora, because it was my mother's name and because she has decided that she will not have a name out of a book.'

Tarquitius put his hand out and steadied himself on the wall.

'Metrodora,' he said.

'Yes.'

'That is a slave's name.'

'It was my mother's name,' said Antonia. 'She was a Bithynian and she was sold twice before she was twenty and she died when I was six. It is a slave's name and a free woman's name and now it will be hers, and in a hundred years there will be a stone in this city with that name on it and no one will know where it came from.' She stood beside him at the wall. 'She has a list of eleven questions. She has read her own bill of sale four times. She has the cloth she was wrapped in and she has your mother's letter, and she has not read it, and she has put it in her sleeve and gone home to the fourth region to think.'

'And she knows my name.'

'She knows your name. She wrote it on a tablet on the first day of the trial, because she writes down the names of everyone who speaks.'

Tarquitius made a sound that was not quite a laugh.

'I have been in the same room with her for six days,' he said. 'I stood up in that court and I spoke for two hours and eleven minutes, and my daughter was in the gallery above my head with a stylus, writing down my name.' He turned and looked at the dying fig tree. 'What does she want?'

'At the moment, nothing from you,' said Antonia. 'She has not asked about you. She has not asked to see you. She asked me whether you had wanted her and I told her the truth, which is that you wanted to do the right thing, which is not the same as wanting a child, and that I did not know what you would have done if you had known, and that nobody would ever know.' She looked at him. 'That is more than most men in your position would be given. Do not spoil it by going to the fourth region.'

'I am not going to the fourth region,' said Tarquitius.

'Swear it.'

'I swore it in August,' he said, and turned. 'I have kept every promise I have made to you this year, and I have made four, and I am fifty-three years old and it is the only thing I have done in the whole of my life that I am not ashamed of.' He walked a few paces away and came back. 'There is a thing I have to tell you. It is not about her. It is about the Senate.'

'Tell me.'

'The province is gone. Asia. The lot was drawn in October and my name was not in the urn; the consul wrote to me to say that he had been instructed to withdraw it. The instruction came from the palace. Nothing was said about the trial and nothing was said about you, but every man in Rome knows what it means.' He said it flatly. 'The emperor does not punish a man for defending a client. He simply decides that a man who has been in a basilica for six days defending a brothel-keeper is a man who is not suitable for a province in a year when the emperor is building a forum.'

'You knew that would happen.'

'I knew it would happen in the spring, when I took the case. I did not know it would happen in this particular way, on this particular day, in a letter from a consul who has never liked me.' He looked at her. 'I am not telling you this so that you will be sorry for me. I am telling you because I am going to be a man with no province and no career and a great deal of time, and because in twenty-five years I have never once been in this position, and I find that I do not know what to do with an afternoon.'

Antonia looked at him.

'You could learn to be a father,' she said. 'You have two daughters. One is nineteen and has left your house and is living in mine. The other is twenty-five and lives above a bakery in the fourth region and has asked for a month.'

'I know,' said Tarquitius. 'That is what I have been standing in this garden thinking about since the second hour.' He put his hands behind his back. 'I am going to ask you something, and you may refuse, and if you refuse I will not ask again.'

'Ask.'

'When she comes to dinner on the twenty-fourth of December,' he said, 'will you tell her that her father asked to be at the table, and that he was told no, and that he accepted it?'

'I will tell her.'

'And will you tell her that he has written her a letter and not sent it?' He took a folded paper out of his sleeve. 'It is not a letter of explanation. I have written eleven of those and burned them; the first was in September and it was four pages and it was about me. This one is one page and it is about her. It says that she is not obliged to read it, and that if she reads it she is not obliged to answer it, and that if she answers it she may say anything at all, including that she wishes never to hear from me again, and that I will abide by it.' He held it out. 'I am not asking you to give it to her. I am asking you to have it, and to give it to her if she asks, and to burn it if she does not.'

Antonia took the letter.

'You have learned something,' she said.

'I have learned that a man of fifty-three cannot make up for twenty-five years in an afternoon,' said Tarquitius, 'and that the only thing he can do is stand in a garden and be available.' He looked at the tree. 'I am going to have it cut down in the spring. It is a great deal of trouble to keep a dying thing alive and it has been dying since my father died, and I have been keeping it out of sentiment and not out of love, and I have decided that there is a difference.'

---

The attack came at the second hour of the night, on the twenty-ninth day of November.

The house had been quiet for a fortnight. The men at the corner had gone after the verdict and had come back a week later, two of them, and had stood at the fountain in the way of men who are being paid by the day and are bored. Meno had counted them. Brennus had walked past them twice and had come back and said, 'They are not from the prefect's office and they are not thieves. They are hired men from the fourth region and they are being paid too much to be standing at a fountain for a fortnight, which means somebody is waiting for something.'

'Waiting for what?'

'For us to get tired of looking at them,' said Brennus. 'A house that has been watching a corner for a fortnight stops watching it in the third week. That is when a thing happens.'

So they did not stop watching.

There were four of them. They came over the garden wall at the second hour, at the corner where the cypress was, and they came quietly, and they had a ladder and a crowbar and a jar of something that smelled of pitch, and they had not counted on a Sequani freedman of forty-four with a stick and nine years of army service and no intention of being surprised in the third week.

Antonia was in the study with Cinnamus when she heard the noise.

It was not a shout. It was a sound she had heard once before, in a street in Puteoli when she was twenty-three, and it was the sound of two bodies meeting a wall.

She was on her feet and through the door before Cinnamus had put down his stylus, and she was in the peristyle when the second sound came: a man screaming, short and high and cut off, and then the crash of something falling into the fountain.

The garden was dark and full of movement.

She saw Meno in the doorway of the kitchen court with a lamp in one hand and his stick in the other, and she saw the boy from the kitchen running with a bucket, and she saw a man on the ground by the wall with his legs moving, and she saw Brennus.

He was standing in the middle of the garden with the crowbar in his hand, and there was a man in front of him with a knife, and they were circling, and Brennus was saying something in a low steady voice that was not Latin.

Then the second man came from behind the fig tree.

Antonia saw it and could not shout in time. Brennus turned and the knife went into him under the arm and he sat down against the wall, quite slowly, with a look of enormous surprise, and the crowbar fell out of his hand and rang on the stone.

Then everything happened at once.

Meno hit the man with the knife across the back of the head with his stick. The boy from the kitchen threw the bucket, which was full, and the water went over everyone. Prima came out of the passage by the bath with the iron bar from the garden door in both hands — she had been in the bath house, she had heard, she had taken the bar out of its brackets — and she hit the man who had come from behind the fig tree on the side of the head, once, and he went down and did not get up.

The fourth man went back over the wall.

The third man — the one who had fallen into the fountain — was on his knees in the water with his hands over his head, and Meno stood over him with the stick and did not hit him again.

'Brennus,' said Antonia.

He was sitting against the wall with his hand pressed to his side and his face grey. There was a great deal of blood, more than she had ever seen, and it was going into the gravel and into the folds of his tunic.

'Do not move him,' said Pamphilus, from the doorway, in the voice of a man who had been in the army. 'Do not move him. Get me light and cloths and the box from my room, and get that man out of the water and tie him to the gate.'

---

Brennus died at the fourth hour of the morning.

He was conscious for most of it. Pamphilus had put a pad on the wound and had packed it and had put his own hands on it and had said, at some point, very quietly, to Antonia, 'It is the artery under the arm. I have seen two of these. There is nothing that can be done and he knows it.'

He knew it. He had been a soldier.

They brought him into the small room by the garden and laid him on the couch — the same couch, and Antonia thought of it and could not stop thinking of it, on which a praetor's son had died in August — and the whole household came in, one at a time, and said something to him, and he answered some of them.

Eucharis kissed his forehead. The boy from the kitchen cried and Brennus told him to stop being a fool and then said that he had done well with the bucket.

Meno sat with him for a long time and they spoke in Thracian, and Antonia, standing in the doorway, understood not one word of it, and Meno came out afterwards with his face perfectly composed and went to his gate and did not speak to anyone for two days.

At the end, when it was nearly light, Brennus said, 'Domina.'

She came and knelt by the couch.

'The letter,' he said. 'To Lugdunum. The magistrate.'

'It was sent in September,' said Antonia. 'There has been no answer. I told you that. I will not lie to you now.'

'No,' he said. 'You would not.' He was quiet for a moment. 'There is money in the box in my room. Three hundred thousand and eleven sesterces, and the eleven is for the boy from the kitchen; tell him it is for the bucket.' His hand moved on the blanket. 'And domina, there is a thing I have never told you. About the two in Lugdunum. The elder was six and the younger was three, and I have written twice a year for nine years and there has never been an answer.' He stopped. 'I have decided that no answer is an answer. I decided it in the summer, in the street, when I told you so, and I have been thinking about it ever since, and I have decided that I was wrong.'

'Brennus—'

'No,' he said. 'Listen. No answer is not an answer. No answer is nothing at all. It is a thing I made up because I could not bear to think that they had forgotten me, so I made up a rule: that silence is a kind of message, and that I could read it, and that it said they were well and had put me behind them.' His eyes were on the ceiling. 'It says nothing. It says that a man wrote two letters a year for nine years and a girl of six could not read them, and a boy of three could not read them, and their mother may be dead or may have married and may have thrown them in the fire.' He turned his head. 'When you find them, do not tell them I decided that. Tell them I wrote.'

'I will tell them,' said Antonia. 'I will go to Lugdunum myself if I have to.'

'You will not,' said Brennus. 'You will be closing a house in March and being tried for something else in the summer.' He almost smiled. 'That is all right. I have been in this house nine years and it is the best house I have ever been in, and I have been in five, and one of them was a senator's, and I want you to know that we all say so, in the kitchen, when you are not there.'

He died at the fourth hour with his hand in hers and the sky going grey beyond the shutters.

---

Antonia stood in the courtyard at the fifth hour of the morning with her hands and her dress and her sleeves covered in blood, and made three decisions in the space of about four minutes.

The first was that the man Prima had killed would not be moved. He was lying where he had fallen, by the wall, with his head at an angle that was wrong, and the gravel around him was dark, and she said, 'Nobody touches him. Nobody covers him. He stays there until the prefect's men come and see him, and then he goes where they take him.'

The second was that the man they had tied to the gate would be given to the prefect's men alive, with his knife and his ladder and his jar of pitch, and that every person in the house would give a statement that day, separately, in their own words, to a clerk, and that Cinnamus would write them all out fair and no one would be coached.

The third was that she would send for the urban prefect herself, at the fifth hour of the morning, before anybody in the street had decided what had happened.

Cinnamus wrote it all down and then said, 'Domina, may I say something?'

'Say it.'

'The prefect will be here by the seventh hour. There will be a charge. A freedwoman of this house has killed a man — a free man, whatever else he was — and the law on this is not the law on a housebreaking; there will be a question of whether the force was proportionate, and there will be a question of whose house it was, and there will be a question about you.' He did not look at her hands. 'There is also a question about whether the dead man was sent by Marcus Sestius, and if he was, then the praetor's household will be in this before the ninth hour with a story of its own, and the story will be that a woman who was acquitted in November has now killed a man in her own courtyard in November.'

'I know,' said Antonia.

'And Prima will be taken. Not today, perhaps; today they will take the man at the gate and the body. But in three days or five there will be a summons, and she will be charged, and a freedwoman who kills a free man in a house of that kind will not be tried by a court that is inclined to be generous, whatever happened in the basilica this month.'

'I know that too,' said Antonia, 'and that is why I am going to do the thing I am going to do, and I want you to write it down before I change my mind.'

'What thing?'

Antonia looked at the courtyard: at the body by the wall, at the ladder, at the jar of pitch, at the blood on the gravel, at the whole of the house she had built standing around her in the grey light with its doors open.

'I am going to defend her,' she said, 'in public, in the same basilica, with the same advocate, and I am going to pay for it out of my own money, and I am going to be in that court every day, sitting behind her, and every man in Rome will see me do it.'

'Domina—'

'And when it is over, whatever the verdict, I am going to give her the house on the Aventine and the two hundred thousand sesterces and a passage to anywhere in the world she wants, and I am going to tell her, in front of witnesses, that she did what I would have done and that this house is hers as much as mine.'

Cinnamus was silent.

'That is the fourth thing,' said Antonia. 'Write it down.'

---

The prefect's men came at the sixth hour, and the prefect himself came at the ninth, and by then the whole street was in the road.

The story was in the city by the afternoon and it was in the Acta the next morning, and it said that four men had come over the wall of the house on the Esquiline with a crowbar and a jar of pitch, and that a freedman of the household, Brennus, a Sequani, had been killed, and that one of the four had been killed by a woman of the household with an iron bar, and that a third had been taken alive and had told the prefect's clerk, before the ninth hour, the name of the man who had paid him.

It was not Marcus Sestius. It was Sestius's steward, who had been his steward for thirty years, and who was arrested at his master's house at the eleventh hour and who said, when questioned, that he had acted on his own initiative, and who would say it for the rest of his life, and who was believed by nobody at all and could not be disproved.

Marcus Sestius came to the house on the second day.

He came in the morning, alone, in a plain toga, and he stood in the atrium with his hands at his sides and said to Meno, 'I have come to pay my respects to the household of the dead man, and to say to the woman who owns this house that I did not send those men.'

Antonia came out of the study and stood in front of him.

'You did not send them,' she said.

'I did not send them.' He looked old. He had lost eleven pounds and his toga hung on him. 'I do not expect you to believe me. My steward has been with me for thirty years; he has watched my son grow up; he has been in a rage since the funeral and he has been drinking and he has said things in my own house that I should have stopped.' He swallowed. 'I did not send him. But he was my steward, and he was in my house, and he was paid with my money, and I am responsible for him in law and in everything else, and I have come here to say so in your atrium where your household can hear it.'

Antonia looked at him for a long moment.

'There is a dead man in my garden,' she said, 'who was not your son and who had never done anything to you, and he was forty-four years old and he had two children in Gaul that he had not seen in nine years, and his name was Brennus, and he was killed by a knife that was paid for with your money, and whatever you say in this atrium will not bring him back.'

'I know,' said Sestius.

'Then why are you here?'

'Because I have a daughter,' said Sestius, 'and she is twenty and she has not spoken to me since the funeral, and because in the last four months I have lost my son, my office, my name and the respect of every man I have ever known, and because there is nothing left to do but this.' He stood in the middle of her atrium. 'I am going to lay the whole of it before the urban prefect, including the fact that my steward has been drinking since August and that I knew it and did nothing, and I am going to pay for the funeral of the man Brennus and for a stone, and I am going to pay whatever the court awards to the household, and I am not asking you for anything at all.'

Antonia stood in front of him.

'He has two children in Lugdunum,' she said. 'A girl of fifteen and a boy of twelve, if they are alive. I have written twice and there has been no answer.'

Sestius looked at her.

'I have an agent in Lugdunum,' he said. 'He is a horse trader and he knows every household in the district. I will write to him today and I will send a man.'

'Why?'

'Because I have nothing left to spend my money on,' said Sestius, 'and because I would like, before I die, to have done one thing that was not about my own name.'

---

They buried Brennus on the fourth day, outside the Colline gate, in the ground where the praetor's son had been buried in August, and the whole household went, and Meno carried the stick with the bronze ferrule and put it on the pyre, and the boy from the kitchen put the nine sesterces in the fold of the shroud, and Antonia stood at the edge of the ground and said nothing at all.

The household held its own mourning that night in the kitchen court, which was not a Roman funeral and had no priest and no flute player, and which was organized by the two cooks without asking anybody's permission.

They put a lamp and a cup of wine and a bowl of salt on the table where Brennus had eaten for nine years. They put his stick against the wall. The boy from the kitchen had kept the eleven sesterces and would not give them up, so they put a stone in the fold of a cloth instead, and the boy put the coins beside the stone, and the cooks said that it was not proper and let him do it.

Then they sat on the benches and told stories about him for four hours, and the stories were not respectful.

He had, it turned out, been afraid of geese. He had been afraid of geese for nine years and had never mentioned it, and the reason the household knew was that in the fourth year a goose had come into the courtyard and he had gone into the bath house and stayed there for an hour, and everyone had assumed that he was ill.

He had a way of saying *domina* that could mean yes, no, I have considered it and I decline, and it had meant all four things in a single afternoon in the seventh year.

He had never once in nine years taken a bribe at the gate, though he had been offered many, and he had told the whole household, at the Saturnalia in the sixth year, when he was drunk, that the reason was not honesty but arithmetic: a doorkeeper who takes a bribe is a doorkeeper who is owned, and he had been owned once and did not intend to be owned again.

Meno sat at the end of the bench with his stick across his knees and said nothing at all, and at the third hour he got up and went to his gate.

Antonia followed him.

He was standing in the doorway with the street beyond him and the cold coming in, and he said, without turning, 'I have been at a gate for thirty years, domina, in four houses, and I have never lost a man on my watch until now.'

'It was not your watch,' said Antonia. 'It was the garden wall, and you were at the gate, and you came when you heard.'

'I heard the first sound and I came with a lamp,' said Meno. 'If I had come with the stick and no lamp I would have been in the garden four heartbeats sooner, and four heartbeats is what it takes, and I have been standing here for an hour working it out.' He turned. 'He was twenty years younger than me and he was a soldier and he had two children and he died in the gravel, and I am sixty-eight and I have no children and I am still here, and there is no arithmetic in the world that makes that come out right.'

Antonia stood beside him in the doorway.

'What do you want to do?' she said.

'What I want,' said Meno, 'is to be at a gate in a house where nobody comes over the wall. And since there is no such house in Rome, I will stay at this one until March, and then I will go to Tibur with my cottage and my two hundred sesterces a year, and I will keep a gate there, and it will be a garden gate, and it will be very boring, and I will think about Brennus every day.' He closed the door and put the bar across it. 'That is all, domina. You may go to bed.'

---

Afterwards, Prima came and stood beside her.

'They will charge me in the spring,' she said.

'They will charge you in January,' said Antonia. 'I have spoken to the prefect's clerk. It will be heard in the same basilica, before a different panel, and it will be a charge of homicide, and it will be argued that you used excessive force against a man who was already on the ground.'

'Was he on the ground?'

'He was coming at you with a knife,' said Antonia, 'and there were four of them and one of them had already killed a man, and there were eleven women and a cook and a boy in this house. I do not care where he was. I care what a court can be made to see.'

Prima was quiet for a moment.

'I hit him once,' she said. 'I want you to know that. I took the bar out of the brackets and I came through the passage and I hit him once on the side of the head, and he went down and he did not get up, and I stood over him and I could have hit him again and I did not.' She looked at the ground. 'I have thought about that for four days. I did not hit him again because he was down and it would have been wrong, and I have discovered that I am a woman who does not hit a man when he is down, and I did not know that about myself, and it is the only thing I have learned from all of this that is worth anything.'

Antonia turned and looked at her.

'I am going to defend you,' she said, 'and it is going to cost me a great deal of money and the last of my reputation, and when it is over you are going to take the house on the Aventine and the money and go, and you are going to be a woman of thirty-two with a house and a deed and no past, and there will be a woman in this city who was in a brothel in the Argiletum at fifteen and killed a man in a courtyard at thirty-one and was acquitted, and she is going to be you.'

Prima looked at the pyre.

'All right,' she said.
